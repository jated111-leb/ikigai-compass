# The Daily Compass #098 — Live the questions

*Saturday, November 7, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In the late 1940s, Richard Feynman was a young professor at Cornell and, by his own account, burned out. Physics had begun to feel like a chore, and he decided he would stop trying to do important work and simply play with it, the way he had as a boy. Soon afterwards, sitting in the university cafeteria, he watched someone throw a plate into the air. As it flew, it wobbled, and he noticed that the red Cornell medallion on the plate seemed to be turning faster than the plate was wobbling. For no reason except that he wanted to know, he worked out the equations of the motion. That playful calculation led him back to questions about the spin of electrons, and from there to the work on quantum electrodynamics that would win him a share of the Nobel Prize in 1965. "The diagrams and the whole business that I got the Nobel Prize for," he wrote in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985), "came from that piddling around with the wobbling plate." There is a nice postscript. Physicists later pointed out, in letters to *Physics Today*, that Feynman had remembered the ratio the wrong way round: for a thin plate, the wobble is faster than the spin. The question mattered more than the answer. This issue is about curiosity: why organisations drift away from exploration, what makes people afraid to ask, and what happens when AI agents can see the answer but are not curious enough to pick it up.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The drift away from exploring

**What happened.** In ["Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v2y1991i1p71-87.html) (*Organization Science*, 1991), the Stanford organisational theorist James March described a tension at the heart of every organisation. Exploitation, refining and executing what already works, pays back quickly, reliably and close to home. Exploration, searching for something new, pays back slowly, uncertainly and often elsewhere. As a result, March argued, adaptive organisations naturally drift towards exploitation, which makes them effective in the short run but "self-destructive in the long run". Later research has looked for exploration at the top. In ["The Exploratory Mindset and Corporate Innovation"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v57y2022i1p127-169_5.html) (*Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*, 2022), Zhaozhao He and David Hirshleifer used a chief executive's holding a doctorate as a rough proxy for an exploratory mindset, and found that such firms invested more in research, produced more novel and original patents, and managed earnings less. But curiosity at the top can also narrow. A study in the *Strategic Management Journal* (2024) by Joseph Harrison and colleagues found that when inventor chief executives keep personally patenting, their firms do more exploitative and less exploratory innovation, deepening the boss's own path. A new review by Todd Kashdan, Patrick McKnight and Spencer Harrison, ["Curiosity at Work: A Productive Inefficiency Model"](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/curiosity-work-a-productive-inefficiency-model) (2026), names the trade-off directly: curiosity slows decisions and uses resources in the short term, while supporting adaptability and breakthroughs over the long term.

**Why it matters.** March's insight explains why curiosity is so hard to sustain in successful companies. Every quarterly target, efficiency drive and performance review rewards exploitation, because its results are visible and attributable. Exploration rarely survives that competition unless it is protected on purpose. The research suggests a few practical defences. Budget for exploration explicitly, as Kashdan and colleagues propose, rather than hoping it happens in spare time. Look for curiosity in people who are not in charge: a new piece in *Harvard Business Review* (October 2026) by Linda Hill and colleagues argues that companies making the most progress with new technology lean on curious employees, often junior, who explore it first. And watch for the narrowing that comes with expertise, including at the top. The most experienced people in the room are usually the best at exploiting what they know, which makes them the least likely to notice what they do not.

*Source: March, Organization Science 2(1) (1991); He & Hirshleifer, JFQA 57(1) (2022); Harrison, Boivie, Stern & Porac, SMJ 45(11) (2024); Kashdan, McKnight & Harrison (2026); Hill et al., HBR (Oct 28, 2026). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. The itch, and the fear of scratching it

**What happened.** In ["The Psychology of Curiosity"](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/) (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1994), the economist George Loewenstein proposed that curiosity is a kind of deprivation: it arises when attention falls on a gap between what we know and what we want to know, and it is strongest when the gap is neither too small to matter nor too large to engage with. New research refines the idea. In ["Knowability predicts curiosity and learning"](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication/2440158) (*Cognition*, 2026), Samuel Recht and Nick Yeung of the University of Oxford asked people answering trivia questions how many possible answers they had in mind. The more candidates people had, the more curious they were and the better they remembered the answer days later. Curiosity, it seems, depends not just on a gap, but on the feeling that the gap can be closed. What stops people closing it is often social. In ["Overly Shallow?"](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers) (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), Michael Kardas, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley ran 12 experiments with more than 1,800 participants and found that people expected deep, probing conversations with strangers to be more awkward and less enjoyable than they turned out to be, because they underestimated how much others cared about what they shared. At work, the cost of that miscalibration is visible. In SurveyMonkey's [2026 State of Curiosity report](https://www.surveymonkey.com/newsroom/2026-state-of-curiosity-report/), a vendor survey of American workers, 95% described themselves as at least somewhat curious, but only 30% said their workplace strongly rewarded curiosity, and nearly four in ten admitted pretending to understand something rather than asking.

**Why it matters.** Curiosity is a natural drive, but asking is a social risk, and in many organisations the risk wins. People who fear looking incompetent pretend to understand; teams then build on misunderstandings that a single question would have caught. For leaders, the lessons are practical. Make gaps visible and closable: people are most curious when they can see what they do not know and believe they can find out. Lower the cost of asking, starting with your own questions, especially the naive ones. And use structures that make questioning normal rather than brave. One is the "question burst" developed by Hal Gregersen of MIT: a group spends a few minutes generating only questions about a problem, without answering or debating them, and then looks for the ones that challenge its assumptions. Gregersen notes that senior people often find it hardest, because they are paid to answer questions, not to ask them.

*Source: Loewenstein, Psychological Bulletin 116(1) (1994); Recht & Yeung, Cognition (2026); Kardas, Kumar & Epley, JPSP (2022); SurveyMonkey (May 2026); Gregersen, HBR (Mar–Apr 2018). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Agents that see but do not look

**What happened.** AI agents increasingly explore software environments, documents and the web on people's behalf. A study from researchers at the AI company Cohere asks whether they are curious about what they find. In ["Agents Explore but Agents Ignore: LLMs Lack Environmental Curiosity"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17609) (arXiv, 2026), Leon Engländer and colleagues placed the complete solution to a task inside the agent's environment and measured two things separately: whether the agent came across it, and whether it used it. The gap was striking. In one test environment, agents saw documentation stating that a command would return the complete solution in [over 90% of attempts](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.17609), but acted on it in fewer than 7%. Simplifying the agents' tools roughly doubled the rate at which they followed up, but even under the best conditions they ignored what they had found in most runs, and fine-tuning on narrow data made them less curious still. A second study suggests AI is better at elaborating than at asking new questions. In ["AI Research Agents Narrow Scientific Exploration"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27905) (arXiv, 2026), Yixuan Tang and Yi Yang had AI research agents generate 37,802 research ideas from shared starting literature, even when prompted to be novel. Compared with what human researchers went on to publish, the ideas clustered tightly, stayed close to the starting papers and mostly recombined existing methods rather than posing new questions. Meanwhile, SurveyMonkey's vendor survey found that leaders are more likely than other workers to take their questions to AI rather than to colleagues.

**Why it matters.** These findings point to a quiet risk. AI is very good at exploitation: answering, elaborating and refining within the space it is given. It is much weaker at the kind of curiosity that notices something unexpected and follows it, or that asks a question nobody has asked before. If organisations hand both their searching and their questioning to AI, they may become more efficient and less exploratory at the same time, exactly the drift March warned about. And when the questions that used to pass between colleagues go to a private chatbot instead, the organisation loses the incidental learning those conversations spread. The practical response is not to stop using AI, but to divide the work deliberately: let machines answer, but keep the asking, and the habit of following up on what looks odd, firmly human.

*Source: Engländer, Althammer, Üstün, Gallé & Sherborne, arXiv 2604.17609 (2026); Tang & Yang, arXiv 2605.27905 (2026); SurveyMonkey (May 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: "What's the go o' that?"** — James Clerk Maxwell, who would go on to unify electricity, magnetism and light, was by all accounts an insatiably curious child. In the first biography of him, published in 1882, Lewis Campbell and William Garnett recorded, from his family's recollections, that the young Maxwell would ask of every object and mechanism around him, ["What's the go o' that?"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/79044), and that if the answer was vague, he would press further: "But what's the particular go of it?" The second question is the more valuable one. General curiosity is common; the habit of refusing a vague answer, and asking for the specific mechanism, is what separates people who learn from people who merely wonder. (Campbell & Garnett, The Life of James Clerk Maxwell, 1882)

**🃏 WILDCARD: mice that pay to know** — are animals curious for its own sake? In a study published in *Nature Neuroscience* in July, Jennifer Bussell and colleagues offered thirsty mice a choice between two water ports with identical odds of reward. At one, an odour revealed in advance whether water was coming; at the other, nothing did. The mice chose the [informative port about 78% of the time](https://www.thetransmitter.org/behavior/wouldnt-you-like-to-know-a-mouse-would/), even though the information did not change what they received, and were willing to give up some reward to get it. About a fifth of the neurons recorded in a decision-making region of their brains, the orbitofrontal cortex, appeared to track the value of the information itself, separately from the value of the water. Wanting to know, it seems, is wired deep. (Nature Neuroscience, Jul 2026)

**Founders who test their hunches** — what happens when entrepreneurs are taught to treat their ideas as hypotheses to test? In a randomised trial published in *Management Science* in 2020, Arnaldo Camuffo, Alessandro Cordova, Alfonso Gambardella and Chiara Spina trained around 250 early-stage Italian founders either in a "scientific approach", forming theories about their customers and testing them, or in general business skills. The scientifically trained founders were [more likely to abandon weak ideas](https://www.innovationgrowthlab.org/blog/confused-or-unimaginative-how-adoption-scientific-approach-improves-entrepreneurial), pivoted in a more focused way and earned more revenue on average. A follow-up across 759 firms in four trials, published in 2024, found that the method led companies to make a few deliberate changes of direction, rather than never pivoting or pivoting constantly. Disciplined curiosity, in other words, pays. (Management Science, 2020; Strategic Management Journal, 2024)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In July 1903 the poet Rainer Maria Rilke, then 27, wrote from Worpswede in northern Germany to Franz Kappus, a young officer cadet who had sent him poems and asked for advice. Kappus was anxious and impatient for answers about his life and his art. Rilke's reply, the fourth of what became *Letters to a Young Poet*, contains some of his most quoted lines. He urged Kappus, in M. D. Herter Norton's translation, "to be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart and to try to love the questions themselves like locked rooms and like books that are written in a very foreign tongue." And then: "Live the questions now. Perhaps you will then gradually, without noticing it, live along some distant day into the answer." Rilke was writing about a young man's life, not about organisations or machines. But his advice runs against the grain of an age that can produce an answer to almost anything in seconds. Curiosity is not only the urge to close a gap as fast as possible. It is also the willingness to stay with a question long enough for it to change you, as the wobbling plate changed Feynman. Answers are becoming cheap. Good questions, held patiently, are not.
