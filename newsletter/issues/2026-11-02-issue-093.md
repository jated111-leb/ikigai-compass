# The Daily Compass #093 — Unto every one that hath

*Monday, November 2, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On January 5, 1968, the sociologist Robert K. Merton published a paper in *Science* with a biblical title: "The Matthew Effect in Science". Merton had noticed something uncomfortable about how scientists receive credit. When a famous scientist and an unknown one make the same discovery independently, or write a paper together, the famous one tends to get most of the recognition. Eminence attracts attention, attention brings resources and students, and those produce more eminence. Merton named the pattern after a line from the parable of the talents in the Gospel of Matthew: "For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance." One of his illustrations involved Lord Rayleigh, the Nobel physicist, whose name was accidentally left off a paper he had submitted to the British Association. The paper was rejected as the work of a crank; when the author's identity was restored, it was accepted. Much of the evidence for Merton's paper came from interviews with Nobel laureates conducted by the sociologist Harriet Zuckerman, and the story has an ironic coda of its own: Merton later acknowledged that he had drawn on her work so heavily that the paper should have appeared under joint authorship. This issue is about the Matthew effect, or cumulative advantage: how early success compounds in markets, careers and AI systems, how much of it is merit and how much is luck, and what leaders can do about it.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The hit that could have been a flop

**What happened.** If success breeds success, how much of success is deserved in the first place? In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts built an online music market to find out. In ["Experimental Study of Inequality and Unpredictability in an Artificial Cultural Market"](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bor/303s19/docs/salganik_dodds_watts.pdf) (*Science*, 2006), they invited 14,341 participants to listen to and download 48 songs by unknown bands. Some participants chose independently; others were sorted into separate "worlds" in which they could see how many times each song had been downloaded by earlier visitors. Every song started at zero. Making popularity visible made the market both more unequal and less predictable. The same song could become a hit in one world and be ignored in another. Quality mattered at the extremes: the best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well. In between, almost anything could happen. Arnout van de Rijt and colleagues later showed that the effect works in real markets. In ["Field experiments of success-breeds-success dynamics"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4024896) (*PNAS*, 2014), they gave a small, random first donation to 100 of 200 new, unfunded Kickstarter projects. Seventy per cent of the projects that received it went on to attract other donors, compared with 39% of the rest. Similar random boosts had lasting effects on Wikipedia awards, product reviews and online petitions, though the first success mattered far more than later ones. The same dynamics are visible in today's venture market. According to Crunchbase, a record 27 companies raised rounds of $1 billion or more in the third quarter of 2026, and those 27, out of roughly 6,000 funded start-ups, [took about a third](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/crunchbase-logs-27-billion-dollar-rounds-in-q3-2026-as-ai-grabs-102b-or-64-of) of the quarter's $159 billion in global venture funding. AI start-ups took 64%.

**Why it matters.** In markets where people can see what others are choosing, from consumer apps to venture capital, early leads compound and outcomes are partly a matter of luck. This has three practical implications. First, the first signal of traction is disproportionately valuable, which is why launch strategy, early reviews and initial reference customers deserve far more attention than their size suggests. Second, past winners are less reliable evidence of skill than they appear; a successful product, fund or executive may have benefited from an early boost that will not recur. Third, for challengers, the Matthew effect is a reason to concentrate resources on winning a small, visible early victory rather than spreading effort thinly. As van de Rijt put it, it is the first success that matters most.

*Source: Salganik, Dodds & Watts, Science 311 (2006); van de Rijt, Kang, Restivo & Patil, PNAS 111(19) (2014); Crunchbase Q3 2026 data, via AI Weekly. ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Just above the line

**What happened.** The cleanest evidence for cumulative advantage in careers comes from comparing people who only just won a competition with people who only just lost it. In ["The Matthew effect in science funding"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5948972) (*PNAS*, 2018), Thijs Bol, Mathijs de Vaan and Arnout van de Rijt studied applicants for the Netherlands' prestigious Veni grants for early-career researchers. Applicants just above the funding threshold and just below it had almost identical review scores. Yet over the next eight years, the narrow winners accumulated more than twice as much research funding as the narrow losers, about €180,000 more, and were far more likely to win the next grant and to become full professors. The authors found no evidence that winning made researchers more productive. The gap came from the status the award conferred and from the losers' behaviour: many of them simply applied for less funding afterwards. A study of America's National Institutes of Health points in a different direction. In ["Early-career setback and future career impact"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6773762/) (*Nature Communications*, 2019), Yang Wang, Benjamin Jones and Dashun Wang compared junior scientists who narrowly missed or narrowly won a major NIH grant. A near miss made people more likely to leave research altogether. But those who stayed went on to publish work that was more highly cited than that of the narrow winners. The setback, it seems, improved the people who persisted.

**Why it matters.** Together the studies describe the two forces at work in every organisation's talent system. Small differences in early recognition, a first promotion, a high-profile project, a coveted award, can compound into large differences in careers, even when the underlying ability is almost identical. Much of the compounding comes not from the winners getting better but from the near-losers giving up. For leaders, this suggests some deliberate correctives. Treat early-career selections as noisy, because they are; look hard at people who just missed out, and encourage them to try again; and, as Bol and colleagues proposed for grant-giving, consider hiding past honours from those making the next decision, so that status does not do the judging. For individuals, the lesson of the NIH study is that a narrow defeat need not compound. It can be the start of a different kind of advantage, provided you stay in the game.

*Source: Bol, de Vaan & van de Rijt, PNAS 115(19) (2018); Wang, Jones & Wang, Nature Communications 10 (2019). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. The machines join the herd

**What happened.** AI systems increasingly decide what people read, buy and cite. A new paper asks whether they too fall into rich-get-richer dynamics. In ["Social Influence and the Allocation of Scientific Attention in AI Populations"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22408) (arXiv, September 2026), Maxim Chupilkin rebuilt the music experiment with AI agents in place of people and economics papers in place of songs. A thousand agents chose which of the 114 regular articles in the 2025 *American Economic Review* to read. Some chose independently; others, in sequence, could see what earlier agents had chosen. When agents could see the choices of others, each picked about 17% fewer papers, choices bunched together, and only 73 papers received any attention, compared with 90 when agents chose independently. In a second experiment, giving a randomly chosen paper a head start of five early selections raised its later selection rate by about 45 percentage points. The agents' choices bore only a modest relationship to papers' actual citations. Real science shows a related pattern. In ["AI expands scientists' impact but contracts science's focus"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07727v4) (*Nature*, January 2026), Qianyue Hao, Fengli Xu, Yong Li and James Evans analysed some 41 million papers. Scientists who used AI published about three times as many papers, received nearly five times as many citations and became research leaders more than a year earlier. But across science, AI adoption was associated with a 4.63% contraction in the range of topics studied and a 22% drop in engagement between scientists, as researchers crowded into the same data-rich problems.

**Why it matters.** AI promises to sift information more objectively than people do. These studies suggest it can instead amplify the herd. Agents that see what other agents chose behave much like the human listeners in Salganik's experiment, turning small early differences into large ones. Researchers who use AI benefit individually, while the collective portfolio narrows. For companies, the implications run in two directions. In markets increasingly mediated by AI recommendations, early visibility may compound even faster than before. And inside organisations, teams using the same AI tools may converge on the same ideas, the same sources and the same strategies. The remedy in both cases is deliberate diversity: tools configured to explore rather than simply follow, and leaders who ask what everyone else is not looking at.

*Source: Chupilkin, arXiv 2609.22408 (Sep 2026); Hao, Xu, Li & Evans, Nature 649 (2026), via University of Chicago and HPCwire. ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the hockey players born in January** — in the early 1980s, at a junior hockey game in Lethbridge, Alberta, Paula Barnsley glanced at the programme and noticed that a striking number of the players had been born in January, February or March. Her husband, the psychologist Roger Barnsley, checked the rosters of elite teams and found the same skew. Canadian youth hockey grouped children by calendar year, so those born early in the year were the oldest and most physically mature in their age group. They were more likely to be picked for elite teams, which brought better coaching and more ice time, which widened the gap year after year. Barnsley's 1985 paper coined the term "relative age effect", and Malcolm Gladwell made it famous in *Outliers*, in a chapter titled "The Matthew Effect". In 2025, [revisiting the evidence](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11841377/) after 40 years, Barnsley found the pattern essentially unchanged: about 40% of elite young players are born in the first quarter of the selection year and about 10% in the last. A cut-off date, an arbitrary administrative choice, still decides careers. (Barnsley, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 2025)

**🃏 WILDCARD: prizes don't sell the next book** — does winning a big literary prize set an author up for life? A study of Italian fiction suggests not. In an IZA discussion paper published in April, the economists Silvia Marchesi, Michela Ponzo, Vincenzo Scoppa and Idola Francesca Spanò analysed weekly bestseller lists from 1975 to 2025. Winning a major literary prize had [virtually no effect](https://ideas.repec.org/p/iza/izadps/dp18563.html) on the sales of an author's later books. Sustained success on the bestseller list, by contrast, had a small but positive effect, stronger for younger and female authors. The authors suggest that prizes send readers to books outside their usual tastes, so those readers do not come back. Not all advantages compound: a credential may fade, while proven demand builds on itself. (IZA DP 18563, Apr 2026)

**The challenger's opening** — will AI shopping assistants simply recommend the biggest brands? A study posted in September, ["Evaluating Brand Retrieval and Ranking in Large Language Model Recommendations"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16304), sampled the same questions repeatedly across several chatbots and found only limited evidence of the popularity bias familiar from older recommender systems. Generic questions often left out large, established brands, while brands with distinctive positioning surfaced when a user described a matching need. Recommendations varied from one run to the next. The authors treat their findings as exploratory, but for challenger brands the implication is encouraging: in AI-mediated discovery, a clear and distinctive position may count for more than sheer size. (arXiv 2609.16304, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The verse that gave Merton his name comes at the end of the parable of the talents, in which a master entrusts money to three servants before a journey. Two invest it and double it; the third, afraid of losing it, buries it in the ground. When the master returns, he rewards the first two and takes the buried talent from the third. "For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance," the King James Version reads: "but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath." Read as a description of how the world works, it is harsh, and the evidence in this issue suggests it is often accurate. But the parable has another reading. The servant who loses everything is not the one who started with least; he is the one who, out of fear, refused to use what he had. More than five centuries ago, Machiavelli made a similar point about fortune. In chapter 25 of *The Prince*, he judged that "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." The Matthew effect is real, and leaders should work to soften it for others. For ourselves, the lesson is the same as the hockey rinks and the near-missed grants: early luck compounds, but so does the decision to keep playing.
