# The Daily Compass #037 — Gather and scatter

*Sunday, August 23, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Every powerful technology has two gravities working at once, and they pull in opposite directions. One draws things together — money, talent, machines, power — into dense clusters that, once formed, are almost impossible to pry apart. The other flings capability outward, cheapening it and pushing it to the edges until what used to require a citadel fits on a laptop. Most arguments about where the future concentrates pick one gravity and ignore the other, which is why they keep getting the map wrong. This issue holds both at once. It watches capital and expertise clump harder than ever — a financial city that no politician can dislodge, a state deliberately force-building an industrial cluster by flooding it with more money than it can absorb — and then watches, in the same week, the frontier of machine intelligence quietly leak out of the data center and onto the desk in front of you. Gather and scatter. The strategic question is never *which one is happening.* It's *which one is winning, where, and what that means for where you should stand.*

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The gravity of gathering

**What happened.** Ben Thompson turns a local political fight into a clean lesson on network effects and their limits ([*"New York and the Power of the Network"*](https://stratechery.com/2026/new-york-and-the-power-of-the-network/)). His natural experiment: New York finance versus modern Hollywood. Caustic city politics — the Mamdani mayoralty — probably *can't* drive the finance industry out of New York, because the value of being in New York is other people who are in New York; the network holds even when the rent and the rhetoric turn hostile. Hollywood is the cautionary opposite: filmmaking, whose cluster once looked just as permanent, has already bled to cheaper geographies, because the thing it clustered around turned out to be movable in a way a capital market is not.

**Why it matters.** This is the oldest law in economic geography, and it's about to decide the map of the AI economy. Alfred Marshall named it in 1890 (this issue's ⏳): when an industry concentrates in a place, "the mysteries of the trade become no mysteries; but are as it were in the air" — knowledge, suppliers, and specialized labor pool together and reinforce each other until the cluster becomes its own moat. The subtle part is Thompson's asymmetry: not all clusters are equally sticky. Finance gathers around trust, information, and proximity to capital — things that genuinely require co-presence — so it holds. Film gathered around infrastructure that a subsidy and a cheaper crew could replicate elsewhere, so it scattered. The question to ask of any AI hub, chip-fab corridor, or talent magnet is not "is it dominant now?" but "what is it actually clustered *around* — and is that thing the kind that stays in the air, or the kind that follows the tax break?"

*Source: Thompson, Stratechery (Aug 14). ~13 min.*

### ◆ 2. Concentration by design

**What happened.** If brief 1 is concentration as a natural force, here is concentration as deliberate statecraft. A ChinaTalk essay reframes China's frenzied AI funding not as a bubble to be embarrassed about but as the *point* — ["Why China's AI Bubble Is Also Industrial Policy."](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/why-chinas-ai-bubble-is-also-industrial) The argument: a state can choose to pour capital into a sector faster than the sector can rationally absorb it, accepting spectacular waste — failed startups, duplicated efforts, overbuilt capacity — as the deliberate price of manufacturing a critical mass that markets alone would build too slowly, or not at all. The froth isn't a symptom of a mistake; it's the mechanism.

**Why it matters.** This detonates the reflexive Western read of "bubble" as weakness — the same read applied all month to the American AI build-out. A bubble that pops and leaves behind fiber, fabs, trained engineers, and a dense supplier web has, in industrial-policy terms, *worked*, whatever it did to its investors; the railway and dot-com manias impoverished shareholders and enriched the century that followed. It rhymes with the deeper thread this newsletter keeps pulling — that structure and mechanism beat raw capability — but points it at geography: China is not trying to build the single best model, it is trying to build the *cluster*, the whole Marshallian atmosphere, on purpose and ahead of schedule. One telling marker the piece cites of how deliberately the walls are going up: the foreign-investor share of Chinese venture deal value fell from roughly 55% in 2018 to about 20% by 2023, hardening after Washington's own outbound-investment controls. Two states are now force-concentrating rival AI ecosystems and calling the waste an investment. The bubble debate has been asking the wrong question: not "will it pop?" but "what will still be standing when it does?"

*Source: ChinaTalk (Aug 18). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 3. The scatter

**What happened.** Now the opposite gravity, in the same week. Tomasz Tunguz reports swapping a laptop-class model into his working agent and finding it holds its own against the cloud giants — ["Birds Don't Fly Like Planes. Neither Does AI."](https://tomtunguz.com/birds-dont-fly-like-planes-neither-does-ai) The striking data: a ~27-billion-parameter Qwen model that runs on a laptop ranked first out of 135 on one widely-watched intelligence index, edging a cloud model roughly *28 times its size*. His analogy explains how: big cloud models are like experts who have memorized so much they leap straight to the answer; small local models, lacking that stored bulk, have to *reason* their way there, closer to first principles — arriving at the same place by a different route, trading memorized scale for inference-time thinking.

**Why it matters.** This is the centrifuge running against briefs 1 and 2. While capital and talent clump into ever-denser, ever-stickier clusters, the actual *capability* — the thing all that concentration was supposed to hoard — is quietly escaping to the edge, onto commodity hardware, into everyone's hands. And the mechanism matters as much as the fact: if the small model reasons rather than recalls, then intelligence is decoupling from sheer scale, which is the one asset the giant clusters were built to monopolize. Tunguz's operator's coda is the tell — stop measuring these models by token speed and start measuring *time to answer*, because the whole competitive frame shifts when the frontier is portable. The comforting story for the incumbents is that the future belongs to whoever gathers the most. The uncomfortable possibility in brief 3 is that the most valuable thing refuses to stay gathered — that even as the citadels grow taller, the treasure is walking out the gate, and will run on the machine already sitting on your desk.

*Source: Tunguz (Aug 22). ~9 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — a quietly important admission, via MIT Tech Review: [we still don't really know how people use AI.](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/18/1142226/how-people-use-ai/) The labs publish usage reports, but only the slices they choose to — so the public picture of what this general-purpose technology actually *does* is curated by its own vendors. The piece profiles a new independent "AI Observatory" pooling consented conversations across seven datasets, already finding that usage skews by model (people lean on one assistant for coding, another for company and roleplay). Ground truth about the most-hyped tool of the decade is, for now, a thing we're being shown rather than something we can see. (Aug 18)

**Nobody knows who "the best" is** — a bracing MIT Sloan Management Review piece (Aydinliyim & Somaya) argues the ubiquitous "hire only the best" is close to incoherent, because *best* is unknowable and context-dependent — the star at one firm is average at another. The real lever for advantage isn't winning a mythical top hire; it's understanding the barriers that keep people from moving, which they sort into ten distinct categories. A useful antidote to talent-worship: competitive edge lives in the system around a person, not a leaderboard ranking of the person. ([MIT SMR](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/), Aug 18)

**Faith, repurposed** — a genuinely startling reframe from ChinaTalk: the Kim dynasty's personality cult isn't a rejection of religion but a *repurposing* of one. Pyongyang was once called the "Jerusalem of the East," and the regime, the discussion argues, "perverted rather than eliminated" the Presbyterian Christianity it inherited — converting its ritual, moral authority, and language of salvation into Kim-worship. A durable reminder of how new ideologies rarely invent their architecture; they move into the building faith already built. ([ChinaTalk](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/north-korean-messiah), Aug 19)

**The end of the long read** — 3 Quarks Daily flags a worry now hard to dismiss: sustained deep reading is collapsing, even among the students once most capable of it, and AI summary tools are pouring accelerant on a fire lit by the feed. The alarming part isn't nostalgia for books; it's that deep reading is the training apparatus for holding a complex thought across time — a capacity you can't summarize your way back into once it's gone. ([3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/), Aug 20)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Concentration of Industries in Particular Localities** — Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics*, 1890 (Book IV, Ch. X). The founding account of why industries cluster and, having clustered, stay: skilled labor pools, specialized suppliers arise, and above all the tacit knowledge of the trade becomes ambient — "as it were in the air" — so that being *inside* the cluster confers advantages no outsider can buy. Why now: it is the source code for every 2026 argument about AI hubs, chip corridors, and China's forced ecosystems (briefs 1 and 2), and its 135-year-old subtlety — that the moat is the *atmosphere*, not any one firm — is exactly what the scatter of brief 3 now threatens to thin. ([Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/marshall/marP27.html), 1890)

**🃏 WILDCARD: order out of chaos** — Quanta reports a graduate student, Alex Cohen, proving the "fractal uncertainty principle," a deep result binding together chaos, quantum theory, and fractal geometry — the kind of proof that reveals three seemingly unrelated corners of mathematics were secretly the same room. A small, bracing counter-melody to an issue about things pulling apart: sometimes the scattered pieces turn out to gather, at a level of abstraction no one had climbed to yet. ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/graduate-student-proves-the-fractal-uncertainty-principle-20260812/), Aug 12)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The two gravities are not a paradox to resolve; they're a permanent condition to navigate. Nearly everything valuable both gathers and scatters, and the whole art is telling which is doing which. Capital gathers — it always has, and the AI build-out is the most concentrated deployment of capital in human history, pooling in a handful of firms and two rival states. Attention gathers, into a few platforms and a few models. But *capability* scatters: the printing press, once a guarded craft, became a machine in every town; literacy, once a priestly monopoly, became universal; and machine intelligence, this very month, took another step off the mainframe and onto the laptop. The uncomfortable, hopeful truth braided through this issue is that the thing worth having is usually the thing that won't stay penned. Marshall's cluster is real and the smart money is right to chase the atmosphere — being where the knowledge is "in the air" is still, for a person choosing where to build a life, an enormous accelerant. But the deeper bet is on the scatter: on the capability you can carry, the skill that lives in your own hands and runs on your own machine, the understanding no citadel can hoard because you reasoned your way to it yourself rather than borrowing it pre-memorized from someone else's cloud. Go stand where things gather, by all means — the cluster is real and it compounds. But make sure the thing you're building is the kind that travels, because in the end the gates never hold, and what survives the scattering is only ever what someone actually carried out.
