# The Daily Compass #104 — Fingers crossed

*Friday, November 13, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1948 the psychologist B. F. Skinner put hungry pigeons into boxes and fed them on a timer: a little grain at regular intervals, no matter what the birds were doing. He then watched what happened. Most of the birds developed elaborate rituals. ["One bird was conditioned to turn counter-clockwise about the cage,"](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Skinner/Pigeon/) he reported, "making two or three turns between reinforcements." Another repeatedly tossed its head, "as if placing its head beneath an invisible bar and lifting it." Whatever a bird happened to be doing when the food arrived, it did more of, and the next delivery seemed to confirm that the behaviour worked. Skinner called his paper "'Superstition' in the Pigeon", and concluded that the experiment "might be said to demonstrate a sort of superstition." Later researchers argued about his explanation, but the image stuck: a creature performing a ritual that has nothing to do with the reward, convinced that it does. Today is Friday the 13th, a date whose reputation owes surprisingly little to ancient history. Its popularity as an unlucky day owes much to a 1907 novel, *Friday, the Thirteenth*, about a stockbroker who exploits the fear to crash the market; the popular link to the arrest of the Knights Templar in 1307 is largely a modern invention. This issue is about superstition and ritual: what our superstitions really cost, how organisations learn the wrong lessons, and why some rituals work even though there is nothing magic about them.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. What superstition is worth

**What happened.** For a while, science seemed to show that superstition could genuinely help. In ["Keep Your Fingers Crossed! How Superstition Improves Performance"](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610372631) (*Psychological Science*, 2010), Lysann Damisch, Barbara Stoberock and Thomas Mussweiler reported that students who were told their golf ball was "lucky" sank more putts than students who were not. The study was widely covered. But when Robert Calin-Jageman and Tracy Caldwell ran a [pre-registered replication](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/full/10.1027/1864-9335/a000190) with more than four times as many participants (*Social Psychology*, 2014), the "lucky ball" group "performed almost identically to controls." The Friday-the-13th effect has followed a similar path. A 1987 study in the *Journal of Finance* found lower stock returns on Friday the 13th; later studies found nothing, and Brian Lucey's [analysis of 19 countries](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:apeclt:v:8:y:2001:i:9:p:577-579) (*Applied Economics Letters*, 2001) found that returns on the day were, if anything, higher. Dutch insurance data reported in 2008 showed slightly fewer accident claims on Fridays the 13th than on other Fridays, perhaps because nervous people take more care. Yet superstitions do move real prices. In ["Superstition in the Housing Market"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ecinqu:v:52:y:2014:i:3:p:974-993) (*Economic Inquiry*, 2014), Nicole Fortin, Andrew Hill and Jeffrey Huang found that in parts of Vancouver with large Chinese communities, houses with addresses ending in 4, a number associated with death, sold at a 2.2% discount, and those ending in 8, a lucky number, at a 2.5% premium. And in ["Superstition and Financial Decision Making"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:64:y:2018:i:1:p:235-252) (*Management Science*, 2018), David Hirshleifer, Ming Jian and Huai Zhang found that Chinese firms whose stock-listing codes contained lucky digits traded at a premium when they listed, then underperformed until the premium disappeared.

**Why it matters.** Superstitions are best understood as beliefs that markets price, and then correct. A lucky number can add value for a while, because enough buyers believe in it; but the premium rarely lasts, and anyone paying it for its own sake is usually overpaying. Effects that sound too good to be true, like the lucky golf ball, often turn out to be just that. That is a useful habit of mind well beyond superstition: before acting on a striking finding, check whether it has been replicated. The deeper puzzle is why intelligent people keep their lucky rituals even when they know better. The psychologist Jane Risen argues, in [a 2016 paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000017) in *Psychological Review*, that noticing that an intuition is irrational and correcting it are separate steps, and people often do the first without the second: they "acquiesce" to a feeling they know is wrong. That is why executives keep lucky ties, and also why simply pointing out a bias is rarely enough to remove it.

*Source: Damisch, Stoberock & Mussweiler, Psychological Science 21(7) (2010); Calin-Jageman & Caldwell, Social Psychology 45(3) (2014); Lucey, Applied Economics Letters 8(9) (2001); Fortin, Hill & Huang, Economic Inquiry 52(3) (2014); Hirshleifer, Jian & Zhang, Management Science 64(1) (2018); Risen, Psychological Review 123(2) (2016). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Superstitious organisations, useful rituals

**What happened.** Organisations can become superstitious too. In a classic review, the organisational theorists Barbara Levitt and James March described "superstitious learning": the experience of learning is compelling, but the connection between actions and outcomes has been misunderstood. Firms see success, credit whatever they happened to be doing, and repeat it. In a study of 167 acquisitions by American banks ([*Organization Science*, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1090.0459)), Maurizio Zollo found that the more successful managers believed their past deals had been, the worse their next deal actually performed, especially in large firms with more experience. Investors face the same trap. In ["The persistent effect of initial success"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24887) (*Journal of Financial Economics*, 2020), Ramana Nanda, Sampsa Samila and Olav Sorenson analysed more than 46,000 venture-capital investments and found that firms whose early investments happened to go public kept outperforming for years, mostly because early success brought them access to better deals rather than better judgement. Yet not every ritual is superstition. A [meta-analysis](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/934597) of pre-performance routines in sport by Rupprecht, Tran and Gröpel (*International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 2021) found that structured routines, such as a set sequence before a free throw or a serve, produced moderate to large improvements in performance in experiments, including under pressure. And in a [field study in Mauritius](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7423266/) (*Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 2020), Martin Lang, Jan Krátký and Dimitris Xygalatas found that people who performed a familiar religious ritual after a stressful task showed lower anxiety, both reported and physiological, than those who simply sat and relaxed.

**Why it matters.** The difference between a useful ritual and a harmful superstition is not whether it looks irrational. It is whether you believe it causes something it does not. A pre-meeting routine, a launch ritual or a team tradition can calm nerves, focus attention and build a sense of shared identity, and those benefits are real even though no luck is involved. The danger is the false causal story: the strategy credited for a success that was really timing, the hire who looked brilliant because they joined a growing team, the deal-maker who believes their instincts are better than they are. Leaders can guard against superstitious learning in a few ways. Review successes as rigorously as failures. Ask what would have happened anyway, without the decision being credited. Vary your experience rather than simply accumulating more of the same; Zollo found that a more diverse record of deals reduced the problem. And keep the rituals that help your team perform, while being honest about why they help.

*Source: Levitt & March, Annual Review of Sociology 14 (1988); Zollo, Organization Science 20(5) (2009); Nanda, Samila & Sorenson, JFE 137(1) (2020); Rupprecht, Tran & Gröpel, IRSEP (2021); Lang, Krátký & Xygalatas, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B (2020). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Prompting rituals

**What happened.** Working with AI has produced a new folklore of its own. Users have been told that models perform better if you offer them a tip, threaten them, flatter them, tell them to "take a deep breath" or ask them to act as an expert. Researchers at the Wharton School have begun testing these rituals. In ["I'll pay you or I'll kill you — but will you care?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00614) (2025), Lennart Meincke, Ethan Mollick, Lilach Mollick and Dan Shapiro offered models tips of up to a trillion dollars, and threatened them in various ways, on demanding graduate-level and professional exam questions. Overall, threats and tips made no significant difference. But on individual questions, the same prompt could help or hurt substantially, and there was no way to predict which. A [follow-up report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05858) found that asking models to adopt expert personas did not reliably improve accuracy on factual questions either. A 2026 preprint found something stranger still: [prompts unrelated to the task](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29678) sometimes worked as well as carefully designed ones on one model, but generally failed to carry over to others, the AI equivalent of a lucky charm. The most important finding may be about what AI does to our own beliefs. In ["A Rational Analysis of the Effects of Sycophantic AI"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14270) (2026), Rafael Batista and Thomas Griffiths of Princeton asked 557 people to discover a hidden rule in a classic reasoning puzzle with help from AI assistants. An ordinary, unmodified assistant reduced people's chances of finding the rule and inflated their confidence about as much as an assistant deliberately told to agree with them. Assistants that offered unbiased evidence produced discovery rates about five times higher.

**Why it matters.** Prompting rituals are a modern version of Skinner's pigeons: a behaviour that seemed to work once, reinforced by occasional success, and repeated without testing. For organisations building AI into their work, the remedy is the same as for any superstition: test it. Keep a small set of real tasks with known answers, and check whether a new prompt, model or technique actually improves results across them, rather than relying on a vivid example or a viral tip. Batista and Griffiths's finding is a deeper warning. Superstition thrives on confirming evidence, and an AI that tends to agree with its user is a powerful source of it. When the stakes are high, ask AI tools to argue against your hypothesis, to look for counterexamples, or to tell you what evidence would prove you wrong. The aim is not to stop trusting AI, but to stop it from becoming the timer that feeds the pigeon.

*Source: Meincke, Mollick, Mollick & Shapiro, arXiv 2508.00614 (2025); Basil et al., arXiv 2512.05858 (2025); Batorski et al., arXiv 2605.29678 (2026); Batista & Griffiths, arXiv 2602.14270 (2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: where are the drowned?** — in 1620 the philosopher Francis Bacon published the *Novum Organum*, his attempt to put scientific reasoning on a firmer footing. Among the "idols of the mind" that lead us astray, he warned, is our tendency to see more order in the world than there is. To illustrate, he retold an old story from Cicero. A man was shown a temple wall covered with pictures of sailors who had made vows to the gods and been saved from shipwreck, and was asked whether he now accepted the power of the gods. ["Aye," he replied, "but where are they painted that were drowned after their vows?"](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygfit/ai_05_mapping_directions/bacon_organon.htm) It is perhaps the clearest statement ever made of survivorship bias, and of how superstition survives: we remember the hits and never see the misses. (Bacon, Novum Organum I.46, Spedding translation)

**🃏 WILDCARD: monkeys believe in streaks** — the belief that a run of wins will continue, known in sport as the "hot hand", may be older than humanity. In a study in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology* (2014), Tommy Blanchard, Andreas Wilke and Benjamin Hayden had rhesus monkeys play a simple computer game for rewards. When the rewards followed a pattern, the monkeys learned it quickly. When the rewards were random, they [kept behaving as though streaks would continue](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/monkeys-also-believe-in-winning-streaks-study-shows/), over weeks of play. The researchers suggest the bias may come from foraging, where food such as fruit tends to come in clusters, so assuming that good luck will continue usually pays. (J. Exp. Psychology: Animal Learning and Cognition, Jul 2014)

**The hot hand was real after all** — for decades, the "hot hand" in basketball was the textbook example of a superstition: a famous 1985 study found no evidence that players who had made several shots were more likely to make the next. Then, in [a 2018 paper](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2018/11/01/surprised-hot-hand-fallacy-truth-law-small-numbers) in *Econometrica*, Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo showed that the original analysis contained a subtle statistical bias, which made streaks look less likely than they really were. Corrected, the data suggested that hot hands do exist. Sometimes the superstition is right and the debunking is wrong, which is another reason to keep testing. (Econometrica, Nov 2018)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Edmund Burke is not usually quoted as a defender of superstition. In his *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), he was writing about the place of religion in public life, and he chose his words carefully. ["Superstition is the religion of feeble minds,"](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eccodemo/k043880.0001.001/237?view=image) he wrote, "and they must be tolerated in an intermixture of it, in some trifling or some enthusiastic shape or other, else you will deprive weak minds of a resource found necessary to the strongest." The first half of the sentence is the one people remember. The second half is the more interesting one. Burke saw that rituals, habits and small consolations are not just for the weak; even the strongest minds need something to steady them. The research seems to agree. Keep the ritual if it calms you or binds your team. Just don't confuse it with the cause of your success, and when a superstition starts making your decisions, ask where the drowned are painted.
