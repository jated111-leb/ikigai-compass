# The Daily Compass #051 — How we read now

*Monday, September 14, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

There is a particular grief loose in the culture: that we have lost the ability to read. Not to decode words — that's fine — but to sink into a long text and stay there, to follow a difficult argument or a slow novel to its end without the mind bolting for the nearest lit screen. The grief is real, and this issue takes it seriously. But it also complicates it from three directions. The first is a reminder of what deep reading actually *is*, at its fullest — not consumption but a slow, repeated, transfiguring encounter that changes as you do. The second is a diagnosis that turns the whole story on its head: our shattered attention is not a moral failing or a lost virtue but a *design* problem, an artifact of the habitats we've built to think in, and therefore fixable. And the third is an account of the stakes — of what specifically dies when we let every empty, bored, frictionful moment get filled. Underneath all of it sits an older and more defiant claim, made once in this very city: that literature is not a tool for anything, that its whole dignity is to be an end in itself. This is an issue about reading — which is to say, about the last widely available technology of deep interior life, and whether we intend to keep it.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Reading a book twice

**What happened.** The novelist Jhumpa Lahiri has published one of the most beautiful pieces of criticism of the year — ["Quiver and Fixity: On Rereading *Jude the Obscure*,"](https://yalereview.org/article/jhumpa-lahiri-quiver-and-fixity) delivered first as a lecture at Yale and printed this spring in *The Yale Review*. It is an account of returning, in midlife, to Thomas Hardy's last and bleakest novel and finding it transfigured — a book she thought she knew revealing itself as a kind of ghost story of shadows and specters, its hero Jude sunk in an "extreme state of isolation" she reads now against Greek myth and the Italian solitary Cesare Pavese. Her deeper subject is the strange physics of language itself: all literary language, she argues, "quivers constantly between meaning and nonmeaning," and reading — especially *re*reading, and the translation she practices alongside it — is the art of staying present inside that shimmer, never fixing it, never quite done.

**Why it matters.** This is the thing the culture is mourning, shown at full power — and it turns out to be almost the opposite of what "reading" usually names now. Lahiri's reading is slow, recursive, and personal to the point of intimacy; the book is not information to be extracted but a room she re-enters at a different age and finds rearranged. That is deep reading's real gift and its real difficulty: it is not fast, cannot be summarized, resists the very extraction our tools are built to perform, and pays out only to the patient. It is also, quietly, a model of how to hold anything worth holding — a person, a faith, a hard problem — in the quivering state between fixed and lost. You cannot skim it, and you cannot have an AI do it for you, because the point was never the conclusion. The point was the time spent inside the ambiguity, and the self that time slowly builds.

*Source: Lahiri, The Yale Review (Spring 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. It's not you, it's the habitat

**What happened.** Against the tide of doom about collapsing literacy, a librarian named Carlo Iacono offers a bracing reframe in *Aeon* — ["What we think is a decline in literacy is a design problem."](https://aeon.co/essays/what-we-think-is-a-decline-in-literacy-is-a-design-problem) His argument: reading did not work for centuries because text is magic. It worked because books came with *built-in boundaries* — they end, the pages hold still, and the places we read them (libraries, quiet rooms) were engineered for depth. Those weren't features of literacy; they were features of the *habitat* where literacy lived. Strip a person of that habitat and drop them into a feed optimized to fracture attention for profit, and their inability to focus is not a personal failure and not the death of the reading mind — it is exactly what the environment was built to produce. "The choice isn't between books and screens," he writes. "The choice is between intentional design and profitable chaos."

**Why it matters.** This is the most useful thing anyone has said about the attention crisis in a while, because it moves the problem from character to architecture — from shame to design, which is to say, from despair to something you can actually fix. The doomer says deep reading is dying and we are becoming shallower people; Iacono says deep reading is *homeless*, and homes can be built. It reframes every anxious parent, every distracted professional, every one of us who can't get through the long article: the failure isn't in your soul, it's in the room, and the room was designed by someone whose profit depends on your distraction. That should make you angrier and more hopeful at once. The cathedral of quiet that a library represents was a deliberate human achievement, not a state of nature — and if we could build it once, in stone, we can build it again, including in the software that now holds most of our reading. The question is simply whether anyone with power wants focus to win.

*Source: Iacono, Aeon (Feb 2026). ~12 min.*

### ◆ 3. What we lose when we're never bored

**What happened.** And here are the stakes, named precisely. Christine Rosen — whose book *The Extinction of Experience* has become a touchstone for this whole argument — writes on ["The Vanishing Gifts of Boredom,"](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2026/01/the-vanishing-gifts-of-boredom/) a defense of the empty moment. Boredom, she argues, is not a bug to be patched but a teacher: it builds mental discipline, it schools us in the plain fact that we can't always control our environment, and above all it gives the mind the *fallow* time it needs — the unstructured, unstimulated blankness out of which imagination and reflection actually grow. When hundreds of millions of people reflexively fill every gap with a feed, she warns, we don't just lose personal depth; we cultivate a civilizational impatience — a diminishing capacity to sit with anything hard, whether a book, a relationship, or a genuinely difficult political problem.

**Why it matters.** Deep reading and empty time turn out to be the same endangered resource seen from two angles: both are what happens when the mind is left alone long enough to do its slow work, and both are precisely what the attention economy is engineered to abolish. Rosen's contribution is to show that the cost is not merely private. A population that cannot tolerate boredom cannot tolerate difficulty, and a civilization that cannot tolerate difficulty cannot solve the problems — climate, governance, the ethics of the very machines filling our idle minutes — that require sustained, uncomfortable, unrewarded attention to even understand. The stakes of the reading crisis, in other words, are not nostalgic. The capacity we're losing in the small moments (waiting for a bus, filling a silence) is the same capacity we will desperately need in the large ones. You practice for the hard book, and for the hard century, in exactly the places we've stopped practicing at all: the boring ones.

*Source: Rosen, Christianity Today (early 2026). ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: City of Beginnings** — Robyn Creswell, *City of Beginnings: Poetic Modernism in Beirut* (Princeton, 2019). The most defiant answer to this whole issue was given here, in this city, seventy years ago. Creswell's intellectual history recovers the circle around the legendary Beirut magazine *Shi'r* ("Poetry") — Adonis, Yusuf al-Khal, Unsi al-Hajj — who in the 1950s set out to put Arabic verse "on the map of world literature," and who insisted, against enormous political pressure to enlist their art for the cause, on a firewall between poetry and politics: the radical claim that a poem answers to nothing but itself. Why now: it is the deep root of everything the briefs above are reaching for. Lahiri's slow reading, Iacono's cathedral of quiet, Rosen's fallow time all rest on the same unfashionable conviction the *Shi'r* poets staked their careers on — that some human activities are ends, not means, and that their uselessness *is* their dignity. In an age that wants every minute to be productive and every text to be extractable, the Beirut modernists' insistence on the sovereign, useless, world-making poem is not a museum piece. It is a battle plan. ([Princeton](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691182186/city-of-beginnings), 2019)

**🃏 WILDCARD: permission to quit** — a delightfully liberating counter-note from the critic Michel Chaouli in *The Yale Review* — ["Do You Actually Have to Finish It?"](https://yalereview.org/article/michel-chaouli-ending-it) — on the strange moral tyranny of the last page, the guilt that keeps us grimly marching through books we've stopped loving. His argument, light-footed but serious, is that the obligation to finish can be the enemy of the pleasure that made us pick the book up, and that abandoning a text can itself be an act of reading well. A small permission with large consequences: the deepest readers are often the most ruthless quitters, because they are reading for the encounter, not the certificate. (2026)

**We were always going to hell in a handcart** — a useful splash of cold water on the panic in briefs 2 and 3: an [Aeon essay by Joe Stadolnik](https://aeon.co/essays/weve-always-been-distracted-or-at-least-worried-that-we-are) documents that the fear of ruined attention is basically ancient. Seneca griped about the scattering of the Roman mind; the Song scholar Zhu Xi worried readers were skating over texts; Reformation-era complaints about distraction abound. The "golden age of deep attention" we imagine we've fallen from is, in every era, located just slightly in the past. It doesn't disprove the current crisis — this time the distraction is engineered and monetized, which is new — but it's a bracing reminder that "kids can't focus anymore" has been true, and false, for two thousand years. (Aeon)

**The novel that can't be filmed** — a prescient 2016 essay by Erik Hoel in *The New Atlantis*, ["Fiction in the Age of Screens,"](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/fiction-in-the-age-of-screens) reads better every year: he argues the novel's survival against film and prestige TV lies precisely in doing what screens *can't* — the interior voice, the digression, the fusion of essay and story and memory that would be destroyed by adaptation. A decade on, as "will it stream?" quietly reshapes what gets written, his prescription holds: literature's future is the untranslatable book, the one whose whole reason for existing is that you have to read it. (2016)

**The base rate always wins** — Michael Mauboussin's ["Bayes and Base Rates,"](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/en-us/individual-investor/insights/consilient-observer/bayes-and-base-rates-2.html) updated this year, is the numerate cousin of deep reading: the discipline of checking a dazzling story against the boring historical distribution. Running growth forecasts against ~19,000 firm-histories back to 1950, he notes that some celebrated projections — OpenAI's implied ~118% annual growth to 2029 among them — would be flatly unprecedented for any comparable company on record. Not impossible; just something no firm has ever done. The outside view is to narratives what rereading is to a novel: the slow, unglamorous second look that keeps you honest. (2026)

**The frontier gets a speed limit** — the week's genuinely notable AI news was a governance one. In an essay, ["We Must Pace the Frontier,"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ceo-outlines-plan-to-pace-the-frontier/) Anthropic's Dario Amodei argued the industry should deliberately *slow the rate* of capability gain, and — the concrete part — pledged to give third-party evaluators like METR permanent, employee-level access to verify that safety commitments are real. The startling bit was the response: within hours, Sam Altman ("I agree with Dario," and OpenAI "will do the same") and Elon Musk ("Dario is right") publicly concurred — a rare, genuine alignment among three bitter rivals, triggered by this summer's rogue-agent incident. A mechanism, not a manifesto; worth watching whether it survives contact with competition. (Sep 12)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Notice that the argument for deep reading is never, quite, about reading. Lahiri rereading Hardy is really describing how to stay present inside something ambiguous without forcing it shut. Iacono's cathedral of quiet is really about who gets to design the rooms we think in. Rosen's defense of boredom is really a defense of the fallow, uncontrolled interior where a self is grown rather than fed. And the Beirut poets who built a firewall around the useless poem were really defending the last territory that answers to no one — the inner life, sovereign and unproductive and free. Put together, they suggest that "we can't read anymore" is the surface reading of a deeper loss: the steady enclosure of the last unmonetized, unmeasured, undirected spaces in a human day. The good news in this issue is Iacono's, and it is real: this is a design problem, not a death, and design can be changed. But it will not change by accident, because the current design is extremely profitable, and no feed will ever volunteer to make you harder to interrupt. The defense of deep reading, then, is not nostalgia and not a self-improvement tip. It is something closer to a political act — the deliberate, counter-cultural decision to keep building quiet rooms, to guard the empty hour, to reread the difficult book slowly and on purpose, and to insist, as three poets did in this city under far worse pressure, that some of what makes us human is gloriously good for nothing at all. The screen is not going away. But the choice of what to do in the ten free minutes is still, for now, ours. Spend one of them somewhere it's hard to reach you.
