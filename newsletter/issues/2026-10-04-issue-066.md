# The Daily Compass #066 — Below replacement

*Sunday, October 4, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Somewhere in the last year or so, probably without anyone noticing, humanity crossed a line it has never crossed before. For the first time, and not because of war, famine or plague, the average woman on Earth is likely having fewer children than it takes to keep the population steady. Nobody announced it, because nobody can see it directly: the official numbers arrive years late, and the United Nations has pushed its next major population projections back to 2027. But two economists have now made the case carefully, and it reframes a lot of what we argue about. The anxieties about automation, pensions, labor shortages, housing, even the meaning of the future, look different once you assume the world is about to start shrinking. This issue takes the claim seriously, then gives the strongest case against panic, and then turns to the gap that may matter most: between the families people want and the ones they end up having.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Terra incognita

**What happened.** In ["Terra Incognita: The Economics of a Shrinking World,"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35809) prepared for the *Annual Review of Economics* and circulated through NBER in September, Jesús Fernández-Villaverde and Patrick Norrick of the University of Pennsylvania estimate that the world is probably already below replacement fertility. They also point out a detail that changes the arithmetic. The familiar figure of 2.1 children per woman is the replacement rate for a rich country with low child mortality, and for the world as a whole the true threshold is closer to 2.2. Their projection has global population peaking around 2056 at roughly nine billion, almost three decades earlier than the UN's central scenario. Fitting a simple statistical model to 236 countries since 1950, they find that the common global force pushing fertility down peaked in 1978. What is driving the decline today is country-specific trends, and 219 of them point downward. The fall is now concentrated in low- and middle-income countries and among poorer and less educated women, the reverse of the old pattern. Their conjecture about the root cause is blunt: modernity itself, which makes a third child expensive and childlessness cheap.

**Why it matters.** Most of the public conversation still treats very low fertility as a quirk of a few rich East Asian countries. The paper's point is that South Korea and Japan were early, not unusual. Thailand, Colombia, Chile and many other middle-income countries are now at fertility levels that no society in history has experienced, and they are reaching them before they are rich, without the savings, pensions or productivity that ageing societies normally rely on. The "modernity" explanation is also uncomfortable for anyone hoping for a policy fix. If the cause is not a particular tax code or housing market but the whole package of urban life, women's education, careers and the opportunity cost of time, then baby bonuses are pushing against the current of everything else people value. The title is the right one. We are entering territory with no map, and the honest starting position is that nobody yet knows what a shrinking world feels like to live in.

*Source: Fernández-Villaverde & Norrick, NBER Working Paper 35809 / PIER (Aug–Sep 2026). ~60 min; the introduction alone is worth it.*

### ◆ 2. Fewer babies, more machines

**What happened.** The standard fear is that fewer young people means slower growth: fewer workers, fewer consumers, fewer inventors. Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne and Andrew Scott test that fear in ["Baby Busts and Growth Booms,"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401) an NBER working paper from July, and find the opposite of what you might expect, at least so far. Across countries, lower birth rates go with *higher* growth in GDP per working-age adult. Across U.S. commuting zones, they go with higher wage growth, with no visible damage to aggregate output or earnings. The mechanism they propose is that technology responds to scarcity. Where young workers become scarce, firms and inventors put their effort into labor-saving machines, and places with lower birth rates show more labor-saving patents and faster growth in high-tech activity. To separate cause from coincidence, the authors use the uneven toll of the Second World War, which thinned some countries' young populations far more than others. What seems to matter is the shrinking share of younger workers, not the size of the population as such.

**Why it matters.** This is the strongest case against demographic panic, and it should be read alongside a harder long-run argument. The Stanford economist Charles Jones, in ["The End of Economic Growth?"](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/baby-bust-could-population-decline-spell-end-economic-growth) (*American Economic Review*, 2022), shows that in standard growth models new ideas come from people, so a population that shrinks forever can produce an "Empty Planet," where living standards stagnate as the number of researchers dwindles. The two papers are not really in conflict. They describe different time scales. Over decades, scarcity of young workers can push economies toward automation and raise output per worker, as Acemoglu and colleagues find. Over generations, a permanently shrinking pool of minds is a different and deeper problem, unless something other than people starts producing ideas. That is exactly why the demographic debate and the AI debate are becoming the same debate. Whether a smaller humanity keeps getting richer may depend less on birth rates than on whether machines can do some of the inventing.

*Source: Acemoglu, Autor, Beirne & Scott, NBER Working Paper 35401 (Jul 2026); Jones, AER (2022). ~45 min for the paper; the NBER Digest summary is 5.*

### ◆ 3. The families people don't have

**What happened.** The Institute for Family Studies marked America's 250th birthday with ["The Demographic Dead End,"](https://ifstudies.org/report-brief/the-demographic-dead-end-2026-state-of-fertility-report) its 2026 State of Fertility report by the demographer Lyman Stone and Peter Foreshaw Brookes. Its foundation is an unusually long reconstruction of American fertility: state birth rates back to 1917, and Massachusetts back to 1660. On that long view, U.S. fertility is now below 1.6 children per woman, and if the recent decline continues, the population peaks around 351 million and starts falling in the 2050s. The most important number in the report, though, is not the fertility rate. It is the gap between that rate and what Americans say they want: on average, about 2.4 children. The authors argue that the gap is not mainly about money. They point to changes in friendship and social life, fewer close friends, fewer couples among one's friends, a culture that makes family life seem less normal, as causes that cash payments cannot reach.

**Why it matters.** This brief shifts the question from economics to something closer to the reader's own life. Brief 1 treats falling fertility as the aggregate result of rational choices in a modern economy. Brief 2 suggests economies can adapt. But if people consistently end up with fewer children than they say they want, then at least part of the decline is not a preference being satisfied. It is a preference being frustrated, by late partnering, by housing, by careers that peak in the same years as fertility, by social circles where children have become rare. IFS has a pronatalist point of view, and its proposed remedies are contestable. The gap it measures, however, shows up in surveys across many countries, and it is a more humane frame than either "crisis" or "choice." The question it leaves is not "how do we make people have more children?" but "what keeps people from the lives they say they want?"

*Source: Stone & Brookes, Institute for Family Studies (Jul 9, 2026). ~20 min for the brief.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the wager about scarcity** — the most famous bet in the history of this argument was settled in October 1990, when the biologist Paul Ehrlich mailed the economist Julian Simon a check for [$576.07](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon%E2%80%93Ehrlich_wager). Ehrlich, author of *The Population Bomb* (1968), believed a growing population would exhaust resources. Simon believed human ingenuity would make resources more abundant. In 1980 Ehrlich chose a $1,000 basket of five metals (copper, chromium, nickel, tin and tungsten) and bet their real prices would rise over the decade. All five fell, and the basket lost more than half its value. Economists have argued since about how much luck was involved (over many other ten-year stretches, Ehrlich would have won), but Simon's core claim, that people are the "ultimate resource" because they produce the ideas that relieve scarcity, is the same one Charles Jones formalized in brief 2. Ehrlich died this March at 93. The fear his book spread has quietly flipped: the worry now is not too many people but too few. ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/03/paul-ehrlich-population-bomb-ecologist-dies-at-93/), Mar 2026)

**🃏 WILDCARD: a little England in Rome** — beneath the walled garden of the Palazzo della Rovere, a stone's throw from St. Peter's, excavators have found 20 [Anglo-Saxon coins](https://www.medievalists.net/2026/10/anglo-saxon-coins-reveal-medieval-englands-little-england-in-rome/) (19 silver pennies and a halfpenny) struck in England over about 40 years in the reigns of Edward the Elder, Æthelstan and Eadred. The site lies in what was the Schola Saxonum, the English quarter of early medieval Rome. Researchers including Rory Naismith and Francesca Tinti argue that the finds show it was not just a hostel for pilgrims but a working neighborhood of expatriates who lived, traded and, unusually, seem to have been allowed to spend their own currency. More Anglo-Saxon coins from the period have now turned up in Rome than papal ones. Migration and diaspora are as old as cities; a thousand years ago, the English were the immigrants. (*Rome and the Others (300–1000)*, Oct 2026)

**The rebound that isn't a reversal** — South Korea, the world's lowest-fertility country, is having its strongest baby rebound on record: births in the first half of 2026 were up 15.4% on a year earlier, the highest first-half total since 2019, and the fertility rate has climbed from 0.72 in 2023 to 0.80 in 2025. Before reading it as a turnaround, note why: a wave of marriages postponed during the pandemic, and a larger cohort of women born in the early 1990s now in their thirties. Even at the higher rate, each generation is still less than half the size of the one before. ([Korea JoongAng Daily](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/opinion/more-babies-on-board/12859024); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2026/08/26/South-Korean-births-rise-for-second-year-as-fertility-rate-hits-080/6841787788113/), Aug 2026)

**AI favors the old** — the one AI item this week comes from two OECD economists, Christophe André and Matthias Schief, who put the two big long-run forces side by side in ["A boost from AI in ageing societies?"](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2026/09/28/a-boost-from-artifical-intelligence-in-ageing-societies/) Ageing is set to drag on growth across OECD countries, and AI could offset much of that if it spreads as fast as computers and the internet did. Their most interesting finding is about who is exposed. Workers' overall exposure to AI peaks in mid-career, but exposure to *automation* is highest among the young and falls quickly with age, because experience complements AI rather than competing with it. An ageing workforce may be better suited to AI than a young one, and the people with the most to lose are those just starting out. (OECD Ecoscope, Sep 28, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In his Tanner Lectures, published as [*Death and the Afterlife*](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Scheffler-Lecture.pdf) (2013), the philosopher Samuel Scheffler asks you to imagine a world borrowed from the novel *The Children of Men*: everyone currently alive will live out a normal life, but no more children will ever be born. Nobody is harmed and nobody dies early. Yet almost everyone finds the scenario devastating, and Scheffler argues that our reaction shows something we rarely notice. Much of what gives our lives meaning, research, building, teaching, art, even planting a tree, depends on the assumption that other people will come after us. In certain respects, he suggests, the survival of humanity after we are gone matters to us more than our own survival. We care about a "collective afterlife" of strangers we will never meet. Today's briefs describe a world very far from his thought experiment. Babies are still being born by the tens of millions, and the economies of a shrinking world may well keep growing. But the gap in brief 3, between the families people want and the families they have, suggests that something is pulling against a desire Scheffler shows to be deep. The right response is neither panic nor indifference. It is to take seriously that the future is not only something we forecast. It is something we bring about, partly in decisions about whether to bring new people into it, and partly in whether we build a world in which they would want to arrive.
