# The Daily Compass #108 — Two kinds of strife

*Tuesday, November 17, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Around 700 BC, a Greek farmer-poet named Hesiod wrote a long poem of advice to his brother Perses, who had cheated him out of part of their inheritance. Near the start of *Works and Days*, Hesiod corrects the old myths. There is not one goddess of Strife, he says, but two. One "fosters evil war and battle, being cruel: her no man loves." The other is far kinder. "She stirs up even the shiftless to toil; for a man grows eager to work when he considers his neighbour, a rich man who hastens to plough and plant and put his house in good order; and neighbour vies with his neighbour as he hurries after wealth. This Strife is wholesome for men. And potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel." ([Hesiod, *Works and Days*, tr. Evelyn-White](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodWorksDays.html)) Twenty-seven centuries later, researchers have confirmed Hesiod's insight with sports data, lab experiments and factory floors: rivals make us work harder. They have also confirmed his warning, because the two Strifes turn out to be sisters. This issue is about rivalry: what it does to people, to teams inside a company and to the race to build ever more powerful AI.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The psychology of rivalry

**What happened.** Economists usually treat competition as impersonal: firms respond to prices, not to people. The NYU psychologist Gavin Kilduff has spent fifteen years showing that rivalry is different. In ["The Psychology of Rivalry"](https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/management/Kilduff/KilduffEtAl%20AMJ%202010.pdf) (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2010), Kilduff, Hillary Anger Elfenbein and Barry Staw defined rivalry as a competitive *relationship*, in which the stakes depend on who the opponent is and on the history between you, not just on what is objectively at stake. Studying American college basketball, they found that rivalries were strongest between teams that were similar, had played each other often and had a history of close games, and that teams played harder defence against their rivals. Rivalry can make people faster. Analysing six years of results from an amateur running club, Kilduff found that runners ran about [five seconds per kilometre faster](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/the-upside-of-rivalry-higher-motivation-better-performance.html) when a rival was in the race (*Social Psychological and Personality Science*, 2014). It can also make them cheat. In ["Whatever It Takes to Win"](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/47395/) (*AMJ*, 2016), Kilduff, Adam Galinsky, Edoardo Gallo and James Reade found across several studies that rivalry increased deception and willingness to use unethical tactics, regardless of the tangible stakes; merely thinking about a rival increased unethical behaviour in unrelated tasks. And it makes people gamble. In more than 2,000 American football games, Christy To and colleagues found that teams were more likely to [go for it on fourth down](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Going-for-it-on-fourth-down-Rivalry-increases-risk-taking-physiological-arousal-and-promotion-focus.pdf) and attempt riskier plays against rivals (*AMJ*, 2018). Companies behave the same way. When Airbus launched its re-engined A320neo in December 2010 and won a huge order from American Airlines in July 2011, Boeing abandoned plans for a new aircraft and rushed out an updated 737, the 737 MAX. After two fatal crashes, a [congressional investigation](https://democrats-transportation.house.gov/news/press-releases/after-18-month-investigation-chairs-defazio-and-larsen-release-final-committee-report-on-boeing-737-max) concluded in 2020 that "tremendous financial pressure" to compete with the A320neo had led Boeing to put production priorities above safety.

**Why it matters.** Rivalry is one of the most powerful motivators a leader can harness, and one of the most dangerous. The same features that make it motivating, similarity, a long shared history and close contests, raise the psychological stakes until winning against *this* opponent matters more than anything else. Kilduff has argued that rivalries between firms work in the same way, leading companies to "hot", emotional strategic moves that market logic alone would not justify ([*Academy of Management Review*, 2019](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/faculty-research/hot-headed-business-rivalries-impact-strategic-decision-making-according-new-paper)). The practical lesson is to use rivalry for effort and to guard against it in judgement. Let a rival push the pace of execution, but take big decisions, about safety, ethics and large bets, in rooms where the rival's name is not the first thing on the slide. Ask of any move: would we do this if they had not done it first?

*Source: Kilduff, Elfenbein & Staw, AMJ 53(5) (2010); Kilduff, SPPS 5(8) (2014); Kilduff, Galinsky, Gallo & Reade, AMJ 59(5) (2016); To, Kilduff, Ordóñez & Schweitzer, AMJ 61(4) (2018); Kilduff, AMR 44(4) (2019); House Transportation Committee (Sep 16, 2020). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Rivals under one roof

**What happened.** Many companies deliberately create rivalry among their own employees, and the results are mixed. The bluntest tool is forced ranking, in which managers must grade a fixed share of staff as top, middle and bottom performers. GE made it famous; Microsoft used it for years until Kurt Eichenwald reported in *Vanity Fair* in 2012 that every current and former employee he interviewed cited it as the most destructive process inside the company. In November 2013, Microsoft [scrapped it](https://www.theregister.com/2013/11/12/microsoft_kills_stack_ranking_reviews/). Field experiments show why the evidence is so contested. When a firm began telling workers where they stood relative to colleagues, with no prize attached, productivity rose and stayed higher ([Blanes i Vidal & Nossol, *Management Science*, 2011](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/blanesiv/tournaments.pdf)). But among teams of fruit pickers on a British farm, publicly ranking teams without prizes cut average productivity by 14%, while a tournament with real prizes raised it by 24% ([Bandiera, Barankay & Rasul, *JEEA*, 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jeurec/v11y2013i5p1079-1114.html)). Rivalry can also turn destructive. In laboratory experiments, ranking feedback raised effort but also led some participants to pay to sabotage others' scores or to inflate their own ([Charness, Masclet & Villeval, *Management Science*, 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:1:p:38-55)); when sabotage was possible, it more than wiped out the gains from competition ([Carpenter, Matthews & Schirm, *AER*, 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:100:y:2010:i:1:p:504-17)). Design matters. Studying 150 teams of Xerox service technicians, Ruth Wageman found that teams did best when tasks and rewards were consistently either collective or individual, and worst when the two were mixed ([*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1995](https://scilit.com/publications/455c97668e942c9f1b6afee6a7dafc05)). And the most useful rival may sit next to you. Analysing more than 21,000 Formula 1 driver-races, Michael Lapré and Candace Cravey found that drivers learned from their teammates' successes and failures, but not from their own driving errors ([*Management Science*, 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v68y2022i12p8741-8756.html)).

**Why it matters.** Internal rivalry works when the competition is over something visible and measurable, when people cannot easily harm each other's results, and when the rewards fit the way the work is actually done. It fails when work depends on cooperation, when people can sabotage or hoard, and when rankings are imposed in a way that humiliates rather than informs. Before designing a contest inside a team, a leader should ask three questions. Is the work individual or interdependent? Can competitors damage each other's results? And does the ranking tell people something useful, or just tell them where they stand in the pecking order? The best internal rivals are like F1 teammates: same car, same data, every reason to learn from each other.

*Source: Eichenwald, Vanity Fair (Aug 2012); The Register (Nov 12, 2013); Blanes i Vidal & Nossol, Management Science 57(10) (2011); Bandiera, Barankay & Rasul, JEEA 11(5) (2013); Charness, Masclet & Villeval, Management Science 60(1) (2014); Carpenter, Matthews & Schirm, AER 100(1) (2010); Wageman, ASQ 40 (1995); Lapré & Cravey, Management Science 68(12) (2022). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. The race nobody can slow alone

**What happened.** The leading AI companies, and the governments behind them, describe their competition as a race, and a decade-old model explains why that is worrying. In ["Racing to the precipice"](https://stafforini.com/works/armstrong-2016-racing-precipice-model/) (*AI & Society*, 2016), Stuart Armstrong, Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman modelled teams racing to build a powerful AI, each deciding how much to spend on safety. More teams and more enmity raised the risk of disaster. More surprisingly, so did more information: the better teams could see each other's progress, the more they skimped on safety to stay ahead. The real race has become highly visible. Epoch AI [estimates](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/us-vs-china-eci) that Chinese models have trailed the American frontier by about seven months on average since 2023, within a range of four to fourteen; Stanford's 2026 AI Index, using a different measure, says the gap has [effectively closed](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report). Testers have complained of less time to evaluate new models: in 2025, the evaluation firm METR said it had tested one major release in a ["relatively short time"](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/16/openai-partner-says-it-had-relatively-little-time-to-test-the-companys-new-ai-models). Yet rivals are also starting to cooperate. In August 2025, OpenAI and Anthropic [ran safety tests on each other's models](https://openai.com/index/openai-anthropic-safety-evaluation/). In September 2026, after talks between Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Vice Premier He Lifeng, the United States and China agreed to set up a [channel for AI safety incidents](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/china-and-u-s-agree-to-establish-ai-safety-channel-and-continue-trade-and-military-talks) and a regular AI dialogue, with no commitment to slow development. The next round is due in [Shenzhen](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/world/article/343409/US-China-to-meet-again-on-AI-safety-in-two-months-in-Shenzhen-Bessent-says), on the sidelines of the APEC meetings, this week.

**Why it matters.** The AI race has the classic structure of a prisoner's dilemma: every player may prefer a slower, safer pace, but none will slow down alone for fear of being overtaken. Kilduff's research adds a human layer. These are rivals in his sense, similar, with a long shared history and close contests, which is exactly when people take more risks and bend more rules. The way out of such dilemmas is rarely unilateral virtue. It is verification and shared rules: outside testers with real access, common safety standards, incident channels that work in a crisis. For leaders deploying AI, the lesson is practical. Do not assume that the speed of the race reflects the maturity of the product. Test the systems you adopt yourself, and treat a vendor's release date as a competitive decision, not a safety guarantee.

*Source: Armstrong, Bostrom & Shulman, AI & Society 31(2) (2016); Epoch AI (Jan 2026); Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026; TechCrunch (Apr 16, 2025); OpenAI (Aug 27, 2025); PBS NewsHour (Sep 2026); The Standard (Sep 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the judge who wrote his own verdict** — in the early 1700s, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz fought bitterly over who had invented calculus. When Leibniz appealed to the Royal Society for a fair hearing, the Society's president, Newton himself, appointed a committee of his supporters, never asked for Leibniz's side and wrote much of the committee's report himself. Its findings were published in 1712 as the *Commercium Epistolicum*, and Newton then [reviewed the book anonymously](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/pub/HistMath/People/Newton/CommerciumAccount) in the Society's journal. Historians now credit both men with inventing calculus independently. The feud did lasting damage: British mathematicians clung to Newton's clumsier notation for a century while the continent built on Leibniz's. Rivalry can corrupt even the referees. (Royal Society, 1712–15)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the head on the wrong end** — the "Bone Wars" between the American palaeontologists Othniel Marsh and Edward Drinker Cope in the late nineteenth century were a scientific gold rush. Racing each other, they named [roughly 140 new species of dinosaur](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/bone-wars-paleontology-feud) between them, including such household names as *Triceratops*, *Stegosaurus* and *Diplodocus*, but many of their hastily named species have since been discarded. The race began with an embarrassment: Cope reconstructed the marine reptile *Elasmosaurus* with its head on the end of its tail, Marsh pointed it out, and Cope reportedly tried to buy up every copy of the journal. Rivalry is a good accelerator and a poor editor. (National Geographic)

**The race back to the Moon** — the new space race is slipping on both sides. After NASA restructured its Artemis programme in February, Artemis III became a 2027 test flight in Earth orbit, with the first crewed landing moved to Artemis IV, [targeted for 2028](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/10/23/science/nasa-artemis-update-spacex-starship-blue-origin). China still aims to land astronauts before 2030, though its south-pole probe Chang'e-7 was [delayed to 2027](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/china-delays-ambitious-change-7-moon-mission-until-2027/) in August, citing safety. Both sides' timelines depend on hardware still in testing. The race has made the Moon a priority again; whether it also makes the missions riskier is the question to watch. (CNN, Oct 2026; Scientific American, Aug 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Around the year 100, Plutarch wrote a short essay with a surprising title: ["How to Profit by One's Enemies"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/De_capienda%2A.html). "It is a trait of the man of sense," he wrote, crediting the idea to Xenophon, "to derive profit even from his enemies." An enemy, Plutarch argued, watches you more closely than any friend, notices every failing and so keeps you honest. He tells of the philosopher Diogenes, asked how to defend himself against an enemy. "By proving yourself good and honourable," came the answer. It is Hesiod's good Strife put as advice, and the best answer to the bad one. A rival can make you better or worse. Which it does depends less on the rival than on what you are trying to beat them at.
