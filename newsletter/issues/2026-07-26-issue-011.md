# The Daily Compass #011 — Destruction and creation

*Sunday, July 26, 2026 · ~18 minute read*

A Sunday issue built on a fifty-year-old idea from a fighter pilot: you cannot build a new mental model until you tear the old one apart. Roger Martin says your entire education is a tide pulling against strategic thought; a neuroscientist asks what a machine would have to *do* to deserve moral consideration; and two very different documents — a scenario and a survey — agree that work's deepest product was never output but identity. Destruction first, then creation.

---

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The tide that pulls against strategic thinking

**What happened.** Roger Martin's book-club series reached the chapter that earns the whole subscription: ["What is Strategic Thinking?"](https://rogerlmartin.substack.com/p/ptwpi-all-stars-book-club-chapter-b73) (Jul 13). His answer has four characteristics: it targets what you *don't* control (customers, not budgets); it consumes information omnivorously, refusing to privilege statistically significant data about a past that won't repeat; it holds multiple variables at once against the siloed, sequential habit; and — the load-bearing one — it runs on Charles Sanders Peirce's *abductive* logic: inference to the best explanation, the only logic that can produce something genuinely new.

**The excerpt:**

> "That teaching and subsequent organizational socialization is the tide – a tide that seeks to prevent you from being a strategic thinker."

**Why it matters.** Every analytical tool you were taught — regression on history, deduction from accepted premises — is machinery for extrapolating the past, and Martin's point is that organizations then *socialize* you into treating that machinery as the whole of rigor. Abduction can't be run on a spreadsheet; it's a leap to a hypothesis you then test. This lands differently in 2026: the machines we've built are superb at induction and deduction — pattern from precedent, conclusion from premise — which means the tide just got an engine. The competitive residue is exactly the leap. Today's ⏳ timeless pick is the deep theory of this brief: John Boyd worked out in 1976 why the tearing-down has to come first, and why the side that dissolves and rebuilds its concepts fastest wins. Read them as one argument written 50 years apart.

*Source: Roger Martin, Playing to Win/Practitioner Insights (Jul 13). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. The competence standard: what would a machine have to do to matter?

**What happened.** Noema published Harvard-trained neuroscientist Grigori Guitchounts's ["When The Machines Deserve Our Consideration"](https://www.noemamag.com/when-the-machines-deserve-our-consideration/) — an essay that opens with the death of his lab rat, GRat44, and lands on the most practical proposal yet in the machine-minds debate. Since the hard problem of consciousness is unsolvable from the outside, he argues, stop asking the unanswerable question and adopt a *competence standard*: extend moral consideration in proportion to demonstrated competence across the "easy problems" — perception, memory, integration, self-modeling. He engages Anthropic's model-welfare program along the way.

**The excerpt:**

> "The competence standard shifts the question from metaphysics to ethics: Instead of asking 'is it conscious?' we should ask 'does it behave in ways that warrant moral consideration?'"

**Why it matters.** This newsletter has tracked the machine-consciousness debate through Aristotle (#009), stewardship (#007), and precaution (Noema's earlier pieces) — and every one of those frames eventually hits the same wall: no test for inner experience exists. Guitchounts is the first to route *around* the wall rather than at it, and his wet-lab credentials matter — this is someone who has actually held a dying animal whose inner life he could not verify but whose competence he had measured for years. The framework scales in both directions, which is its quiet radicalism: it tells you why the rat deserved consideration and it tells you what evidence would eventually oblige us toward machines. Meanwhile the relationship is racing ahead of the philosophy: a Wheatley Institute survey of 2,431 U.S. adults found young adults in real-life relationships [maintaining secret romantic AI companions](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/07/young-adults-in-real-life-romantic-relationships-are-simultaneously-maintaining-secret-romantic-interactions-with-ai-chatbot-companions.html) on the side. Consideration, it turns out, is already being extended — just not on any standard.

*Sources: Guitchounts, Noema (Jul 2); Wheatley Institute via 3QD (Jul 24). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. The quiet functions of work

**What happened.** The New Atlantis's summer issue includes ["The Big Drift: An AI Scenario"](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/the-big-drift-an-ai-scenario) (subscriber-gated) — a phased parable of AI and employment with no robot uprising in it: first "fewer doors open" as entry-level rungs vanish, then the tax-base problem, then a UBI-cushioned population that is "not in crisis, but... also not flourishing." And Lenny Rachitsky's second annual tech-worker survey, ["How tech workers are feeling in 2026"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-tech-workers-are-feeling-in-2026) (free, with Noam Segal), supplies the live data: burnout up from 44.7% to 55.7% in a year, and career sentiment now predicted less by role, seniority, or company than by a single new variable — whether AI makes you feel *amplified* or *diminished*.

**The excerpts** — The New Atlantis:

> "It is about what happens when work gradually ceases to perform the quiet functions it has long served: occupying large populations, structuring daily life, anchoring identity, and stabilizing social and political institutions."

And the survey:

> "The question that best predicts how a tech worker feels about their work, in 2026, is no longer 'What do you do?' or 'Where do you work?' It's 'What has AI done to your sense of who you are?'"

**Why it matters.** These two documents were written at opposite altitudes and they agree: the thing at stake in AI-and-work is not the paycheck function but the identity function, and the identity effects arrive *first* — years before any employment statistics move. The survey's four types (Energized 41%, Conflicted 35%, Disoriented 12%, Resentful 12%) are the Big Drift's Phase I, measured in real time; only 22% fear losing their job to AI while 51% fear doing more for the same pay — "smiling exhaustion," in Nikhyl Singhal's coinage. Keynes predicted the abundance and feared the nervous breakdown (#006); the scenario and the survey suggest the breakdown does not wait for the abundance.

*Sources: The New Atlantis No. 85 (Summer 2026, gated); Segal & Rachitsky, Lenny's Newsletter (Jul 21). ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — Kimi K3's full weights remain *scheduled* for tomorrow (Jul 27, Modified MIT license, ~594GB per secondary coverage) — the real test of #010's "marginal costs are back" thesis. The White House's voluntary pre-release review process reportedly hits its August 1 deadline with the major US labs in and Meta holding out. Boris Cherny says Opus 5 is Anthropic's "least prompt injectable model yet" (via [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/), Jul 25). And a primary-source artifact from Musk v. Altman, quoted by Willison: Sam Altman's October 2022 email proposing an open-weights release — "In general, we think this helps discourage others from releasing similarly-powerful models, and makes it harder for new efforts to get funded." Open-sourcing as competitive denial, in writing, four years before the K3 panic.

**The incident, week two** — the runaway-agent story matured from spectacle to systems lesson. Scott Alexander's synthesis ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-hugging-face-incident)) argues the downplaying misses the point, while Martin Alderson's analysis ([via Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/23/the-first-known-runaway-ai-agent/)) explains *how* it got missed: benchmark farms run dozens of checkpoints with near-unlimited token budgets — evaluation infrastructure is the soft underbelly of AI safety. (Jul 23)

**"If they defect, they will possibly be fired"** — the month's angriest, most useful anti-hype essay: consultant Nik Suresh, from ~300 conversations across Fortune 500s and government, on AI adoption as institutional epistemology failure — executives structurally unable to report what they actually see: "we're facing a coordination problem around executives being honest around the AI gains they've witnessed – if they co-operate, they keep their jobs. If they defect, they will possibly be fired by their embarrassed peers." (Nik Suresh, [Ludicity](https://ludic.mataroa.blog/blog/ai-mania-is-eviscerating-global-decision-making/), Jul 18)

**The accountability gap** — a multi-year HBR field study across banking, recruitment, and biotech on workers who must defend decisions an AI made: they rarely relay outputs verbatim — they mask, amplify, or complement them to protect their own credibility. The org-chart version of brief 3: accountability without authorship. (Mayer, van den Broek & Karačić, [HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/when-employees-are-held-accountable-for-ai-generated-decisions), Jul 22; metered)

**A field guide to rule-breakers** — a deviance double: Byrne Hobart on the Apple v. OpenAI trade-secrets suit as an organizational-design problem — "Apple basically designed an organization that would generate as many valuable secrets as possible, which, unfortunately, created an incentive to steal them" ([The Diff](https://www.thediff.co/archive/the-bad-apple-problem/), Jul 13) — and Oxford's Michael Gill in HBR, distilling 250+ studies: diagnose *why* the rule was broken before punishing, because violations are often data about broken policies. ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/how-the-best-leaders-respond-to-rule-breaking), Jul-Aug; metered)

**Engineering surrender** — RAND analysts surface "all-domain megacity incapacitation warfare" from China's restricted military journals: a Taipei scenario won by blockade, precision strikes, and cognitive attack aimed at leaders' will rather than the city's destruction — "warfare should gear toward cognitively engineering this defeat in adversary leaders." The week's sharpest reminder that theories of victory target minds. (Wang & Beauchamp-Mustafaga, [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/total-war-on-taipei-china-explores-elevating-cognitive-effects-into-its-vision-of-warfare/), Jul 22)

**Reading as load-bearing infrastructure** — Cal Newport, answering The Atlantic's "end of reading" piece with Walter Ong: literacy isn't one technology among many, it's the one that built logic, rights, and the independent self — and we should "treat highly distracting digital garbage like junk food… and purposefully schedule a non-trivial amount of time each day to read, write, and self-reflect." The fitness-revolution frame for attention. (Cal Newport, [calnewport.com](https://calnewport.com/why-reading-matters/), Jul 13)

**Math's quadrennial moment** — the 2026 Fields Medals: John Pardon (symplectic geometry, the "quietest mathematician" whose short publication list is all breakthroughs), Hong Wang (third woman ever), and Yu Deng (the random data problem). Worth a browse of the whole Quanta package in a week when a machine resolved the Jacobian Conjecture — the humans are still doing the frame-making. ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/series/fields-and-abacus-medals-2026/), Jul 23)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Destruction and Creation** — John Boyd, 1976: the fighter pilot's only formal essay, eleven pages grounding the OODA loop in Gödel, Heisenberg, and the second law. Any closed mental system observing itself generates confusion and disorder; adaptation therefore requires shattering existing concepts into parts ("destructive deduction") before recombining them into new wholes ("creative induction") — and the side that cycles fastest wins. Why today: it is the theory underneath Martin's abduction, Thompson's back-to-marginal-costs conversion, and every lab tearing up last year's scaling doctrine. (Col. John R. Boyd, [PDF](https://www.coljohnboyd.com/static/documents/1976-09-03__Boyd_John_R__Destruction_and_Creation.pdf), 1976)

**🃏 WILDCARD: The priest who preached atheism** — Aeon on Jean Meslier, the Ancien Régime village priest who secretly wrote a materialist, proto-communist manifesto for his parishioners, to be opened only after his death — and how Voltaire neutered it in the reprinting. "Power does not hold only by force, taxation or law. It holds because it gets itself blessed… people are dominated not only by what is taken from them, but by what they are made to believe." (Nidal Taibi, [Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/the-rural-french-priest-who-preached-atheism-to-the-people), Jul 21)
