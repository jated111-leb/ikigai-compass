# The Daily Compass #054 — Choose a center

*Saturday, September 19, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

For most of the modern history of business, "strategy" meant finding a position and defending it — a category to own, a spot in the value chain to hold, a moat to dig and man. The whole discipline was spatial: you looked at the terrain, you picked the hill, you fortified. But the terrain has gone liquid. Industries are unbundling faster than anyone can map them; the moats that took a decade to build are being reverse-engineered in a quarter; and the single most reliable business model of the last twenty years — high-margin, per-seat software — is being punished in public markets as artificial agents threaten to make the "seat" obsolete. When the positions themselves dissolve, the old question ("where should we stand?") stops having an answer, and a stranger one takes its place: not *where do we stand* but *what do we choose to be about*. This is a business issue — unusually, for us — but the deeper move in it is the one that shows up everywhere this newsletter looks: what to do when the map you were handed no longer describes the ground. And it ends, fittingly, with the discovery that even physicists can no longer agree on the map of the universe — and have decided that's the exciting part.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Not a position — a center of gravity

**What happened.** The strategist Rita McGrath, who a decade ago declared the era of durable "competitive advantage" over, has a constructive answer for what replaces it. In *Harvard Business Review*, ["The Power of Strategic Centering"](https://hbr.org/2026/07/the-power-of-strategic-centering) argues that in a "dematerializing economy" — where value has fled physical assets for data, software, and capability, and where old anchors like a product category no longer hold still — the winning move is not to defend a position but to *choose a center of gravity*: a single, coherent organizing principle around which everything else is arranged. The center can be a mission, a specific customer, a technology, a regional ecosystem, or the relentless erasure of some particular friction — but the point is to pick one and commit, because the commitment is what buys coherence, speed, and the freedom from paralysis. Her sharpest example is Novartis, which centered itself on "inventive medicines" and then, crucially, *shed* what didn't fit — eye care, consumer products, generics — because a center is defined as much by what it excludes as by what it pursues.

**Why it matters.** This is the deepest reframe in the issue, and it inverts the emotional logic of strategy. The old positional model was fundamentally defensive — build the wall, hold the ground, keep others out. Centering is the opposite: it's an act of *self-definition* under uncertainty, and its hardest part is not choosing what to do but choosing what to *decline*. In a world of dissolving boundaries, the constraint is the asset: a company that will chase anything has no gravity, no coherence, and burns itself out in politics and pivots; a company with a real center can move fast precisely because most decisions are already made by the center itself. There's a personal reading here too, which is why it belongs in a newsletter that isn't only about business. When the external structures that used to tell you who you were — the career ladder, the profession, the stable category — go liquid, the answer is not to cling harder to a position that's washing away. It's to choose, deliberately, what you are about, and to accept that the choosing means letting the rest go.

*Source: McGrath, Harvard Business Review (Jul/Aug 2026). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The moat everyone is copying (and mostly won't get)

**What happened.** If a center of gravity is the abstract answer, here is the concrete fight over it playing out in venture right now. The hottest idea in startups this year is what a16z's Joe Schmidt calls ["Trading Margin for Moat"](https://a16z.com/services-led-growth/): deliberately burning gross margin by embedding senior "forward-deployed engineers" inside customers to set the software up, integrate it, and make it work — because enterprises buying AI, in his memorable line, are "like your grandma getting an iPhone: they want to use it, but they need you to set it up," and the resulting deep integration is expensive for a rival to rip out. It is a direct heresy against twenty years of Silicon Valley gospel that only high-margin, self-serve software is a good business. But his own colleague Marc Andrusko fired the warning shot in ["The Palantirization of Everything"](https://a16z.com/the-palantirization-of-everything/): most companies copying this playbook are cargo-culting the *tactic* without the thing that makes it work. Palantir's embedded engineers succeed because there is a real, reusable platform underneath the bespoke work; copy only the on-site engineers and you don't become Palantir, you become "Accenture for X with a nicer front-end" — a consulting firm dressed as software, drowning in thousands of unmaintainable custom deployments.

**Why it matters.** This is McGrath's abstraction turned into a knife fight, and it dramatizes exactly how moats form and fail in 2026. The forward-deployed-engineer craze is an industry watching its old moat (proprietary software that sells itself) evaporate and scrambling for a new one (integration so deep it can't be removed) — and the Andrusko caution is the timeless truth underneath: *a tactic that everyone can copy is not a moat.* When dozens of $10M startups march into the same market with the identical "Palantir for X" pitch, the embedded-engineer model stops being differentiation and becomes table stakes, and the margin they all burned buys nothing. The lesson rhymes with brief 1: depth is only defensible if it flows from a genuine center — a real platform, a real reusable core — and not from imitating the surface of someone who has one. The market is about to run a live, expensive experiment on which of these startups had a center and which just had engineers on planes.

*Source: Schmidt and Andrusko, a16z (2026). ~11 min.*

### ◆ 3. The market has already voted

**What happened.** And here is the terrain map, drawn in stock prices. Tomasz Tunguz, surveying 87 public software and platform companies, finds a startling pattern in ["The CIO's Choices Are Clear in 2026"](https://tomtunguz.com/cio-choices-clear-2026/): *category, not growth, now separates the winners from the losers.* Infrastructure and developer tools are up roughly 68% on the year at premium multiples (around 10x revenue); security is up; and the great sprawling middle — "business applications," the 48-company bulk of public SaaS — is down about 36%, trading at a third the multiple, in what he flatly calls the carnage. The kicker is that growth *doesn't* explain it: the winners and losers are all growing at similar rates, around 20%. What the market is pricing is not how fast you grow but *what layer of the stack you occupy* — and it is savagely punishing the horizontal, per-seat "business application" layer, because that is precisely where AI agents threaten to dissolve the seat-based pricing model that the whole category was built on.

**Why it matters.** Read the three briefs together and they resolve into one picture. McGrath says: choose a center. The a16z fight says: make sure your center is a real platform, not a copied tactic. And Tunguz shows the market has already started grading the exam — it is paying up for the layers CIOs believe are *necessary infrastructure* for the AI era, and marking down, brutally, the layer whose entire business model AI is poised to unwind. For anyone building or advising a company (or a career) in software, the strategic instruction is unusually blunt: the question is no longer "how fast can we grow?" but "are we the pick-and-shovel layer everyone will need, or are we the seat-priced app that agents make redundant?" The positions of the old world — a nice horizontal SaaS tool with good retention — are exactly the ones dissolving fastest. The market is not rewarding effort or even growth. It is rewarding *structural position in a changing stack*, which is only another way of saying: it is rewarding companies that chose the right center, and punishing the ones that are standing, well-run and growing, on ground that is disappearing beneath them.

*Source: Tunguz, tomtunguz.com (2026). ~9 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: What Is Strategy?** — Michael E. Porter, *Harvard Business Review*, 1996. Thirty years on, still the sharpest sentence in the field: *operational effectiveness is not strategy.* Porter's argument, written against the total-quality/best-practices mania of the 1990s, is that running the same activities better than rivals is a race everyone can join and no one can win — imitation drives all competitors toward the same frontier, and the gains get competed away. Real strategy, he insists, is doing *different* activities, or the same activities differently, in a way that is mutually reinforcing and — his deepest point — that requires deliberate *trade-offs*: choosing what *not* to do, so that your position can't be cheaply copied by a rival unwilling to give up their own. Why now: every brief above is Porter's essay wearing 2026 clothes. McGrath's "center" is Porter's insistence on a coherent position with real exclusions; the forward-deployed-engineer bubble is a whole industry mistaking operational effectiveness (great deployment) for strategy (a defensible difference); and Tunguz's carnage is the market finally punishing the undifferentiated. The AI-efficiency mania is the total-quality mania again, and Porter's thirty-year-old warning is the antidote: being excellent at what everyone is racing to be excellent at is not a strategy. It's a treadmill. ([HBR](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy), 1996)

**🃏 WILDCARD: nobody agrees on the universe** — a delicious dispatch from the frontier of humility: the largest survey of physicists ever conducted — [more than 1,600 of them](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260912220041.htm), run out of the Perimeter Institute and led by Niayesh Afshordi — found that almost none of the field's "standard answers" command a majority. The reigning model of cosmology, ΛCDM, actually *lost* a head-to-head to time-varying dark energy (25.9% to 24%), likely on the strength of recent DESI data; string theory tops the quantum-gravity poll at a mere 19%; the leading interpretation of quantum mechanics claims only about a third of the room. The one near-consensus is that the Big Bang was not the beginning of time. Afshordi's gloss is the whole issue in a sentence: "The interesting point is not that physicists are confused. It is that the frontier is genuinely alive." The map of the *universe* is up for grabs, and the people who know it best find that thrilling rather than terrifying. A useful posture to borrow. (Sep 12)

**The moat is a transformer** — a bracing bit of physical realism from [Works in Progress](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/ai-is-bottlenecked-by-the-grid): the true bottleneck on the AI build-out isn't chips or capital, it's the electrical grid. A data-center campus can be built in one to two years; the substations, high-voltage transformers, and transmission lines needed to *power* it take five to ten. While everyone argues about models and margins, the binding constraint is a supply chain for grid hardware that no amount of software cleverness can accelerate — and, quietly, one of the most durable "moats" of the whole AI era may turn out to belong to whoever controls the boring, physical, slow-to-build machinery that turns electricity into compute. The center of gravity of the AI economy might be a copper coil. (Sep)

**Buying back the commons** — a widely-shared essay by Johann Kurtz, ["Young Adults Are Poor Despite Every Metric Which Suggests Otherwise,"](https://becomingnoble.substack.com/p/part-ii-young-adults-are-poor-despite) offers a haunting reframe of why the young feel broke while the statistics say they're fine. Every prior generation, he argues, inherited a stock of *free* social capital — safe streets, trustworthy neighbors, functional local schools, a default supply of marriageable peers — and today's young adults must buy all of it back at retail: the catchment-area house premium, the gated community, the private tutor, the dating-app economy. The liquidation of a shared commons doesn't show up in GDP, but it shows up in the felt cost of a decent life. A reminder, under an issue about where value hides, that some of the most important value is the kind that was once ambient and is now, expensively, for sale. (2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

There is a common nerve running under the business briefs and the physics wildcard, and it's worth naming, because it's the real subject. For a long time, in strategy and in science alike, the goal was to *find* the right answer — the defensible position, the true model of the cosmos — as if it were out there waiting to be discovered and then held. What this issue quietly documents is the end of that posture in both domains at once. The strategist can no longer *find* a durable position, because the positions dissolve; the physicist can no longer *find* the settled model, because the data keep unsettling it. And in both cases the mature response turns out to be the same, and it is not despair. It is to stop looking for a fixed point to stand on and start making a *choice* about where to put your weight — knowing the ground may move, committing anyway, and staying honest that your center is a decision and not a discovery. Porter saw it thirty years ago: strategy is defined by what you refuse. McGrath sees it now: coherence is a choice, and the choosing is the hard part. And Afshordi's physicists, staring at a universe that won't confirm any of their maps, have landed on the wisest attitude of all — that the absence of a settled answer is not a crisis but a sign that the frontier is alive. The old comfort was the fixed position: the moat, the model, the merited rung. The new competence — in a company, a career, a cosmology — is the willingness to choose a center in full view of the fact that nothing is fixed, and to find in that not vertigo but a strange kind of freedom. You don't get to stand on solid ground anymore. You get to decide which way to lean.
