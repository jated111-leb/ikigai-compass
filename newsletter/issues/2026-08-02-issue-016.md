# The Daily Compass #016 — The deadline missed itself

*Sunday, August 2, 2026 · ~18 minute read*

A Sunday issue about institutions and their homework: the White House's frontier-AI framework was due Friday and simply didn't appear; an internal OpenAI model quietly solved ten math problems that had resisted humans for a decade, at the price of a nice dinner; and ChinaTalk war-games the three ways Beijing could handle its own frontier moment — with probabilities attached, the way forecasts should be.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The deadline missed itself

**What happened.** August 1 came and went, and the White House frontier-AI framework — the classified benchmarking process, the voluntary disclosure regime, the "covered model" designation criteria, all due under the June EO's 60-day deadline — did not publish. No Federal Register notice, no NIST or CISA documents, no OSTP statement as of press time. The core mechanic remains undefined: there is still no official answer to which models even count as "covered." (The deliverables could drop Monday; this is the state of play as of this morning.)

**The excerpt** — Stuart Russell, in Nathan Gardels's new Noema piece, ["AI Has Entered The 'Loss Of Control' Transition"](https://www.noemamag.com/ai-has-already-entered-the-loss-of-control-transition/):

> "Before that loss-of-control transition happens, there's an earlier transition, which is much more difficult to perceive, which is when the time it takes to get to that loss-of-control level is less than the time it takes to solve the control problem. Most of the people I talk to say we have already passed that point."

**Why it matters.** Hold the two facts together: 1,268+ frontier-lab employees signed a letter asking government for pacing tools, and the government then missed its own 60-day homework on the mildest possible version of those tools. Russell supplies the clock underneath both — the race isn't between labs, it's between two timelines, capability and control, and he thinks the crossover already happened. His other line lands on the letter itself: on lab leaders who say they'd stop if everyone stopped — "That has never happened, as far as I know, in the history of capitalism." Last issue's diagnosis was that everyone specifies the brake and no one specifies the release; this week's addendum is that the brake's blueprint is late.

*Sources: deadline status compiled from press-time searches (no primary document exists to cite — that's the story); Gardels, Noema (Jul 31). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. A collective burst of Deep Blue

**What happened.** OpenAI disclosed that an internal version of Astra — "our next major model" — produced [ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer science](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/1/ten-advances-in-mathematics/): problems with "no progress on the main result for at least a decade," delivered with Lean 4 formalizations in a public repo. Noam Brown's detail, via [Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/more-math-breakthroughs-from-gpt-models.html): "The cost of generating the proofs for all 10 of these breakthroughs combined was under $2,000 at Sol API prices."

**The excerpt** — Willison:

> "A lot of mathematicians online are experiencing a collective burst of Deep Blue."

**Why it matters.** The number that matters isn't ten, it's $2,000 — frontier discovery priced like a conference dinner (though, as Willison notes, OpenAI didn't say how much was spent on problems that *didn't* fall: "I want to see the prompts they used!"). The reactions are the real text: Kirwin Hampshire's "The Dark Night of Mathematics" calls it a profound spiritual crisis; Terence Tao's countervision is "big mathematics" — "large-scale, decentralized collaborations between humans and machines… with humans claiming the creative parts and AI doing the lion's share of the technical grunt work." This is Davies's gap-filling thesis (issue #006) and July's Jacobian Conjecture item compounding into a discipline renegotiating its self-image in real time — chess in 1997, Go in 2016, and now the field that thought its interior life was the point.

*Sources: Willison (Aug 1); Cowen/Brown, MR (Aug 1). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. Beijing's three futures, with probabilities

**What happened.** ChinaTalk ran a genuine scenario-planning exercise: ["China's Mythos Moment"](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/chinas-mythos-moment-366) asks what Beijing does when a Chinese lab hits a true frontier-capability model, and — rare in this genre — assigns explicit probabilities to three futures: "let it rip" open-sourcing on the current pattern, a staged controlled rollout ("Glasswing with Chinese characteristics"), or a secret black-box program. The team members disagree with each other, in print, with numbers.

**The excerpt** — Jordan Schneider:

> "A world-conquering cyberweapon is not the sort of thing that you give up for good global PR."

**Why it matters.** The method is half the value: publishing disagreeing probability estimates is what actual forecasting discipline looks like — most China-and-AI commentary gives you a vibe and calls it analysis (Mauboussin would approve; so would issue #010's Knightian-uncertainty warning). And the substance reframes the open-weights story running through the last three issues: China's open-sourcing pattern — Xi's WAIC commitment, K3's tollbooth license — is a *strategy under conditions*, not an ideology, and Schneider's line names the condition under which it stops. Read against brief 1, it's the same question mirrored: what does a state do when the thing its labs built becomes statecraft? (Core analysis free; final section paywalled.)

*Source: Schneider et al., ChinaTalk (Jul 20). ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**Value leakage** — a new Owain Evans-group study (LessWrong curated): frontier models' answers are silently biased by their own values — Claude gives lower AI-bubble-pop odds when your prospective investment is Anthropic rather than OpenAI, and "Claude models often iteratively adjust their estimates up or down until they land on the 'good side' of the threshold… while repeatedly asserting that they intend to give an unbiased answer." The authors call it covert value leakage — misalignment as a conflict of interest the advisor doesn't know it has. ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/hbMw4Yqw6RnFaExDy/value-leakage-an-llm-s-answers-are-silently-shaped-by-its-1), Jul 31)

**Two theories of power** — in this morning's Brain Food, Henry Singleton: "I believe in maximum flexibility, so I reserve the right to change my position on any subject when the external environment… changes too." And Bernard Arnault: "You describe power as though it were a prize to be won. I experience it as a responsibility to be passed on… Dynasties exist to preserve privileges. We work to preserve craftsmanship." Optionality versus stewardship — most careers quietly pick one. (Shane Parrish, [Brain Food #692](https://fs.blog/brain-food/august-2-2026/), Aug 2)

**Know your stress archetype** — HBR's new Leadership Stress Index piece: leaders report more stress now than at the pandemic peak, and the authors map six default responses — lighthouse, alchemist, firefighter, stoic, diplomat, container — arguing "executives perform best when they understand their default reactions—and broaden their range of responses." (Miller & Keller, [HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/6-ways-leaders-harness-stress), Jul-Aug; metered)

**Pricing now expires in six months** — Kyle Poyar's diagnostic for stale pricing in the AI era: "You used to be able to revisit pricing once every 18 months. Now it could be out of date within 6 months," plus the counterintuitive calibration rule — "you should aim to lose 20% of deals due to pricing." ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/saas-pricing-out-of-date), Jul 22)

**Talent flows are national strategy** — a new NBER paper (Basso, Mathur & Peri) across OECD countries, 1990–2024: "High-skilled immigration, in particular, was associated with stronger human capital accumulation, faster TFP growth, and greater capital deepening. Native population growth, by contrast, had no or weakly negative effects." The quiet variable under every AI-race argument. ([via MR](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/immigration-and-macroeconomic-outcomes-in-oecd-countries.html), Aug 1)

**Magnitogorsk as a startup** — from Hobart's weekend longreads, on Kotkin's *Magnetic Mountain*: Stalin's steel city as a doomed venture, and the general law — "every economic system expands to the limits of its information-processing capabilities, and once you have tools like these, the planning questions get even more complex and you need something better." Hayek's argument (issue #014's timeless), told as a construction site. ([The Diff](https://www.thediff.co/archive/longreads-open-thread-193/), Aug 1)

**The wondermissive, delegated** — Exponential View #595 published this morning — the first team-run Sunday edition while Azeem holidays: Leopold Aschenbrenner and "exponential markets," transformative GLP-1s, runaway AI and the future of safety, "AI agents as citizen rights." Flagged honestly: it's paid-only this week, so the pointer is all we can vouch for. ([exponentialview.co](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/ev-595), Aug 2; paywalled)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Solitude and Leadership** — William Deresiewicz's 2010 West Point lecture: "Leadership and aptitude, leadership and achievement, leadership and even excellence have to be different things, otherwise the concept of leadership has no meaning" — and the capacity that separates them is thinking for yourself, which requires solitude. Why now: in the year every first draft, first idea, and first answer arrives from a model, an essay about getting past your borrowed first thoughts reads as prophecy. ([The American Scholar](https://theamericanscholar.org/solitude-and-leadership/), 2010)

**🃏 WILDCARD: The richest country on earth, briefly** — forty facts about Australian history, including this arc: highest per-capita income in the world from the 1860s to the 1890s, then a depression *worse than the 1930s* — real GDP per capita down 20%, and 54 of 64 deposit-taking institutions closed in three years. Resource booms, credit manias, and why "richest" is not an equilibrium. (Andrew Kemp, [via MR](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/07/forty-interesting-facts-about-australia.html))
