# The Daily Compass #091 — Give him a mask

*Saturday, October 31, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Early on the morning of April 30, 1943, the British submarine HMS *Seraph* surfaced off the coast of Huelva in south-west Spain, and its crew slipped a body into the sea. The dead man wore the uniform of a Royal Marines officer, Major William Martin, and carried a briefcase chained to his belt. Inside were personal letters, theatre ticket stubs, a photograph of a fiancée and, most importantly, official correspondence hinting that the Allies' next landings in the Mediterranean would come in Greece and Sardinia, not Sicily. Major Martin never existed. The body belonged to Glyndwr Michael, a homeless Welshman who had died after swallowing rat poison, and every item on him had been chosen by British intelligence to make the mask convincing. A fisherman recovered the body, the documents reached German intelligence, and German forces were reinforced in Sardinia and Greece. The historian Michael Howard later called Operation Mincemeat perhaps the most successful single deception operation of the war. It worked not only because the props were good, but because it told the enemy something it was already inclined to believe. On Halloween, the one night of the year when everyone wears a mask on purpose, this issue is about the other kind: why deception succeeds, why people are so bad at seeing through it, and what happens when AI can supply anyone with a perfect face and voice.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Tell them what they already believe

**What happened.** In 1980 a study commissioned by the CIA's Office of Research and Development distilled the history of military deception into a set of rules, *Deception Maxims: Fact and Folklore*. The first is known as Magruder's principle, after the Confederate general John Magruder, who in 1862 marched the same troops repeatedly past Union observers to make a small force look like a large one. The maxim states that it is generally easier to induce an opponent to maintain a belief they already hold than to present evidence that changes it. The US Army's own [deception doctrine](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/army/fm90-2/90-2ch1.htm) cites the cover plan for D-Day as its most prominent application. Many German commanders expected the invasion to come at the Pas-de-Calais, the narrowest point of the Channel. Operation Fortitude did not try to talk them out of it. It fed the expectation, with a fictional army group under General Patton, dummy equipment, fake radio traffic and reports from double agents such as Juan Pujol García, known as Garbo. Even after the landings in Normandy, German forces were held near Calais for weeks, waiting for the "real" invasion. Mincemeat followed the same logic: Hitler already feared an Allied move through the Balkans.

**Why it matters.** The principle explains why the most dangerous deceptions, in war and in business, are the ones that flatter what we already think. A competitor's feint works best when it confirms your view of their strategy; a fraudulent investment works best when it fits your thesis; a forecast that matches your plan gets less scrutiny than one that contradicts it. The defence follows directly. When a piece of evidence arrives that neatly confirms a strongly held belief, especially one that others know you hold, that is the moment to slow down, not speed up. Ask who benefits if you are right, what you would expect to see if you were being misled, and whether you have looked for any evidence against. Good strategists apply Magruder's principle in both directions: to understand how they might mislead others, and to understand how easily they could be misled themselves.

*Source: Deception Maxims: Fact and Folklore (ORD/CIA, 1980); US Army FM 90-2, Battlefield Deception; Howard, Strategic Deception in the Second World War. ~25 min.*

### ◆ 2. The truth default

**What happened.** If deception usually works, part of the reason is that humans are poor lie detectors. In ["Accuracy of Deception Judgments"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/) (*Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 2006), Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo pooled 206 documents covering 24,483 judges and found that people correctly identified lies and truths just under 54% of the time, barely better than tossing a coin. Professionals who assess credibility for a living did no better than lay people, and people judged more statements true than false. The communication scholar Timothy Levine has argued that this is not a defect but a design: in his truth-default theory, people assume others are honest unless something forces them to doubt, because most communication is honest and suspicion is expensive. A new study shows how strong that default is, even when people have help. In "Humans incorrectly reject confident accusatory AI judgments" (*Computers in Human Behavior*, September 2026), Riccardo Loconte, Merylin Monaro, Pietro Pietrini, Bruno Verschuere and Bennett Kleinberg gave 373 participants the verdicts of an AI lie detector that was right 90% of the time. With its help, [people reached only 76% accuracy](https://psypost.org/humans-actively-undermine-ai-lie-detectors-because-they-dont-want-to-accuse-people-of-lying): they overrode the system most often when it confidently accused someone of lying. The task was hypothetical, but the pattern was clear. People were more reluctant to call someone a liar than to trust a machine.

**Why it matters.** For leaders, these findings have an uncomfortable implication. Most organisations' defences against fraud, misconduct and misrepresentation rely heavily on someone noticing that something feels wrong, and the evidence says intuition is a weak shield. That does not mean becoming suspicious of everyone, which would destroy the trust organisations depend on. It means building verification into processes so that it does not rest on anyone's willingness to accuse a colleague: dual approval for large payments, call-backs on known numbers, independent checks of key claims. The Loconte study also carries a lesson for AI deployment. A detection tool is only as good as the willingness of people to act on it, and humans may quietly override precisely the warnings that matter most. If a system flags a risk, the procedure for responding should be agreed in advance, not left to the discomfort of the moment.

*Source: Bond & DePaulo, PSPR 10(3) (2006); Levine, Journal of Language and Social Psychology (2014); Loconte et al., Computers in Human Behavior 182 (2026), via PsyPost. ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. The face on the call

**What happened.** In January 2024 an employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup joined a video call with what appeared to be the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. Every other participant was a deepfake. Over the following days the employee made 15 transfers to five bank accounts, totalling HK$200 million, about US$25.6 million; Arup [confirmed the loss](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/05/16/tech/arup-deepfake-scam-loss-hong-kong-intl-hnk) in May 2024. In September 2026 a larger case came to light in Italy. Scammers posing on WhatsApp as Carlo Messina, chief executive of Intesa Sanpaolo, persuaded the chairman of its private bank, Fideuram, to authorise transfers for a supposedly confidential deal, reinforced by calls in a cloned voice of a well-known lawyer. About [€95 million was wired abroad](https://cybernews.com/news/ai-voice-scam-hits-italian-bank-for-95-million/), mostly to accounts in China and Hong Kong, and roughly €36 million is still missing; the chairman has resigned ([Il Post](https://www.ilpost.it/2026/09/25/fideuram-truffa-whatsapp-ai/)). Synthetic faces are not merely convincing. In a study in *PNAS* in 2022, Sophie Nightingale and Hany Farid found that people could [tell AI-generated faces from real ones](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2120481119) only at around chance, and rated the synthetic faces 7.7% more trustworthy. Detection is improving. Researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder, led by Sangtae Ha and Eric Keller, presented DeepSFU at the SIGCOMM conference in August: by reading the compressed video stream and checking about one frame in a hundred, it detected deepfakes almost perfectly in tests on 1,000 videos while using [143 times less computing power](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2026/10/12/ai-deepfakes-are-getting-more-realistic-cu-scientists-developed-better-way-spot-them) than earlier methods. Mainstream video platforms do not yet offer real-time detection.

**Why it matters.** Both frauds combined Magruder's principle and the truth default. They exploited what employees already expected, a confidential instruction from the top, and the natural reluctance to doubt a senior colleague's face or voice. The lesson is that identity can no longer be established by how someone looks or sounds. Payment and approval processes should assume that any voice, face or message can be faked, and require confirmation through a separate, pre-agreed channel for anything large or unusual, however senior the requester appears. There is a second risk, which the political scientists Kaylyn Jackson Schiff, Daniel Schiff and Natália Bueno call ["the liar's dividend"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073) (*American Political Science Review*, 2025): once fakes are common, the guilty can dismiss genuine evidence as fake. In five pre-registered experiments with more than 15,000 American adults, politicians who claimed a real scandal was misinformation gained support, mainly when the evidence was a text story rather than video, and fact-checks largely erased the gain. For organisations, the parallel is clear. The answer to synthetic masks is not only better detection. It is a reliable record of what is real.

*Source: Arup, via CNN (May 2024); Cybernews and Il Post (Sep 2026); Nightingale & Farid, PNAS 119(8) (2022); Ha, Keller et al., SIGCOMM '26, via CU Boulder Today; Schiff, Schiff & Bueno, APSR 119(1) (2025). ~35 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the panic that mostly wasn't** — 88 years ago last night, on October 30, 1938, Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre broadcast its adaptation of H.G. Wells's *The War of the Worlds* on CBS, with Martians landing in New Jersey. The next day's newspapers reported mass hysteria across America, and the story became the founding legend of media panic. In 2013 the media historians Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow went back to the evidence. On the night of the broadcast the C.E. Hooper ratings service telephoned 5,000 households, and only 2% said they were listening to the Welles programme; none described it as a news broadcast. Few anecdotes of hysteria were ever confirmed. Their explanation is that newspapers, losing advertising to radio, had every reason to portray the new medium as irresponsible. Some listeners were genuinely frightened, but [far fewer than legend says](https://www.npr.org/2013/10/30/241797346/75-years-ago-war-of-the-worlds-started-a-panic-or-did-it). The most successful fake of the night was the story about the fake. (Pooley & Socolow, Slate, 2013, via NPR)

**🃏 WILDCARD: butterflies that cast illusions** — butterflies are notoriously hard to catch, and a study published in *Nature* on September 30 suggests one reason. A team from the universities of Exeter and Essex, including Jolyon Troscianko, combined high-speed filming of real butterflies with computer models of bird vision, simulations covering 757 wing forms from 397 European species, and a touchscreen game in which human volunteers tried to catch virtual butterflies. Striped and banded wings, flapping in flight, create [misleading motion signals](https://essex.ac.uk/news/2026/09/30/butterflies-use-optical-illusions-to-dodge-predators), much like the barber-pole illusion, and patterns predicted to be most confusing made people strike behind their target. The study modelled only bird vision, and the least confusing species, often brown or red, may rely on camouflage or warning colours instead. Nature, it seems, invented the moving mask long before we did. (Hancock, Briolat, Hughes et al., Nature, Sep 2026)

**The founder's mask** — in September Ilit Raz, founder of the AI recruiting start-up Joonko, [pleaded guilty to securities fraud](https://justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/founder-and-former-ceo-artificial-intelligence-company-pleads-guilty-securities-fraud) in Manhattan, admitting she raised about $27 million on false claims about customers and revenue. When an investor asked for bank records in 2023, she supplied a forged statement and then fictitious purchase orders; the company went bankrupt in 2024. A new NBER working paper, ["Venture Fraud"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34868), by Alexander Dyck, Freda Fang, Camille Hébert and Ting Xu, finds that among newly public companies, VC-backed firms were 54% more likely to face fraud charges than comparable firms, that fraud is more likely where founders hold strong control rights, and that founders caught in fraud went on to raise money for new start-ups much like everyone else. The market, in other words, does not reliably punish the mask. (US Attorney, SDNY; NBER WP 34868, Feb 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In "The Critic as Artist", the long dialogue published in *Intentions* in 1891, Oscar Wilde has one of his speakers observe that the great poet sings not in his own person but in one he has assumed. "Man is least himself when he talks in his own person," Wilde wrote. "Give him a mask, and he will tell you the truth." It is a strange thought to end an issue about deception, but it is the right one for Halloween. Masks are not only tools of fraud. The actor, the novelist and the child in costume use them to say things they could not say as themselves, and leaders often grow by trying on a role before it feels natural. What separates the honest mask from the dishonest one is not the disguise but the consent: everyone at a Halloween party knows the faces are false. The frauds in today's issue worked because their victims did not know a mask was being worn. As AI makes every face and voice available to anyone, the most valuable thing an organisation can build is not suspicion of every mask, but clear ways of knowing when one is on.
