# The Daily Compass #107 — The day of two noons

*Monday, November 16, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

This Wednesday is the anniversary of one of the strangest days in American history. Until 1883, every town in North America kept its own time, set by the sun overhead, and the railroads ran on dozens of different operating times, about fifty by most counts. A traveller crossing the country had to reset a watch again and again, and timetables were a maze. The fix came not from government but from the railroads themselves. William F. Allen, secretary of the railroads' General Time Convention, drew up a plan for a handful of time zones, building on earlier proposals by Charles Dowd and Sandford Fleming. The Convention approved it in Chicago on October 11, 1883, and at noon on November 18 the switch was made. In cities east of each zone's meridian, clocks struck twelve, were stopped or turned back, and struck twelve again: "the day of two noons." Observatories [telegraphed the new time](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2024/11/whose-time-is-it-anyway-a-brief-history-of-standardized-time-zones-in-the-united-states/) across the network. Some places resisted for years, and Congress did not make standard time law until 1918, thirty-five years later. A private standard, set by the players who needed it most, had quietly reorganised the life of a continent. This issue is about standards: how they are won, what they do inside organisations, and the race to set the standards for AI agents.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. How standards are won

**What happened.** Standards look like dry technical documents, but they are often the outcome of fierce competition. In ["The Art of Standards Wars"](https://cmr-mig.berkeley.edu/1999/02/41-2-the-art-of-standards-wars) (*California Management Review*, 1999), Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian studied a century of these contests and found that markets with strong network effects tend to "tip" towards a single winner, which makes the fights bitter. They identified seven assets that decide them: an installed base of users, intellectual property, the ability to innovate, first-mover advantage, manufacturing strength, strength in complementary products and brand. The two decisive tactics were pre-emption, getting there first, and managing expectations, because buyers back the format they believe will win. Stanley Besen and Joseph Farrell ([*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 1994](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jecper/v8y1994i2p117-31.html)) framed the basic choice: firms can compete *for* the market, in a standards war, or *within* it, under a shared standard. The Blu-ray war shows how quickly a contest can end. On January 4, 2008, Warner Bros. said it would release films only on Blu-ray; within weeks Netflix, Best Buy and Walmart followed, and on February 19 Toshiba [abandoned HD DVD](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2008/02/20/business/its-official-toshiba-pulls-plug-on-hd-dvd). Expectations, not technology, decided it. Agreeing a standard by committee is slow in a different way. Studying the Internet Engineering Task Force, Timothy Simcoe ([*American Economic Review*, 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:102:y:2012:i:1:p:305-36)) found that standards production slowed as the internet commercialised and companies fought over who would benefit. Sometimes the cost of having no standard is measured in disaster. In the Great Baltimore Fire of February 1904, fire engines from Washington and Philadelphia arrived to help, but their hoses did not fit Baltimore's hydrants; the fire destroyed more than 1,500 buildings, and a century later [a NIST study](https://www.nist.gov/publications/major-us-cities-using-national-standard-fire-hydrants-one-century-after-great-0) found that many large American cities still did not use the national standard.

**Why it matters.** Standards are now an arena for governments as well as firms. The European Union simply ordered a common charger: USB-C has been [mandatory](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/electrical-and-electronic-engineering-industries-eei/radio-equipment-directive-red/one-common-charging-solution-all_en) for phones and other small devices since December 28, 2024, and for laptops since April 28 this year. China has made standard-setting a national strategy; its participation in international standards committees has grown steadily, though a [Carnegie analysis](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2023/02/what-washington-gets-wrong-about-china-and-technical-standards) found it is still far from dominating them. For leaders, three lessons follow. First, decide early whether you want to fight for a market or cooperate within a shared standard; the strategies are different, and mixing them is costly. Second, in a standards war, perception is reality: alliances, announcements and the commitments of key partners matter as much as technical merit. Third, if no standard exists in your industry, someone will eventually create one, a rival, a consortium or a regulator, and it is better to help write it than to have to adapt to it.

*Source: Shapiro & Varian, CMR 41(2) (1999); Besen & Farrell, JEP 8(2) (1994); Simcoe, AER 102(1) (2012); Japan Times (Feb 20, 2008); Seck & Evans, NISTIR 7158 (2004); European Commission; Carnegie Endowment (2023). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Standards inside the organisation

**What happened.** Inside companies, standard procedures have a bad reputation: they are associated with bureaucracy, box-ticking and the death of creativity. The research is more interesting. In ["Two Types of Bureaucracy: Enabling and Coercive"](https://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Paul-Adler/research/ASQ%20copy-1.pdf) (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1996), Paul Adler and Bryan Borys argued that what matters is not how many rules an organisation has, but how they are designed. "Enabling" rules help people master their work and solve problems; "coercive" rules exist to extract compliance. Adler had seen the difference at NUMMI, the joint venture between General Motors and Toyota in a former GM plant in Fremont, California. In ["Time-and-Motion Regained"](https://hbr.org/1993/01/time-and-motion-regained) (*Harvard Business Review*, 1993), he described how team members timed each other's work and wrote their own standard procedures, turning a tool of control into a tool of learning; in the same plant under GM, absenteeism had often run above 20%. Steven Spear and Kent Bowen made a similar point in ["Decoding the DNA of the Toyota Production System"](https://hbr.org/1999/09/decoding-the-dna-of-the-toyota-production-system) (*HBR*, 1999): Toyota specifies work in great detail, but treats each specification as a hypothesis to be tested and improved. A line often attributed to Taiichi Ohno, the system's architect, puts it simply: where there is no standard, there can be no improvement. Standards do have costs, though. Mary Benner and Michael Tushman ([*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2002](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/tqm-iso-9000-six-sigma-do-process-management-programs-discourage-innovation/)) tracked ISO 9000 certification and patents in the photography and paint industries over two decades, and found that more process management shifted innovation towards incremental improvements and away from genuinely new ideas. Even a simple standard can succeed or fail depending on how it is introduced. A 19-item surgical checklist tested in eight hospitals around the world was followed by a fall in deaths from 1.5% to 0.8% ([*NEJM*, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/)). But when Ontario made checklists mandatory in 101 hospitals, a study of more than 200,000 operations found [no significant change](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/healthcare/surgical-safety-checklists-may-not-improve-patient-outcomes-after-all) in deaths or complications ([*NEJM*, 2014](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa1308261)). Critics suggested the checklist was often not really used.

**Why it matters.** The question for leaders is not "more process or less?" but "whose process, and for what?" Standards work best when the people who do the work help write them, when they are treated as the current best method rather than the final word, and when they free attention for problems that really need it. They work worst when imposed from above, measured by compliance and applied to work that is still being invented. Benner and Tushman's remedy is to separate the two: apply standard processes rigorously where the work is understood, and protect exploratory teams from them. The Ontario checklist is a warning for anyone rolling out a new standard, including the new AI tools many firms are now standardising on: a mandate can change the paperwork without changing the behaviour.

*Source: Adler & Borys, ASQ 41(1) (1996); Adler, HBR 71(1) (1993); Spear & Bowen, HBR 77(5) (1999); Benner & Tushman, ASQ 47(4) (2002) and AMR 28(2) (2003); Haynes et al., NEJM 360 (2009); Urbach et al., NEJM 370 (2014). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. The plumbing of AI agents

**What happened.** For AI agents to be useful, they need to connect to tools, data and each other, and a standards race has been under way to decide how. Over the past year it has turned into consolidation. In December 2025, the Linux Foundation launched the [Agentic AI Foundation](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/12/agentic-ai-foundation) to host open projects donated by AI companies, including the Model Context Protocol, the most widely used way of connecting AI models to tools and data, and OpenAI's AGENTS.md. Its founding members included most of the largest AI and cloud companies. Google's rival Agent2Agent protocol, for communication between agents, had already gone to the Linux Foundation; by April it had [more than 150 supporting organisations](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/a2a-protocol-surpasses-150-organizations-lands-in-major-cloud-platforms-and-sees-enterprise-production-use-in-first-year), and in August it [moved into the same foundation](https://axios.com/2026/08/17/a2a-agentic-ai-foundation-open-ai-standards). The Model Context Protocol published its first specification under the foundation's governance [on July 28](https://aaif.io/blog/mcp-2026-07-28-whats-changing-and-how-to-migrate). But security has lagged behind adoption. In ["Exposed by Design"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00150) (arXiv, July 2026; not peer reviewed), Nicolás Padilla tested 414 of these connection servers reachable from the open internet and found 68 vulnerabilities, with most servers lacking standard authentication. Government standards are running late too. The EU's AI Act depends on technical standards that European standards bodies have struggled to finish, and this summer the EU [postponed](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-ai-omnibus-enters-force-amending-ai-act) the main obligations for high-risk AI systems to December 2027 and August 2028. In February, America's NIST launched an [AI Agent Standards Initiative](https://nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/announcing-ai-agent-standards-initiative-interoperable-and-secure) focused on interoperability and agent security.

**Why it matters.** The AI agent market is following the pattern of the 1883 railroads more than that of the Blu-ray war: rather than fight to the death, the major players have chosen to cooperate on shared plumbing and compete on what runs over it. That lowers the risk for companies building on these protocols, since they are less likely to back a loser. But it changes where the risks lie. A shared standard spreads its weaknesses as quickly as its benefits, and the security of agent connections is still immature. Leaders should treat the new protocols as they would any shared infrastructure: adopt them, but test every connection before trusting it, give agents only the access they need, and watch the standards bodies, because the rules being written now will shape the market for a decade. Note, too, that most adoption figures in this field come from the foundations themselves: "supporting organisation" does not mean "running it in production".

*Source: Linux Foundation and Agentic AI Foundation announcements (Dec 2025–Aug 2026); Padilla, arXiv 2608.00150 (2026); Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; NIST (Feb 17, 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: one measure throughout the realm** — standards are among the oldest demands of government. Clause 35 of Magna Carta, sealed in 1215, declares: "There is to be [one measure of wine throughout our kingdom](https://magnacarta.cmp.uea.ac.uk/read/magna_carta_1215/Clause_35), and one measure of ale, and one measure of corn, namely the quarter of London," with one width for cloth and the same rule for weights. The barons wanted fair trade, and a single measure was the way to get it. It was not new: English kings had been trying to impose uniform weights and measures since the tenth century. Nor did it work quickly; local measures survived for centuries. Declaring a standard has always been easier than enforcing one. (Magna Carta, cl. 35, 1215)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the unit who ran the standards body** — in October 1958, fraternity brothers at MIT laid a new pledge, Oliver Smoot, end to end across the Harvard Bridge to measure it. The bridge came to [364.4 smoots](https://betterworld.mit.edu/the-smoots-mit-legacy-is-measurable/), "plus or minus one ear", and the markings are still repainted today. The joke has a perfect ending: Smoot went on to chair the American National Standards Institute and then, from 2003 to 2004, served as president of the International Organization for Standardization. He is probably the only person to have been both a unit of measurement and the head of the world's standards body. (MIT; ISO)

**The toll booth on a de facto standard** — startups can become standards without asking permission. Plaid's connections to bank accounts became the default way American fintech apps reached customer data. But a standard built on someone else's infrastructure can be taxed. Last year JPMorgan Chase began charging data aggregators for access that had been free, and in September 2025 Plaid [agreed to pay](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/plaid-pay-jpmorgan-data-open-banking-fintech/760264/). The banks are backing their own industry standard, FDX, which reports more than 100 million accounts on its interfaces, while the federal open-banking rule remains tied up in court and under revision. The lesson for founders: owning the standard is not the same as owning the pipes. (Banking Dive, Sep 2025; FDX)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

**Planet Money — "The History of Light (Nobel Edition)"** (from the archive; October 2018)
David Kestenbaum and Jacob Goldstein follow the economist William Nordhaus as he works out how many hours of labour it took to buy an hour of light, from Babylonian oil lamps to the light bulb. It is a story about measurement: by choosing a better unit, labour time per unit of light, Nordhaus showed that standard price statistics had missed most of the improvement in living standards. Short, vivid and a good reminder that what you measure, and how, determines what you see. **Queue it**. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

There is an old joke among engineers, usually attributed to the computer scientist Andrew Tanenbaum: "The nice thing about standards is that you have so many to choose from." It is funny because it is true, and because every standard begins as one option among many. The railroads' time zones were one plan among several; so were the Whitworth and Sellers screw threads, which Britain, Canada and the United States finally reconciled in an accord signed on November 18, 1948, sixty-five years to the day after the day of two noons. Standards look permanent once they win. They are, in fact, agreements, made by people who decided that the cost of disagreement had become too high. The question for any leader is whether to be in the room when that decision is made.
