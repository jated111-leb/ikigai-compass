# The Daily Compass #112 — To err

*Saturday, November 21, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On the evening of December 28, 1978, United Airlines Flight 173 was approaching Portland, Oregon, with 189 people on board when the landing gear came down with a loud thump and a warning light failed to confirm it was locked. The captain, a highly experienced pilot, decided to circle while the crew worked out what had happened and prepared for a possible emergency landing. For about an hour the plane held over the suburbs. The flight engineer and first officer mentioned the dwindling fuel, but never forcefully enough to break the captain's focus on the gear. Then the engines began to fail, one after another, and the plane came down in a wooded neighbourhood about six miles from the airport. Ten people died. The landing gear, it turned out, had come down after all. The [National Transportation Safety Board](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/AAR7907.pdf) concluded that the captain had failed to monitor the fuel and that the other crew members had failed to get their concern across, and it urged the FAA to ensure crews were trained in what it called flight deck resource management, with assertiveness for junior crew and participative leadership for captains. In 1981 United launched the first comprehensive [crew resource management](https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/N8082U) programme, which has since spread across aviation and into medicine. The mistake that mattered was not mechanical but social: everyone noticed, and nobody said so in a way that was heard. This issue is about mistakes: how organisations learn from them, how leaders should treat them, and whether machines can learn to admit them.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The reporting paradox

**What happened.** The safest industries are not the ones with the fewest reported errors; they are the ones that report the most. Commercial aviation's confidential reporting system, run by NASA, has been [operating since April 1976](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/publications/callback/cb_555.html). It was born of a failure to share: in December 1974 a TWA jet crashed into a ridge near Mount Weather, Virginia, killing all 92 people on board, weeks after a United crew had narrowly avoided the same mistake and reported it only within their own airline. Under the system, pilots and controllers who report their own inadvertent errors to NASA within ten days generally escape FAA penalties, and NASA strips identities before analysing them. It now receives [more than 100,000 reports a year](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/ASRS_ProgramBriefing.pdf). Over the same period flying became dramatically safer: Arnold Barnett and Alexandre Reig Torra of MIT estimate that the global risk of death per passenger boarding fell from about 1 in 350,000 in 1968–77 to [about 1 in 13.7 million](https://news.mit.edu/2024/study-flying-keeps-getting-safer-0807) in 2018–22 (*Journal of Air Transport Management*, 2024). Hospitals show the same pattern on a smaller scale. In ["Learning from Mistakes Is Easier Said Than Done"](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/learning-mistakes-easier-said-done-group-and-organizational-influences-detection-and) (*Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*, 1996), Amy Edmondson found that nursing units with better leadership and relationships recorded *more* medication errors, most plausibly because staff were more willing to report them. Organisations also learn more from failure than from success. Studying more than 4,600 orbital rocket launches between 1957 and 2004, Peter Madsen and Vinit Desai found that organisations [learned more from failed launches](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2010.51467631) than from successful ones, and that the lessons lasted longer (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2010). When learning fails, the cost can be enormous. General Motors' investigation into a faulty ignition switch, led by Anton Valukas in 2014, found a decade of inaction and described the "GM nod", where everyone agrees to act and no one does; a compensation fund later counted [124 deaths](https://money.cnn.com/2015/12/10/news/companies/gm-recall-ignition-switch-death-toll/). Even aviation can slip. In its report this year on the January 2025 midair collision near Washington's Reagan National Airport, the NTSB found that the FAA had data on [more than 15,000 close-proximity events](https://www.ntsb.gov/news/press-releases/Pages/NR20260127.aspx) near the airport between 2011 and 2024, and had not analysed them.

**Why it matters.** The lesson for any organisation is that a low count of reported errors is not good news until you know why it is low. The systems that improve fastest make reporting easy, safe and useful: they protect people who report honest mistakes, they separate learning from punishment, and they act on what they hear. The GM and Washington cases show the two ways this breaks down: people stop raising problems because nothing happens, or the problems are raised and recorded but nobody analyses them. Leaders should measure not only how many errors are reported, but how quickly reports lead to changes, and should treat a sudden fall in reports as a warning, not a success.

*Source: NASA ASRS (CALLBACK, Apr 2026; Program Briefing); Barnett & Reig Torra, JATM (2024); Edmondson, JABS 32(1) (1996); Madsen & Desai, AMJ 53(3) (2010); Valukas report (May 2014); Feinberg compensation fund (Dec 2015); NTSB AIR-26-02 (2026). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Leaders who own their mistakes

**What happened.** Most organisations train people to avoid errors. The evidence suggests they should also train people to make them. In a meta-analysis of 24 studies with more than 2,000 participants, Nina Keith and Michael Frese found that "error management training", which encourages trainees to make errors and learn from them, [beat error-avoidant training](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/effectiveness-of-error-management-training-a-meta-analysis/), with the advantage growing on tasks unlike those practised in training (*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2008). Culture matters too. In studies of 65 Dutch and 47 German organisations, Cathy van Dyck, Frese and colleagues found that firms with an "error management culture", where mistakes are discussed openly and fixed quickly, tended to perform better on both goal achievement and economic measures ([*JAP*, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.90.6.1228)), though the evidence is correlational. People resist learning from their own failures. In five studies, Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach found that people given failure feedback [learned less](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31702452/) than those given success feedback, apparently because failure threatens the ego; they learned better from watching others fail (*Psychological Science*, 2019). A later replication found the effect robust, though a 2025 commentary argued that part of it reflects how one task was scored. Leaders can make it easier. Interviewing 55 leaders, from chief executives to front-line supervisors, Bradley Owens and David Hekman found that [admitting mistakes](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/Owens%20and%20Hekman%20Leader%20Humility%202012.pdf) and being teachable were at the heart of humble leadership, which signals to followers that growth is expected (*AMJ*, 2012). And in experiments by Li Jiang, Leslie John and colleagues, leaders who voluntarily disclosed a weakness were [seen as more authentic](https://www.pubmed.gov/36201838/), and no less competent (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 2022). Failure alone does not teach, though. Paul Gompers and colleagues found that venture-backed founders whose previous start-up had failed were [scarcely more likely to succeed](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2010/04/16/performance-persistence-in-entrepreneurship-and-venture-capital) next time than first-time founders, while previously successful founders did much better (*Journal of Financial Economics*, 2010).

**Why it matters.** "Fail fast" is half a lesson. Failure is valuable only when people examine it, and the research shows that people are naturally bad at examining their own. Leaders can close the gap in three ways. Go first: admitting your own mistakes, voluntarily and specifically, makes it safe for others to admit theirs, and costs less credibility than most leaders fear. Separate the post-mortem from the performance review, so that analysing a failure does not feel like a trial. And make learning from failure social: review others' mistakes as well as your own, since people learn more readily from someone else's error than from their own.

*Source: Keith & Frese, JAP 93(1) (2008); van Dyck, Frese, Baer & Sonnentag, JAP 90(6) (2005); Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach, Psychological Science (2019); Owens & Hekman, AMJ 55(4) (2012); Jiang, John, Boghrati & Kouchaki, JEP: Applied 28(4) (2022); Gompers, Kovner, Lerner & Scharfstein, JFE 96(1) (2010). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. Machines that know they're wrong

**What happened.** AI systems make mistakes with great confidence, and a growing body of research asks whether they can be taught to notice. In ["Why Language Models Hallucinate"](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/) (September 2025), OpenAI researchers argued that part of the problem is how models are graded. Most benchmarks score answers simply as right or wrong, so saying "I don't know" earns nothing and guessing pays. Their own data made the point: on one test, a newer model that declined to answer about half the time was wrong 26% of the time, while an older model that almost always answered got slightly more questions right but was wrong 75% of the time. Their proposed fix is to penalise confident errors more than honest uncertainty. Researchers are building tools to detect doubt. Sebastian Farquhar and colleagues at Oxford showed that sampling several answers to the same question and measuring how much their *meanings* disagree can [flag likely fabrications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0) (*Nature*, 2024), though not errors a model has been systematically trained into. Self-correction is harder than it looks. A Google DeepMind team found in 2023 that models asked to review their own reasoning, without outside feedback, [often made it worse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798), changing more right answers to wrong than the reverse; later work trained models specifically to [correct their own attempts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12917) and reported significant gains (*ICLR*, 2025). OpenAI has also experimented with training a model to file a separate ["confession"](https://openai.com/index/how-confessions-can-keep-language-models-honest/) about whether it had followed its instructions, rewarded for honesty independently of its main answer (company research, December 2025). Meanwhile, the cost of uncaught errors is visible. In October 2025, Deloitte agreed to [refund part of its fee](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/06/deloitte_ai_report_australia/) to the Australian government after a report it had produced was found to contain fabricated references and a made-up quote from a court judgment; a corrected version disclosed the use of a generative-AI tool.

**Why it matters.** The OpenAI paper makes a point that applies well beyond AI: systems learn what they are rewarded for, and if guessing is never penalised, confident guessing is what you get. The same is true of people and organisations that punish "I don't know". For leaders deploying AI, the practical lessons are clear. Prefer tools and settings that are allowed to say they are unsure, and treat a system that always has an answer with suspicion. Never let an AI system be the only check on its own work; the research shows self-review without outside feedback can make things worse. And reward the people who catch AI errors, as the aviation system rewards pilots who report their own, because the errors that matter most are the ones that look right.

*Source: Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang, arXiv 2509.04664 (2025); Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn & Gal, Nature 630 (2024); Huang et al., ICLR (2024); Kumar et al., ICLR (2025); OpenAI (Dec 2025; company research); The Register (Oct 2025). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: only a fool persists** — the proverb "to err is human" has a precise ancestor. In 43 BC, in one of his speeches against Mark Antony, Cicero declared: ["Cuiusvis hominis est errare, nullius nisi insipientis in errore perseverare"](https://www.lexundria.com/cic_phil/12.5/y), "Anyone can make a mistake, but only a fool persists in it." Around AD 400 St Jerome compressed the first half into "errasse humanum est", and the second half, "to persist is diabolical", was added later. Confucius made the same point in the *Analects*: ["To make a mistake and not correct it: this is what one calls really making a mistake."](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.html) Two traditions, centuries apart, agreed that the error is forgivable; the refusal to correct it is not. (Cicero, *Philippics* 12.5; Confucius, *Analects* 15.30)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the most expensive typo** — in 1631, the royal printers Robert Barker and Martin Lucas produced an edition of the King James Bible with a single missing word. The Seventh Commandment read: ["Thou shalt commit adultery."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicked_Bible) The "Wicked Bible" was discovered, King Charles I ordered the copies destroyed, and the printers were hauled before the Star Chamber, fined £300 and stripped of their licence. A handful of copies survive, now prized by collectors. It remains the classic case for proofreading, and for the rule that the smallest errors can carry the largest consequences. (Wicked Bible, 1631)

**Machines that correct themselves** — quantum computers are so error-prone that they are useless without error correction, and the field has crossed important thresholds. In December 2024 Google showed that its Willow chip's error rate [fell as its error-correcting code grew larger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13687), the long-sought "below threshold" result. In February this year Quantinuum reported computing with dozens of error-corrected logical qubits on its Helios machine, with [error rates 10 to 100 times lower](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22211) than the raw physical qubits (preprint). Useful quantum computing depends less on avoiding errors than on catching and fixing them faster than they accumulate. (Google; Quantinuum)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1711, a 22-year-old Alexander Pope published *An Essay on Criticism*, a long poem in rhyming couplets advising critics on how to judge the work of others. Most of its lines are forgotten. One became a proverb. Warning critics against pride and malice, Pope wrote: ["Good-nature and good-sense must ever join; / To err is human, to forgive, divine."](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7409) It is usually quoted as a consolation for the person who made the mistake. In context it is advice for the judge. That is the harder half, and the one that organisations most need: the people who make errors will keep making them, but whether those errors are reported, examined and learned from depends on how the rest of us respond. The crew of Flight 173 did not lack information. They lacked a culture in which it was safe, and expected, to insist.
