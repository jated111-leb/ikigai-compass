# The Daily Compass #077 — The empty square

*Saturday, October 17, 2026 · ~14 minute read*

Before dawn on February 1, 1997, a fire broke out at a factory in Kariya, Japan, owned by Aisin Seiki, one of Toyota's closest suppliers. The plant made a small brake part called a proportioning valve, and it made almost all of them: roughly 99% of Toyota's supply came from that one building. Toyota, the inventor of just-in-time manufacturing, kept only a few hours' worth of the part on hand. Within days its assembly plants across Japan had stopped, and the fire is reported to have cost the company some $195 million. What happened next became a famous case study. Suppliers and partners across Toyota's network, many of which had never made the part, improvised production with whatever machines they had, and the assembly lines were running again within about a week. The story is usually told as a triumph of cooperation. It is also a warning. A system tuned for perfect efficiency had no spare capacity, so when one piece failed, everything stopped. This issue is about slack: the spare time, money and capacity that look like waste on a spreadsheet and turn out to be what keeps systems alive.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The high price of efficiency

**What happened.** In a 2019 *Harvard Business Review* article, ["The High Price of Efficiency"](https://hbr.org/2019/01/the-high-price-of-efficiency), the strategy scholar Roger Martin, former dean of the Rotman School of Management, took aim at one of the most cherished goals in management. Eliminating waste, he argued, has become the holy grail of business and government, and pursued without limit it creates fragile systems: companies, supply chains and whole economies optimised for a narrow set of conditions and unable to cope when those conditions change. Martin's remedy was balance. Organisations should value resilience as much as efficiency, even though its rewards arrive later and are harder to measure. He suggested limiting scale, introducing some productive friction, favouring patient capital and changing how business is taught. He expanded the argument in a 2020 book, *When More Is Not Better*, with a memorable diagnosis: efficiency itself is not the problem; the trouble lies in the proxies we use to measure it.

**Why it matters.** Martin's point echoes an older book by the software consultant Tom DeMarco, *Slack* (2001), which argued that organisations need some unused capacity in order to change at all. Think of the classic sliding-tile puzzle: it can be solved only because one square is empty. Fill every square and nothing can move. A team in which every person is fully booked cannot respond to an emergency, learn a new skill or take on an unexpected opportunity, because there is no time to do so. The practical lesson is to ask, for any system you run, where its empty square is. If the honest answer is "nowhere", the system is efficient right up to the moment it breaks. Building in a little deliberate slack, a spare supplier, a person with unallocated time, a cash buffer, is not waste but insurance, and it is usually much cheaper than the crisis it prevents.

*Source: Martin, Harvard Business Review (Jan–Feb 2019); Martin, When More Is Not Better (2020); DeMarco, Slack (2001). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. Why 85% full is full

**What happened.** The clearest evidence on slack comes from hospitals. In a 1999 paper in the *BMJ*, ["Dynamics of bed use in accommodating emergency admissions"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC28163/), Adrian Bagust, Michael Place and John Posnett built a computer simulation of a typical acute hospital in England, with emergency patients arriving at random and staying for random lengths of time. Their findings became one of the most cited rules of thumb in health management. Risks became noticeable once average bed occupancy rose above about 85%. At 90% or more, the hospital could expect regular bed shortages and periodic crises in which emergency patients could not be admitted at all. The reason is variability. Patients do not arrive evenly, and when they bunch together, a hospital that is nearly full on average has no room to absorb the surge.

**Why it matters.** The same mathematics governs any system with uneven demand: call centres, software servers, airports, ports and the inbox of a busy executive. In queueing theory, waiting times do not rise steadily as a system gets busier; they rise slowly at first and then explode as utilisation approaches 100%. That is why a team running at 95% of capacity feels not slightly busier than one at 80% but dramatically worse, with every small delay cascading into the next. The 85% figure itself comes from a model of one hypothetical hospital and should not be treated as a law of nature; English health bodies have used both 85% and 92% as "safe" thresholds. But the principle is robust. Wherever demand is unpredictable, the right target for utilisation is well below full, and managers who push for 100% are buying short-term efficiency with long-term chaos.

*Source: Bagust, Place & Posnett, BMJ 319 (1999). ~15 min (paper).*

### ◆ 3. When the data centres let go

**What happened.** The newest test of slack is the electricity grid, now being asked to absorb the vast new demand from AI data centres. On July 22 this year, a mechanical failure knocked out a high-voltage transmission line in Northern Virginia, the world's largest data-centre market. The fault was cleared as designed, but data centres in the area, sensing the disturbance, switched themselves off the grid and onto backup power to protect their equipment. According to [PJM](https://insidelines.pjm.com/pjm-dominion-review-large-load-transfer-event/), the grid operator for much of the eastern United States, about 3,800 megawatts of load disappeared, roughly the output of several large power plants, the largest such event in its history. The grid held, but it suffered a large imbalance between supply and demand and big swings in voltage and frequency. In September PJM [proposed rules](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/regulations/3-8-gw-load-drop-prompts-potential-pjm-rules) requiring large data centres to "ride through" such disturbances rather than disconnecting, and the North American reliability regulator, NERC, is developing its first standards for large computing loads.

**Why it matters.** The event is a vivid example of what happens when individually sensible decisions remove slack from a shared system. Each data centre was protecting itself, but together they created a shock the grid had to absorb with whatever reserve it had. And reserves are getting thinner. NERC's long-term assessment has warned that data-centre growth is pushing up forecast peak demand sharply and raising the risk of shortfalls in several regions, although some analysts argue that its forecasts are too pessimistic. The lesson extends beyond electricity. AI promises enormous gains in efficiency, but the infrastructure that supports it, power, chips, cooling, a handful of key suppliers, is being built at a speed that leaves little margin for error. The companies and countries that do best may be the ones that build slack into the system before the next line trips.

*Source: PJM Inside Lines (2026); Data Center Knowledge (Sep 2026); NERC. ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: four hours of stock** — the details of the [1997 Aisin fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Aisin_fire) are worth remembering for anyone designing a supply chain. Toyota normally split orders for parts between two suppliers, but for this complex valve, which required specialised tooling, it had made an exception and relied almost entirely on one. Its factories held roughly four hours of stock. The fire shut down 18 of its assembly plants in Japan for almost a week. Toyota did not abandon just-in-time afterwards, and its relationship with Aisin survived. But the episode became a standard lesson in what management writers would later call concentration risk: the cheapest supply chain on a normal day can be the most expensive one on a bad day. (Wikipedia; company histories)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the animal kingdom's missing chapter** — the first animals in the fossil record appear around 570 million years ago, but a new study led by the University of Oxford, published in *Science Advances*, [argues that animals may have evolved up to 200 million years earlier](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2026-09-23-new-study-suggests-animals-may-have-evolved-vastly-earlier-than-fossil-evidence). The team re-examined the fossil sites that scientists have used to cap the age of the animal kingdom and concluded that they do not rule out older animals; the earliest creatures may simply have been too small and soft to leave traces. Molecular-clock estimates then suggest an origin some 800 to 700 million years ago. The authors are careful: until older fossils are found, the birth date of the animal kingdom remains uncertain. (University of Oxford / Science Advances, Sep–Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

About two and a half thousand years ago, the *Tao Te Ching* made the case for slack in three images. Thirty spokes meet at the hub of a wheel, but it is the empty centre that lets the wheel turn. Clay is shaped into a pot, but it is the hollow inside that holds what you put in it. Doors and windows are cut into the walls of a house, but it is the empty space within that makes it a place to live. The text concludes, in most translations, that we work with what is there, but we use what is not there. It is a strange lesson for an age that measures success by how full things are: full calendars, full factories, full grids. Yet the passage describes something every engineer and every good manager eventually learns. The capacity you are not using is not wasted. It is what lets you turn, hold and shelter when the unexpected arrives, which it always does.
