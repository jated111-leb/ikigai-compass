# The Daily Compass #032 — Lossy

*Tuesday, August 18, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

In engineering, a *lossy* process is one where you don't get out what you put in — some of it is always shed in the transfer, converted to heat or noise or nothing at all. This issue argues that the great promised gains of the moment are all lossy in exactly that sense: what a system produces is reliably more than what it manages to keep. A worker saves eleven hours with AI and spends six of them babysitting the machine. A military fights two decades of war and files away almost none of the lessons. And even the vaunted flywheel of AI improving itself may leak most of its promise to friction. The productivity paradox — economics' 1987 ghost, the observation that you could see the computer age everywhere except in the numbers it was supposed to move — was the first proof of the pattern. The question this week is never how much any of these systems can generate. It's how much survives the handoff.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Botsitting

**What happened.** Rebecca Hinds and Paul Leonardi, in HBR, put a name and a number to the hidden labor of the AI office — ["How Much Time Do Your Employees Spend 'Botsitting'?"](https://hbr.org/2026/08/how-much-time-do-your-employees-spend-botsitting) *Botsitting* is the work of making AI useful: feeding it missing context, checking its outputs, catching and correcting its errors, rerunning the prompt when it drifts. Their data draws the paradox sharply: 87% of digital workers now use AI, 75% say it makes them more productive — some eleven hours a week saved — and yet only 13% say their *organization* performs significantly better. The missing hours have somewhere to go, and they go to botsitting: roughly 6.4 of them a week.

**Why it matters.** This is the productivity paradox (this issue's ⏳) reincarnated with a mechanism. The gain is real and it is measured at the individual keystroke — *I finished that in twenty minutes* — but it evaporates before it reaches the ledger, absorbed by a new and invisible category of oversight work that nobody is counting because it doesn't look like work; it looks like using the tool. It reframes every "AI made me 30% faster" claim, and pairs with last week's #030 finding that the market quietly declines to buy the best model: faster at what, exactly, and does the speed survive the checking? The uncomfortable lesson for anyone deploying AI at scale is that the productivity is the easy part. The retention is the hard part — and the botsitting tax is precisely what eats the difference between the two.

*Source: Hinds & Leonardi, HBR (Aug 2026). ~12 min.*

### ◆ 2. The lessons that didn't stick

**What happened.** In War on the Rocks, Jonathan Schroden asked the bleak retrospective question — ["What Have We Learned from the 9/11 Wars? Not Nearly Enough."](https://warontherocks.com/what-have-we-learned-from-the-9-11-wars-not-nearly-enough/) His argument, drawn from a forthcoming book on two decades of Afghanistan operations, is that the US produced an enormous quantity of hard, costly, specific knowledge about counterterrorism and counterinsurgency — and then failed to institutionalize almost any of it. The experience was generated at maximum price. The retention was close to zero.

**Why it matters.** This is botsitting at the scale of a nation's memory, and it lands the issue's deeper point: experience is not the same thing as memory. An organization can live through the most expensive education imaginable and keep none of it, because *producing* knowledge and *retaining* it are different operations that require different machinery — and only one of them happens automatically. Retention takes deliberate structure: schools that change when the world does (the exact argument of issue #027), doctrine that outlives the officers who wrote it, incentives to remember the inconvenient thing. Most institutions build the machinery to act and never build the machinery to remember, so they relearn the same lessons at full cost every generation. The gap between what an organization has *lived* and what it has *learned* is not a footnote. It is where the next avoidable disaster is already waiting.

*Source: Schroden, War on the Rocks (Aug 17). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Lossy self-improvement

**What happened.** The engine under the entire AI-takeoff story is recursive self-improvement: AI that improves AI, each generation designing a better next one, compounding toward a fast takeoff. The bull case is concrete and serious — Anthropic's Jack Clark has put roughly 60% odds on meaningful autonomous self-improvement by 2028, and reports that Claude already writes the large majority of Anthropic's own merged code ([TIME's survey of the debate](https://time.com/article/2026/08/07/ai-recursive-self-improvement-anthropic-openai/)). Nathan Lambert's counter-thesis names the friction: ["lossy self-improvement."](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/lossy-self-improvement) Each round of improvement leaks — to verification costs, to absorption limits, to the sheer difficulty of integrating a gain into a working system — so the flywheel is lossy, and a lossy flywheel does not run away.

**Why it matters.** This is the same law as briefs 1 and 2, applied to the machines themselves — and that's the point. If a knowledge worker's gains leak to botsitting, and a superpower's hard-won lessons leak to institutional amnesia, on what grounds would AI's self-improvement be the single process in the known universe that suffers no loss in transmission? The bull case models the *production* of intelligence — how fast a system can generate an improvement. The question the whole month keeps circling, from Greenblatt's takeoff timeline to the pacing debate, is really about *retention* — how much of each round actually survives, integrated and verified, to compound into the next. Clean exponentials assume a lossless channel. Nothing in this issue, or in physics, has one. The debate isn't optimism versus pessimism about intelligence. It's a bet on the size of the loss term — and almost no one is modeling it.

*Sources: via TIME (Aug 7); Lambert, Interconnects (Mar 2026). ~20 min.*

**Mogadishu is booming** — the week's best antidote to determinism, via Cowen: the city that has been a global byword for state failure is in a genuine construction boom — a 26-floor luxury tower opening this year, diaspora capital returning, a homegrown coffee chain expanding, a commerce minister saying simply "business is booming." A reminder that once a security threshold is crossed, order and prosperity can compound from below, off the edge of every failed-state model — the rare gain that *isn't* lossy, because people are fighting to keep it. ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/somalia-facts-of-the-day.html), Aug)

**The detector arms race** — on the newest Hard Fork, Pangram's Max Spero on building an AI-"slop" detector that reportedly works — and the deeper worry underneath it: if the open internet narrows to the output of a few chatbots, culture homogenizes into "taste slop," and the ability to tell the generated from the genuine becomes a live economic skill. Generation and detection are now in an arms race, and detection is the underdog. ([Hard Fork](https://www.nytimes.com/column/hard-fork), Aug 14)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Productivity Paradox of Information Technology** — Erik Brynjolfsson, 1993 (naming the puzzle Robert Solow quipped in 1987: "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics"). For decades, businesses poured money into computers and the aggregate productivity numbers refused to move — the gains were real at the desk and invisible in the economy, lost in the gap between output and outcome. Why now: botsitting (brief 1) is the paradox's 2026 edition, and Brynjolfsson's eventual resolution is the warning worth keeping — the gains finally showed up only after firms did the slow, unglamorous work of *reorganizing around* the technology, not just adopting it. Adoption is lossy. Reorganization is what makes it stick. ([MIT / CCS](http://ccs.mit.edu/papers/CCSWP130/ccswp130.html), 1993)

**🃏 WILDCARD: fluids, from the bottom up** — for two centuries physicists described how liquids flow with a single continuum theory that treated the fluid as a smooth, seamless whole. Quanta reports a modern reframing that rebuilds the theory "from the bottom up," out of the actual jostling parts. A quiet reminder that even the most settled science is a model that can be reopened — and that the smooth story is usually hiding a grainier truth. ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/theory-of-fluids-enters-the-21st-century-20260817/), Aug 17)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

If everything leaks, it's worth asking what doesn't. In a Foundation for American Innovation conversation, the writer and maker Simon Sarris makes [the case for a more hands-on life](https://www.thefai.org/posts/can-the-internet-enable-a-more-hands-on-life-with-simon-sarris) — that skills like woodworking, building, growing, making are not inherited but chosen and slowly cultivated, and that the internet, for all its flattening, can still be the thing that teaches you to use your hands. Set against an issue about gains that evaporate in transmission, this is the one channel that isn't lossy: a skill built patiently into your own body and judgment is a gain that no oversight tax can eat, no institution can forget on your behalf, no flywheel can leak away. The productivity of the machine belongs to whoever is counting the tokens. The mastery in a pair of hands belongs to you, and it compounds quietly, and it does not evaporate. In a week spent watching promised gains bleed out through friction, that may be the only kind of improvement worth being sure of: the kind that survives the handoff because there is no handoff — because it is already, permanently, yours.
