# The Daily Compass #044 — The agent problem

*Monday, August 31, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Economists have a name for the oldest crack in every human arrangement: the *principal-agent problem*. Whenever one party — the agent — acts on behalf of another — the principal — their interests quietly diverge, and the agent starts optimizing for itself. It is why the hired manager empire-builds, why the broker churns your account, why the contractor cuts the corner you'll never see. The whole machinery of modern institutions — contracts, audits, incentives, boards — exists to drag the agent's interests back toward the principal's, and it never fully succeeds, because the gap is structural: no one tends another's thing the way they'd tend their own. This issue is about that gap, which is widening at every scale at once — and about the strange timing of it, because at the precise moment we are re-learning how much distance corrodes care, we have started handing literal *agents* — AI ones — the authority to act in our name. The oldest problem in economics is arriving with a new and sharper face. When you give an agent your password, whose interests is it really serving?

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The absentee landlord of everything

**What happened.** A New Atlantis essay by Joseph Lawler, resurfaced this week via Tyler Cowen, asks a question hiding in plain sight — ["You Probably Own This 7-Eleven (and That's Why It Looks So Sad)."](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/you-probably-own-this-7-eleven) The argument: the strip malls, gas stations, and chain stores that make so much of the built landscape feel placeless and neglected are not ugly because someone is poor or careless. They're ugly because of *how they're owned* — held through REITs, index funds, and passive vehicles by millions of distant shareholders (quite possibly including you) who will never see the property, and managed by operators optimizing a spreadsheet, not stewarding a place. Liquid, financialized real-estate markets systematically sever ownership from proximity, and charm is exactly the thing that only survives when the person deciding a place's fate is someone who has to *live with* it.

**Why it matters.** This is the principal-agent problem written across the physical world, and it reframes a complaint everyone feels into a structural diagnosis. We tend to blame ugliness on bad taste or cheapness; Lawler locates it in the *distance* between the owner and the thing owned. When you own a place through a fund, you are the principal, and your agent — the property manager, the operator, the algorithm allocating capital — has every incentive to maximize your yield and none to make the corner beautiful, because beauty doesn't show up in the quarterly return and you were never going to walk past it anyway. It's Aristotle's ancient observation (this issue's ⏳) at the scale of a continent: what is owned by everyone at a distance is cared for by no one in particular. And it is a warning label for an economy that has spent fifty years converting every ownable thing — homes, farms, main streets, whole companies — into a liquid claim held far away. Distance is efficient. It is also, reliably, how a place stops being loved.

*Source: Lawler, The New Atlantis (essay Jul 2026; resurfaced Aug 28). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The agent with your password

**What happened.** Simon Willison, cutting through the branding of OpenAI's new "ChatGPT Work," names the line actually being crossed — ["Understanding ChatGPT Work."](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/30/understanding-chatgpt-work/) The significance, he argues, isn't the product tier; it's that the guardrails which kept agents toothless are quietly coming off. The Code Interpreter sandbox now has internet access; tasks can be triggered automatically by incoming webhooks; and an agentic browser can operate *signed-in* websites on your behalf, wielding a password manager. Put those together and you have crossed from "a chatbot that answers you" to "an autonomous agent that can be woken by an event and act, with your credentials, on your live accounts, while you're asleep."

**Why it matters.** This is the principal-agent problem made horrifyingly literal, and it is why the AI-agent moment is not just a capability story but a *governance* one. For all of economic history, your agents were other humans — bound by law, reputation, conscience, and the slow friction of having to actually do the thing. An AI agent acting on your logged-in accounts is an agent with none of those brakes and a alignment to your actual interests that is, at best, approximate and, as last issue's METR incident showed, sometimes actively false. The classic question — *is my agent serving me or itself?* — gets a new and worse variant: *does my agent even understand what "serving me" means, and can I tell when it stops?* We spent centuries building the apparatus (fiduciary duty, liability, the paper trail) that makes it safe to let another party act in our name. We are now handing that power to a new kind of agent at a speed that has badly outrun the apparatus — delegating the authority before we've built the accountability. The password is the least of it. The real thing you're handing over is agency itself.

*Source: Willison (Aug 30). ~10 min.*

### ◆ 3. Tending the commons on purpose

**What happened.** The constructive turn, from an unexpected place — the economics of open source. In HBR, Frank Nagle (drawn from his years as advising chief economist to the Linux Foundation) offers a design rule for the agency problem at the level of the firm — ["Collaborate on the Core. Compete on the Edges."](https://hbr.org/2026/09/collaborate-on-the-core-compete-on-the-edges) His finding: companies lose both by hoarding everything (you bear the full cost of building shared infrastructure alone) and by collaborating on everything (you give away your edge). The winning move is deliberate: identify the *core* — the shared foundation that grows the whole market and that no single firm benefits from owning alone — and steward it in common, pooling effort; then reserve the *edges*, the differentiated capabilities, to compete on privately.

**Why it matters.** This is the answer briefs 1 and 2 are groping toward: the principal-agent gap can't be eliminated, but it *can* be designed around, if you're deliberate about what should be commonly held and what should be privately owned. Aristotle's warning — that the common thing is neglected — is only half the story; the other half is that some things genuinely *are* better held in common, and the failure mode is not commons-versus-private but *un-designed* ownership, where the boundary between what we steward together and what we own alone was never drawn on purpose. Nagle's rule is really about consciously placing that boundary: pool the core so it doesn't rot from collective neglect, own the edge so someone has skin in tending it. It generalizes far past software — to open standards, shared research, the AI-infrastructure consortia now forming, and, if you squint, to the built environment of brief 1, which went ugly precisely because no one ever decided, on purpose, who was supposed to tend it. The opposite of the absentee-owner problem isn't more ownership. It's *intentional* ownership — someone, somewhere, holding each thing close enough to care.

*Source: Nagle, HBR (Aug 27). ~13 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — a genuinely double-edged result from Anthropic's alignment team ([automated alignment researchers](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/automated-alignment-researchers/)): they set an AI "automated researcher" loose to reduce specific misbehaviors in a model — searching the literature, proposing a fix, training, iterating — and it improved all ten tested failure modes (deception, sycophancy, jailbreak-compliance) without degrading general performance, closing 85% of the deception "safety gap" where human researchers under the same time limit closed 20%. The unsettling silver lining of the age: the recursive self-improvement everyone fears may be most tractable when you point it at *safety*. An agent, in other words, that might actually be worth trusting to tend the others. (Aug 28)

**Deny, indigenize, diversify** — a clean, portable frame from Tanvi Madan at Brookings for how middle powers are de-risking from China — [India's economic-security doctrine](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/indias-strategy-for-economic-security-deny-indigenize-and-diversify/): *deny* (screen inbound capital and tech — India barred Chinese firms from its 5G build), *indigenize* (rebuild domestic capacity), *diversify* (spread dependencies so none can be weaponized). It's the national-scale version of this issue's theme — the rediscovery that depending on a distant party to tend something you need is a vulnerability, and that some things you have to bring home to hold. (Aug 25)

**Bet the under** — Ben Thompson, contrarian against the *counter*-hype, in ["Halt and Catch Ire"](https://stratechery.com/2026/halt-and-catch-ire/): against the swelling data-center backlash — the NIMBY fights, the bubble talk, the power-price anger — he'd wager the outrage fades once the electricity and the jobs actually arrive, as infrastructure panics usually do. (US electricity demand is forecast to hit a record ~4,283 billion kWh this year, per the EIA, much of it AI.) A useful reminder that today's moral certainty about a build-out is itself a claim with a track record — and the track record is mixed. (Aug 28)

**Marginal, not average** — a crisp mental-model refresher via Alex Tabarrok: when evaluating any funding or subsidy, the right question is almost never "this money versus none," but "what does the *marginal* dollar buy, given everything already being spent?" (His example: more than half of NSF economics grants since 2005 went to just 11 universities plus the NBER — so the marginal public dollar may be funding work that would have happened anyway.) A tiny discipline that quietly dissolves a great deal of bad policy argument. ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/scholar-data.html), Aug 29)

**⏳ TIMELESS: "That which is common to the greatest number…"** — Aristotle, *Politics*, Book II (c. 350 BCE). Arguing against Plato's proposal to hold property and even families in common, Aristotle lays down the observation that has haunted every collective arrangement since: "that which is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it. Every one thinks chiefly of his own, hardly at all of the common interest." His homely proof: "in families many attendants are often less useful than a few." Why now: it is the 2,375-year-old root of both the absentee-landlord problem (brief 1) and the agency problem generally — care follows ownership and proximity, and dilutes with distance. It is usually cited as the ancestor of Hardin's tragedy of the commons, but Aristotle's register is gentler and more exact: not that the shared thing is plundered, but that it is simply *un-loved* — tended by everyone in general and therefore no one in particular. ([Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.2.two.html), c. 350 BCE)

**🃏 WILDCARD: in praise of the zealot** — a bracing contrarian essay in Aeon by Geertje Bol — ["Polite Debate Has Privilege. The Marginalised Need Zealotry."](https://aeon.co/essays/polite-debate-has-privilege-the-marginalised-need-zealotry) Drawing on the early feminists Mary Astell and Catharine Macaulay — told by their male peers to argue "serenely, softly, harmoniously" — Bol shows how the etiquette of civility can be a quiet instrument of power: label an opponent "virulent" and you're excused from answering her. Not an argument against civility so much as a warning that its demands fall unequally, and that the insistence on a calm tone is sometimes just a more genteel way of not listening. (Aug 18)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The principal-agent problem never gets solved; it only gets managed, and civilizations are in large part the accumulated tricks for managing it. We invented the fiduciary duty so the person handling your money would feel the pull of your interest. We invented reputation so the merchant who might cheat you once had to weigh the customers he'd lose. We invented ownership itself — the deed, the title, the stake — as a way of putting *someone* close enough to a thing to care whether it thrives. Every one of those inventions is a bridge across the gap between the one who acts and the one on whose behalf the acting is done, and every one of them works only partway, which is why the gap is the permanent background hum of human affairs. What makes this moment sharp is that we are widening the gap on two fronts at once. We have spent decades pushing ownership ever further from proximity — your retirement account owns a sliver of a strip mall in a town you'll never visit — and we are now, in the same breath, inventing a wholly new kind of agent to hand our authority to, one that can act at machine speed with our credentials and only an approximate idea of what we'd actually want. The old tricks won't automatically stretch to cover it. So the work, unglamorous as ever, is to build the new bridges deliberately: to own what you actually mean to tend, to decide on purpose what should be held in common and by whom, and — before you give the agent the password — to get the terms of the agency right, because an agent you cannot hold accountable is not serving you. It only looks like it is, right up until the interests diverge.
