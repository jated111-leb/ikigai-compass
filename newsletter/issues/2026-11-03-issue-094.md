# The Daily Compass #094 — Strong at the broken places

*Tuesday, November 3, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Around 2010, as Netflix moved its streaming service from its own data centres onto Amazon's cloud, its engineers built a piece of software with a deliberately alarming name: Chaos Monkey. Its job was to break things. As the company explained in a blog post in July 2011, Chaos Monkey would pick a server at random from each cluster and, at some point during business hours, turn it off without warning. The timing was the point. By causing failures on purpose, while engineers were at their desks to watch and fix them, Netflix forced its systems to be built so that the loss of any single machine would not matter. The tool was soon joined by a "Simian Army" of others that simulated larger outages, and it was later released as open-source software. Chaos engineering has since become a discipline of its own, and it rests on an old idea: some systems get stronger only when they are stressed. Muscles, bones, immune systems and, sometimes, organisations and people share this property. But stress can also break things, and the line between stress that strengthens and stress that harms is not always obvious. This issue is about that line: what makes some organisations come out of shocks stronger, why the harder way to learn often works better, and what happens when AI quietly removes the struggle.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Better than resilient

**What happened.** In *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder* (2012), Nassim Nicholas Taleb argued that we lack a word for the opposite of fragile. "Robust" or "resilient" will not do, he wrote, because the resilient resists shocks and stays the same; the antifragile gets better. The concept is easiest to see in biology, but the evidence on companies suggests that true antifragility is rare. In ["Roaring Out of Recession"](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/9-of-companies-emerge-from-recession-stronger-heres-how) (*Harvard Business Review*, 2010), Ranjay Gulati, Nitin Nohria and Franz Wohlgezogen studied 4,700 public companies through the recessions of the early 1980s, 1990s and 2000s. Seventeen per cent did not survive as independent companies, and most of those that did took years to recover. Only about 9% came out of a downturn better than they went in, outperforming both their rivals and their own pre-recession growth. They were not the companies that cut hardest or spent most. They combined defence and offence: cutting costs mainly by making operations more efficient rather than simply cutting staff, while continuing to invest in research, marketing and new assets. Toyota built a version of the same principle into daily operations. On its assembly lines, any worker who spotted a problem could pull the andon cord to stop the line, and supervisors came to thank, not blame, the worker who did. Small, frequent disruptions surfaced problems while they were still cheap to fix.

**Why it matters.** Most organisations are designed to avoid stress, and many succeed until a big shock arrives. Antifragile organisations instead expose themselves to small, survivable stresses on purpose, as Netflix and Toyota did, and treat each one as information. That is the practical meaning of the idea: not heroics in a crisis, but routines that turn small failures into learning before the large ones arrive. There is an important caveat. In ["From resilience to antifragility?"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11625-026-01883-z) (*Sustainability Science*, September 2026), Philip Arthur, Tyler DesRoches, Daniel Fischer and Henrik Thorén argue that while antifragility can be a better goal than simply bouncing back, a system that becomes stronger overall may do so by pushing fragility onto particular groups at its margins. For leaders, the question is not only whether the company gains from disorder, but who absorbs the stress.

*Source: Taleb, Antifragile (2012); Gulati, Nohria & Wohlgezogen, HBR (Mar 2010); Arthur et al., Sustainability Science (Sep 2026). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. The method that feels worse and works better

**What happened.** The psychologists Robert and Elizabeth Bjork coined the term "desirable difficulties" for ways of learning that feel harder and slower but produce better long-term results. One of the clearest demonstrations is a 2008 study by Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork, ["Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the 'enemy of induction'?"](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-interleaved-training-and-category-learning-kang.pdf) (*Psychological Science*). Participants studied paintings by 12 artists, six by each, and were then asked to identify the artist of new paintings they had never seen. Paintings studied interleaved, with artists mixed together, were classified far more accurately than those studied in blocks, one artist at a time: 59% against 36%. Yet nearly 80% of participants believed that blocking had worked as well or better, even after the test. The difficult method worked better and felt worse. Stress itself may work similarly, depending partly on how we see it. In ["Rethinking stress"](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/crum_et_al._2013_-_rethinking_stress.pdf) (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2013), Alia Crum, Peter Salovey and Shawn Achor studied nearly 400 employees of a large financial institution during the 2008 financial crisis. Those randomly assigned to watch short videos presenting stress as enhancing, rather than debilitating, came to see stress more positively and reported fewer health symptoms and better work performance. And a meta-analysis by Jeffery LePine, Nathan Podsakoff and Marcie LePine (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2005) distinguished two kinds of workplace stress: "challenge" stressors such as workload and responsibility, which tend to raise motivation, and "hindrance" stressors such as red tape and unclear roles, which mainly drag performance down.

**Why it matters.** Leaders often judge training, development and work design by how they feel, and the research suggests that feelings are a poor guide. The methods that build skill fastest, such as mixing problems, testing instead of rereading and spacing practice over time, feel less productive than the alternatives. Some stress is the same. Stretch assignments, demanding goals and real responsibility are challenge stressors that people tend to grow from, especially if they understand them that way. Bureaucracy, confusion and pointless obstacles are hindrances that wear people down without making them stronger. A useful discipline for any manager is to sort the pressure in their team into these two piles, and to work hard on removing the second while protecting the first.

*Source: Kornell & Bjork, Psychological Science 19 (2008); Crum, Salovey & Achor, JPSP 104(4) (2013); LePine, Podsakoff & LePine, AMJ 48(5) (2005). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. When the tutor does the work

**What happened.** If struggle is part of learning, what happens when AI removes it? A randomised trial at the University of Maryland gives a sobering answer. In "The Effects of Course-Integrated AI Tutoring on Student Performance and Engagement" (October 2026), Jing Liu, Tracy Sweet and colleagues randomly assigned 30 instructors, teaching 2,379 undergraduates in fall 2025, to offer or not offer students a GPT-4o study assistant built into their course website. Students with access to the tutor [received lower final grades](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/students-offered-an-ai-tutor-got-lower-grades-and-disengaged-from-course-materials-university-of-maryland-trial-finds), by 0.37 standard deviations, and engaged less with the existing course materials. The losses were larger for first-generation students. The authors caution that the result does not show AI tutoring cannot work; their point is that a tool should be judged by what it replaces. A second set of experiments suggests why. In ["AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721) (2026), Grace Liu, Brian Christian, Tsvetomira Dumbalska, Michiel Bakker and Rachit Dubey ran randomised trials with 1,222 participants on maths and reading problems. AI help improved performance while it was available. But after only about ten minutes of assistance, people who then lost access performed worse than those who had never had it, and were more likely to give up. The damage was concentrated among those who used the AI to get answers, rather than hints.

**Why it matters.** Most AI tools are designed to remove friction, and for many tasks that is exactly right. But some friction is how people learn. In a comment piece in *Communications Psychology* in February, ["Against frictionless AI"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41735544/), Emily Zohar, Paul Bloom and Michael Inzlicht argued that AI as a supplement for people who already have a skill is very different from AI as a substitute for people still building it. For organisations, the implication is to decide deliberately where struggle is worth keeping, particularly for junior staff and for skills the company will depend on in future. That can mean configuring tools to give hints and questions rather than answers by default, keeping some work AI-free, and judging tools not only by the output they produce today but by the capability they leave behind.

*Source: Liu, Sweet et al., EdWorkingPaper 26-1598 / IZA DP 18989 (Oct 2026); Liu, Christian, Dumbalska, Bakker & Dubey, arXiv 2604.04721 (2026); Zohar, Bloom & Inzlicht, Communications Psychology (Feb 2026). ~35 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the trees that needed wind** — a popular story holds that trees grown inside Biosphere 2, the sealed ecosystem built in the Arizona desert in the early 1990s, grew quickly but toppled before maturity because there was no wind to make them strong. It is a lovely parable, and it is [poorly sourced](https://letgrow.org/metaphor-alert-trees-need-wind-to-grow-strong/): the versions in circulation cite no study, and conditions inside the dome went wrong in many ways at once. The science it borrows from is real, though. In 1973 the plant physiologist M.J. Jaffe published a paper in *Planta* coining the word ["thigmomorphogenesis"](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00387472) for the way many plants respond to being touched or shaken. Rubbing the stems of young bean plants gently for about ten seconds a day markedly slowed their growth in height, and later research found that mechanically stressed plants tend to grow shorter and stockier, with thicker stems. The lesson survives the myth: a little buffeting builds the structure that calm conditions never require. (Jaffe, Planta, 1973)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the birds that trained with ankle weights** — researchers in Jonas Rubenson's lab at Penn State fitted young guinea fowl with a small weight, about 4% of body mass, on one lower leg while they grew. As adults, these birds carried the extra load almost effortlessly: in a paper in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, the team reports that they moved the weight with [about 22% less energy](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/chronic-limb-loading-results-in-remarkable-load-carriage-economy-in-growing-fowl/) than birds that had grown up unloaded, the lowest cost of load-carrying the authors know of in any legged animal. Stress applied during growth became built-in capacity. (Proceedings of the Royal Society B, Jul 2026; reported Oct 2026)

**Why some failures lead somewhere** — not all failure makes people stronger. In ["Quantifying the dynamics of failure across science, startups and security"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07562) (*Nature*, 2019), Yian Yin, Yang Wang, James Evans and Dashun Wang modelled how each new attempt builds on earlier ones, using data on grant applications, start-up founders and other repeated attempts. They found a sharp threshold. People who reuse and refine what worked in earlier failures improve steadily towards success; those who start over each time do not improve at all. Early on, eventual winners and losers look almost identical. The difference is not how often they fail but how much each failure teaches them. (Nature 575, 2019)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Near the end of *A Farewell to Arms* (1929), Ernest Hemingway's narrator, Frederic Henry, reflects on courage and the war. "If people bring so much courage to this world the world has to kill them to break them, so of course it kills them," he says. "The world breaks every one and afterward many are strong at the broken places." The second sentence is quoted everywhere as a promise of resilience. The passage goes on, more darkly, to say that the world kills those who will not break, "the very good and the very gentle and the very brave impartially". Hemingway was not writing a motivational poster, and the full passage is a useful corrective to the cheerful version of today's theme. Stress does not strengthen automatically. It strengthens when it is survivable, when there is time to recover, and when people and organisations learn from what broke. Chaos Monkey turned off one server at a time, in working hours, with engineers watching; it did not switch off the whole company at midnight. The art, for leaders, is in the dose: enough strain to build strength at the broken places, and never so much that nothing is left to heal.
