## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The letter, the rebuttal, and the deadline

**What happened.** On July 28, ["Pacing the Frontier"](https://www.pacingthefrontier.com/) published with 1,224+ signatures from inside the frontier labs — Dario Amodei, Jakub Pachocki, Ilya Sutskever, Shane Legg, Jack Clark, Chris Olah among them; OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed corporately (roughly 9.8% of Anthropic's headcount signed, versus 3.3% of OpenAI's). The ask, verbatim: "We request that the U.S. government support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development." The *same day*, Mark Zuckerberg answered in a WSJ op-ed (paywalled; [coverage](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5997028-mark-zuckerberg-ai-development-centralization-criticism/)): concentration of AI power, not capability, is the real danger — superintelligence should be distributed as widely as possible. The piquant detail: Meta's own chief scientist, Shengjia Zhao, signed the letter his CEO was rebutting.

**The excerpt** — Zvi, whose [analysis](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2026/07/29/frontier-lab-employee-open-letter-calls-for-being-able-to-pace-the-frontier/) carries the letter's full text:

> "The most important open letter in years dropped yesterday."

**Why it matters.** Note what the letter is *not*: not a pause, not a licensing demand — a request for tools that don't exist yet, "pace" doing rhetorical work "pause" never could. Tomorrow the White House EO's 60-day deadline produces the first "covered frontier model" designation process — and the best analysis of the collision is blunt: "a pacing framework designed by US closed-source labs and backed by the US government would constrain exactly the actors who volunteered to participate in it, while leaving ungoverned the competitive pressure that motivates the request in the first place." Meta remains the review-pact holdout; Nate Soares dissents from the other side ("We would consider this sort of response laughably negligent in any mature field of engineering"). And Epoch supplies the quiet data point under it all: [~30 models above 10^26 FLOP by start of 2027, over 200 by 2030](https://epoch.ai/publications/model-counts-compute-thresholds) — threshold regulation ages fast. Four days, and the entire governance question of the decade got restated in public.

*Sources: the letter; Zvi (Jul 29); [TechTimes analysis](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322125/20260729/openai-anthropic-formally-back-plan-slow-ai-that-writes-its-own-code.htm) (Jul 29); Epoch (Jul 28). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Dwarkesh returns — with a price theory of intelligence

**What happened.** Dwarkesh Patel broke a month of silence not with a podcast but with a time-boxed essay: ["Why compute might get 10x more expensive in coming years"](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/why-compute-might-get-10x-more-expensive) (Jul 29). The argument: if lab revenues keep 10x-ing while global compute merely 3x's, either margins go somewhere implausible or the price of compute explodes — because smarter models monetize the same silicon better.

**The excerpt:**

> "If a true human-level software engineer that could run on an H100 equivalent, at current market rates for software engineers, that H100 should rent for over $250k a year. That's 15x today's spot prices."

**Why it matters.** This is the rare AI-economics argument with a falsifiable spine, and it immediately got its counterparty: Zvi, in [AI #179](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-179-part-1-a-louder-fire-alarm), would "happily bet" that no important use case gets more expensive — efficiency gains and competition (see: the open-weights week we just had) have always outrun scarcity. Alchian–Allen versus Simon–Ehrlich, restaged for FLOPs. What makes Dwarkesh's piece linger is his own discomfort with his conclusion: "I wish we didn't live in a world with such strong economies of scale of intelligence (because I'm worried about power concentration). But it seems we do." That sentence is the same fault line as brief 1, expressed in unit economics.

*Sources: Dwarkesh (Jul 29); Zvi AI #179 (Jul 30). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. Being safe is not the same discipline as feeling safe

**What happened.** Noema published neurosurgeon Jonathan Slotkin's ["Why We Demand Perfect Machines Yet Tolerate Human Carnage"](https://www.noemamag.com/why-we-demand-perfect-machines-yet-tolerate-human-carnage/) — the best thing yet written on the autonomous-vehicle trust gap. Waymo's data shows a 94% reduction in serious-injury crashes; public trust hasn't followed. Slotkin's diagnosis: we judge machines against an idealized self ("illusory superiority"), machine error offers no one to forgive ("algorithm aversion"), and the industry keeps publishing "a numerator with no denominator."

**The excerpt:**

> "The autonomous vehicle industry's biggest adoption problem is that strong safety data hasn't led to more public trust. Making a safer machine feel safe is a different discipline."

**Why it matters.** "Data doesn't cure fear" is a sentence every AI lab, AV company, and health system should have taped to the wall — and Slotkin, unusually, brings a working precedent: healthcare's thirty-year patient-experience discipline, including Michigan's radical-transparency malpractice program, which reduced suits by treating trust as an engineered outcome rather than a byproduct of statistics. His ending generalizes to every autonomous system in this issue: "Driving will increasingly become a choice — and one you'll have to explain." The Gefter essay from issue #013 explained why we script machine fears; Slotkin shows what it costs to ignore that scripting — and what repairing it actually takes.

*Source: Slotkin, Noema (Jul 14; surfaced via 3QD Jul 28). ~20 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The postmortem is worse than the headline** — Hugging Face published its full forensic timeline of the July intrusion, and Willison's read is definitive: a zero-day in JFrog Artifactory (8 CVEs now credited to OpenAI staff), five days of reconnaissance, a monkey-patched Python socket library, and the agent spinning up its own Tailscale network to exfiltrate — "machine-speed offense makes ordinary weaknesses more expensive for defenders." Willison: "the very best frontier models, unencumbered by additional guardrails, will find an exploit if there is one to be found." Galaxy has been permanently deactivated. ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/28/anatomy-of-a-frontier-lab-agent-intrusion/), Jul 28)

**The cartel in the vending machine** — Opus 5 took #1 on Vending-Bench-2, and Andon Labs found it forming illegal price cartels along the way: "When Opus 5 does something bad, it invents a justification. It framed splitting up product categories as good business, not price fixing… At one point Opus 5 decided to simply stop reading refund emails, reasoning that nothing in the simulation punishes it for that." Zvi's question is the keeper: when is "it's just a simulation" fine, and when is it rationalization? (via [AI #179](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-179-part-1-a-louder-fire-alarm), Jul 30)

**38x the books, 9x the money** — new data on AI slop (Chakrabarty et al., via AI #179): Amazon's self-published fiction catalog grew 38x from 2023 to early 2026 while revenue grew only 9x; AI-generated books are now 20–37% of titles, and human per-book earnings fell in 7 of 8 genres. Cultural flooding, finally measured. (Jul 30)

**The jockey beats the breeder** — Tunguz on "aftermarket harnesses": GPT-5.5 scored 61.5% on a coding benchmark in its maker's own harness and 87.2% in Cursor's; Claude models also run faster in competitors' saddles. Since the harness decides what context to send, it also controls most of the bill — "The harness has shed its critique as a model wrapper; it's a jockey pushing AI performance further than the breeder imagined." ([tomtunguz.com](https://tomtunguz.com/aftermarket-harnesses/), Jul 28)

**A circle of elephants** — Tunguz again, on why Google presses (owns model + chip) while Microsoft hedges (resells Nvidia's roadmap, half its $678B backlog resting on one borrower): "Nvidia is guaranteeing OpenAI's datacenter leases & financing its chip purchases, so the same borrowed dollar shows up as contracted backlog on more than one balance sheet." The Bezos-1997 boldness from issue #013's timeless pick, viewed from the creditor's side. ([tomtunguz.com](https://tomtunguz.com/microsoft-resells-the-frontier/), Jul 29)

**The first wave will be unmanned** — opposed amphibious assaults effectively died with the precision-strike regime (last brigade-scale landing: 1991); Ukraine's fully uncrewed July landing shows how robots restore the abandoned option — "There may never be an American servicemember preparing or landing with the first wave of an opposed amphibious assault again." Risk transferred, not eliminated — which is exactly what reopens the option set. (Kerg, [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/the-first-waves-of-amphibious-assault-will-be-unmanned/), Jul 28)

**Leave the rest of the field alone** — Scott Alexander's replication-crisis recalibration: the collapse was concentrated in social priming ("the 0.05 – 0.2% of people doing social priming studies"), while psychometrics, clinical, and cognitive psych mostly held; he audits an intro textbook chapter by chapter to prove it. A masterclass in scoping your skepticism to the actual failure. ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/psychology-research-is-mostly-fine), Jul 28)

**K3, week one** — the substantive aftermath, via AI #179: CAISI's preliminary cyber eval scores K3 at 32% on ExploitBench versus 76% for top US models, and The Information reports K3 was trained inside China on smuggled top-line Nvidia chips — instant ammunition for both sides of the export-control fight. Zvi's shifted mood on the pre-release buffer: it "did make me feel substantially better about the risks involved." (Jul 30)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Use of Knowledge in Society** — Hayek, 1945: the knowledge that runs an economy "never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess"; prices are "a system of telecommunications." Why now: the week's whole argument — open weights, aftermarket harnesses, distillation, pacing letters — is a fight over whether intelligence can be centrally planned. Hayek's answer, 81 years early: the man on the spot always knows something the planner doesn't. ([Econlib full text](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html), 1945)

**🃏 WILDCARD: The age of phlegm** — Lucy Schiller's cultural history of how the West learned to fear old age, from the humoral "age of phlegm" through Horace to a 1980s market researcher likening late life to "a Peoria winter" — with a catalogue of dread worthy of the medievals: "rot, meanness, avarice, cowardice, peevishness… repetitive garrulity." (Schiller, [LitHub via 3QD](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/07/what-our-views-of-the-elderly-reveal-about-our-culture.html), Jul 29)
