# The Daily Compass #084 — Accidents and sagacity

*Saturday, October 24, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On 28 January 1754 the English writer Horace Walpole sat down to thank his friend Horace Mann, the British envoy in Florence, for a portrait. In the course of the letter he described a lucky find he had made while researching the sitter's family arms, and he gave the experience a name. It was, he wrote, "almost of that kind which I call Serendipity", after "a silly fairy tale, called The Three Princes of Serendip", whose heroes were "always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things which they were not in quest of". Two details are worth noticing. Walpole misremembered his own source: he recalled the princes deducing a mule they had never seen, when in the tale it is a camel. And his definition needed two ingredients, not one. Serendipity was never pure luck; it was accident met by sagacity. Exactly a century later, in December 1854, Louis Pasteur told an audience at the new Faculty of Sciences in Lille that "in the fields of observation, chance favours only the prepared mind." This issue is about that pairing: how much of success is luck, how organisations can make useful accidents more likely, and whether AI is quietly narrowing the range of things we stumble upon.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Don't copy the winner

**What happened.** Business writing loves the outlier: the founder, the fund manager or the company with the most spectacular results, studied for lessons everyone else can copy. In ["Top performers are not the most impressive when extreme performance indicates unreliability"](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1116048109) (*PNAS*, 2012), Jerker Denrell and Chengwei Liu showed why that habit can mislead. The forces that make outcomes unpredictable, noise and self-reinforcing dynamics in which early success breeds more success, also make extreme results weak evidence of ability. Whether the very top performer is the most skilled depends on whether such a result could be reached by skill alone. Where it usually requires luck as well, the best evidence of ability lies a little below the top, among the consistently strong performers, not with the single most spectacular one. In a later paper, ["In search of behavioral opportunities from misattributions of luck"](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/114019) (*Academy of Management Review*, 2019), Denrell, Christina Fang and Liu turned the idea into strategy. Because most people mistake luck for skill and ignore regression to the mean, assets, firms and people that have recently been lucky or unlucky are often mispriced, and a strategist who reads luck correctly can buy what others undervalue.

**Why it matters.** The practical lessons are uncomfortable for anyone who benchmarks against "best in class". First, be wary of imitating the single most extreme success; its recipe may include a large dose of fortune that cannot be copied. Second, judge people and projects on a longer record and on the quality of their decisions, not only on the last outcome. Third, expect regression: the star of this year is more likely to be ordinary next year than the steady performer just behind. A well-known simulation by Alessandro Pluchino, Alessio Biondo and Andrea Rapisarda, "Talent vs Luck" (2018), makes the same point in toy form. When talent is spread on a bell curve but careers are buffeted by random events, the most successful agents are usually of only average talent. It is a model, not evidence, but it shows how easily luck can masquerade as merit.

*Source: Denrell & Liu, PNAS 109(24) (2012); Denrell, Fang & Liu, Academy of Management Review 44(4) (2019); Pluchino, Biondo & Rapisarda, Advances in Complex Systems (2018). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Engineering the collision

**What happened.** If luck matters, can an organisation make more of the useful kind? Two natural experiments suggest that physical space is one lever. In ["Microgeography and the Direction of Inventive Activity"](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/120163) (*Management Science*, 2018), Christian Catalini studied the Jussieu science campus in Paris, where the removal of asbestos forced laboratories to move into new buildings in ways the scientists did not choose. Labs that happened to be placed together became much more likely to collaborate, and the effect was strongest for pairs that would otherwise have found it hard to discover each other. A later study by Maria Roche, Alexander Oettl and Catalini, "Proximate (Co-)Working" (*Management Science*, 2024), looked at startups in a coworking space that were assigned desks they had not picked. Startups tended to adopt the web technologies used by their neighbours, but the [effect faded sharply beyond about 20 metres](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-much-does-proximity-influence-startup-innovation), and it was strongest between firms that worked in very different markets yet socialised with each other.

**Why it matters.** These studies give Pasteur's line a practical form. Chance encounters can be engineered, by deciding who sits near whom, who shares a kitchen and which teams are mixed, but their value depends on difference and on the social habits that turn a passing conversation into an exchange of ideas. That matters for the continuing debate about remote and hybrid work. Distance makes it easy to keep up with people we already know and hard to bump into people we don't, and it is the second group that produces the surprising combinations. Leaders do not need everyone in the office every day to act on this. They need to design for the right collisions: bringing unlike teams together on purpose, protecting informal time, and treating the accidental conversation as part of the work rather than a break from it.

*Source: Catalini, Management Science 64(9) (2018); Roche, Oettl & Catalini, Management Science (2024), via HBS Working Knowledge. ~25 min.*

### ◆ 3. The narrowing answer

**What happened.** Serendipity depends on meeting the unexpected, and more and more of our first encounters with a topic now happen through an AI chatbot rather than a page of search results. A team at the University of Copenhagen led by Dustin Wright and Isabelle Augenstein set out to measure what that does to the range of information people see. In ["Epistemic Diversity and Knowledge Collapse in Large Language Models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04226), accepted at the EMNLP 2026 conference and [publicised by the university on September 28](https://via.ritzau.dk/pressemeddelelse/15174582/ai-chatbots-giver-os-et-smalt-udvalg-af-viden-vi-risikerer-et-videnskollaps-siger-forskere), they tested 27 language models on 155 topics, from marriage and nuclear weapons to K-pop, using many different phrasings drawn from real user questions and analysing some 70 million individual claims. Every model offered less varied information than a Google search. Even the most diverse, OpenAI's GPT-5, gave at least 18.7% less variety. Newer models did somewhat better than older ones, and models that looked things up before answering were more varied. The answers also leaned towards English-language knowledge over local knowledge.

**Why it matters.** The authors are careful: "knowledge collapse has not happened yet," Augenstein says, and the study measures breadth, not accuracy. But the mechanism is plain. Models learn the most common patterns in their training text and smooth away the rest, and if future models are trained on text produced by earlier ones, the range could narrow further with each generation. For a reader who relies on a chatbot for research, the risk is not a wrong answer but a typical one: the consensus view, delivered fluently, with the odd footnote and the strange adjacent idea quietly removed. The remedy is the old one. A prepared mind needs varied inputs, so use AI for speed but keep sources that can surprise you: original papers, specialist voices, other languages, and the occasional search that leads somewhere you did not mean to go.

*Source: Wright, Augenstein et al., arXiv 2510.04226 / EMNLP 2026; University of Copenhagen release (Sep 28, 2026). ~30 min (paper).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the window that was probably shut** — the most famous lucky accident in science is Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928, usually told as a mould spore drifting through an open window onto a forgotten culture plate. Historians have [picked the story apart](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2002/176/4/howard-florey-alexander-fleming-and-fairy-tale-penicillin). Colleagues later said the window was usually kept shut, and a likelier source of the spore was the laboratory of the mycologist C. J. La Touche on the floor below. More importantly, the accident alone did not produce a medicine. Fleming published in 1929 and then largely moved on. It took Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley and their Oxford team, more than a decade later, to purify penicillin and prove in experiments on mice in 1940 that it could cure infection. The accident was Fleming's; the sagacity was spread across many people and many years. (Medical Journal of Australia, 2002)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the coral that healed by touch** — in 2025 the Stanford marine biologist Steve Palumbi noticed that two fragments of coral had fallen against each other in a lab tank, one healthy and one bleached, and that the bleached one was regaining colour where they touched. A study led by the doctoral student Danny Collins, published this month in *Science Advances*, followed up the accident. When bleached fragments were placed in contact with healthy fragments of the same colony, their tissues fused into a "nutrient highway" and they [recovered within about seven weeks](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/15/theyre-not-dead-yet-bleached-corals-recover-in-record-time-after-touching-healthy-clones), while fragments kept a few millimetres apart died. It works only between genetic clones; unrelated corals attack each other with stinging cells. It is a small lab study, but a fine example of a prepared mind noticing a fallen piece of coral. (Euronews / Science Advances, Oct 2026)

**Boards pay for luck** — if luck is hard to see, boards often fail to see it. In a classic study, ["Are CEOs Rewarded for Luck?"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7604) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2001), Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan found that oil-company chief executives were paid more when oil prices rose, a gain they had done nothing to earn, and that such "pay for luck" was weaker in better-governed firms. Bad luck is punished too. Dirk Jenter and Fadi Kanaan, studying 3,365 CEO departures from 1993 to 2009, found that a fall in the performance of a CEO's whole industry from the 90th to the 10th percentile [doubled the probability](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12068) that the CEO was forced out, even though theory says boards should filter out shocks that hit every firm alike. (QJE 2001; Journal of Finance 2015)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Pasteur's line is a comfort to the diligent: prepare well, and chance will tend to reward you. An older text offers the necessary counterweight. "I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all." The writer of Ecclesiastes was not arguing against effort; the same book urges its readers to work with all their might. He was arguing against the belief that effort guarantees the result. Both truths belong together. Preparation raises the odds, widens the surface on which good accidents can land and lets us recognise them when they come. It does not abolish chance. The sensible response is the one Walpole's princes modelled: travel widely, notice what you were not looking for, and hold your successes, and other people's failures, a little more lightly.
