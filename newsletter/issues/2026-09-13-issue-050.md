# The Daily Compass #050 — The visible hand

*Sunday, September 13, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Adam Smith's most famous image is a hand you cannot see: the market as a vast, leaderless machine that turns a million selfish decisions into an order no one designed and no one could. For the better part of forty years that idea reigned almost unchallenged — the presumption that the economy steers itself, that the steering wheel is a dangerous illusion, that the best thing the powerful can do is keep their hands off. This week that presumption looks quaintly dated from three directions at once. A frontier AI lab has published a formal model of the economy's entire future, the better to anticipate and shape it. A sweeping new federal law is re-engineering the American housing market provision by provision. And a serious argument is circulating that the United States should deliberately use its energy position to break OPEC while the cartel is weak. Everywhere, the hand is becoming visible again — states and labs and strategists reaching for controls the last generation was taught not to touch. This issue is about that return, and about the eighty-year-old warning that still stands over all of it: that the knowledge you would need to steer an economy well is precisely the knowledge no steerer can ever have.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The lab models its own endgame

**What happened.** In one of the more remarkable documents of the year, Anthropic's economics team — led by Anton Korinek and the Stanford growth economist Chad Jones — has published ["Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI,"](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios) a formal attempt to model what the company's own product does to the macro-economy. The framework runs three scenarios. In the "modest" case, AI adds less than half a point to GDP growth by 2030. In the "substantial" case (the median expert guess), GDP rises about 8% by 2030 while cognitive employment falls ~4%. In the "extreme" case, annual GDP growth hits 15% — a boom without historical precedent — even as the *labor share of income falls from 60% to 45%* and nearly one in five cognitive workers is unemployed. And the paper's throughline, true across all three: AI systematically redistributes income away from labor and toward the owners of capital.

**Why it matters.** Set aside the specific numbers, which are scenarios, not forecasts. The striking thing is the *gesture*: a company is modeling the economic future of the whole society the way a central planner once dreamed of doing, and putting a distributional question — who gets the income when machines do the thinking — at the dead center. That question is the oldest in political economy, and it has been politically dormant for a generation of "a rising tide lifts all boats" optimism. The Anthropic paper drags it back into daylight with the authority of growth theory: the tide may rise spectacularly, and a shrinking share of the boats may be yours. This is the visible hand in its purest modern form — not a state but a lab, reaching not for a lever but for a model, trying to see and shape an outcome the market is supposed to settle on its own. Whether you trust the model or not, the impulse behind it — *we can and must anticipate the whole thing* — is the defining impulse of the moment.

*Source: Korinek, Jones et al., Anthropic (Sep 9, 2026). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The state re-engineers a market

**What happened.** While the lab models, the state legislates. Brian Potter, whose *Construction Physics* is the best close-reading of how building actually works, has been conducting a provision-by-provision autopsy of the [21st Century ROAD to Housing Act](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-will-the-21st-century-road-to-21c) — the largest federal housing bill in decades, running to dozens of separate provisions. It is a catalogue of deliberate market engineering: legalizing single-stair apartment buildings, slashing the environmental-review red tape that strangles construction, deregulating manufactured homes, sharpening incentives for small-dollar mortgages, and forcing large institutional investors out of single-family ownership. Potter's discipline is the point — he refuses to grade the law by its ambitions, asking of each provision, narrowly and concretely, whether it will actually put more roofs over more heads, or merely look like it.

**Why it matters.** Housing is where the abstract argument about markets and planning becomes a place to live or not. For years the debate was ideological trench warfare — markets versus regulation, YIMBY versus NIMBY — and here instead is the state doing something unfashionably specific: reaching into a market that has plainly failed to clear and re-wiring its rules, one mechanism at a time. The ROAD Act is the visible hand at its most legitimate and its most testable, because supply is a number you can count afterward. It is also the quiet rebuke to both extremes — to the purist who says *never touch it* (the market has produced a generational shortage) and to the grandstander who says *just fix it* (most of these provisions will do far less than their titles promise). The lesson Potter keeps pressing, and it is the issue's lesson, is that steering an economy is not one decision but a thousand small ones, and that most of them are duller, and weaker, than the people pulling the levers want to believe.

*Source: Potter, Construction Physics (Sep 3, 2026). ~13 min.*

### ◆ 3. Breaking the cartel while it's down

**What happened.** The third hand reaches for an adversary. In *The New Atlantis*, Robert Zubrin argues the United States should seize its moment of energy abundance to deliberately [break OPEC](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/why-and-how-we-should-break-opec-now) — to use American production to shatter the cartel's power to set the world oil price, slashing transport costs, insulating the economy from oil shocks, and starving petro-authoritarian regimes of their rents. The timing is uncanny, because the cartel is cracking on its own: the UAE announced in April that it would leave OPEC and OPEC+ entirely, ending a membership dating to 1967; Saudi Aramco has been pricing its flagship crude for Asian buyers at its widest discount in years; and, as [Semafor reported](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/03/2026/opecs-new-threat-comes-from-within) this month, OPEC's real problem is no longer too much oil but too little will among its members to manage it together. Zubrin has ridden this hobby-horse for twenty years; the news has finally ridden up beside him.

**Why it matters.** Here the visible hand turns outward and frankly strategic — not correcting a market but weaponizing a position within one, using abundance as a lever to dismantle a rival's pricing power. It is the most aggressive face of the week's theme, and the most double-edged. The same reasoning that says "use our strength to break their cartel" is the reasoning that built the cartel in the first place; economic power deliberately wielded is economic power that invites deliberate retaliation, and the line between breaking a cartel and simply becoming the new one is thinner than its advocates admit. But the deeper point for this issue is that even *markets for the most fungible commodity on earth* turn out to be arenas of conscious statecraft, not spontaneous order — that behind the serene price of a barrel sit a hundred very visible hands, pushing. The age of pretending the economy runs itself is over on every front, including the one Adam Smith would have thought most purely a market of all.

*Source: Zubrin, The New Atlantis (No. 85, Summer 2026); Semafor (Sep 3). ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Use of Knowledge in Society** — F. A. Hayek, *American Economic Review*, September 1945. Eighty-one years old this month, and the ghost presiding over every brief above. Hayek's argument was not the crude one that planning is inefficient; it was deeper and epistemic. The knowledge an economy runs on, he wrote, "never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess" — the shopkeeper's feel for his street, the trader's nose, ten thousand local truths that are tacit, fleeting, and impossible to ship to a central office before they go stale. The price system works, for Hayek, precisely because it coordinates all that without anyone needing to *hold* it. Why now: the visible hand is back in fashion on the bet that we can finally gather and compute what Hayek said couldn't be — the state through statute, the lab through a model, and, increasingly, the claim that a large enough AI could at last perform the central calculation he declared impossible. Before cheering or fearing any of it, read the strongest case for why the steering wheel was left untouched for a reason. ([Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html), 1945)

**🃏 WILDCARD: in praise of the wide mind** — a lovely [Aeon essay](https://aeon.co/essays/four-islamic-polymaths-prove-breadth-beats-specialisation) makes the case against hyper-specialization through four giants of the Islamic golden age — al-Biruni, Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), Ibn Sina (Avicenna), and al-Khwarizmi — who moved freely between mathematics, medicine, astronomy, optics, philosophy and poetry, and produced work more durable for the roaming. A bracing counter-melody to an age that rewards the ever-narrower specialist (and now automates him): the deepest breakthroughs came from minds that refused to stay in their lane. For a reader in Beirut especially, a reminder that this intellectual inheritance is not a museum — it's a method. (Sep 2026)

**The impoverishment beneath the boom** — a quietly devastating [Noema essay by Afra Wang](https://www.noemamag.com/a-new-kind-of-creative-poverty/) moves from Joan Didion through Baudelaire to ask what "creativity" becomes when a machine can generate plausible expression on demand. Her worry isn't a scarcity of output — output has never been cheaper — but the slow impoverishment of the *inner* conditions that make expression mean anything: the friction, the felt stakes, the private struggle that used to be the price of saying something true. The human counterpoint to brief 1's capital share: some of what AI redistributes isn't income, it's the difficulty that made the work worth doing. (Sep 9)

**How a subculture actually moves a number** — against a national fertility rate near 1.6, some Chabad and ultra-Orthodox communities sustain something closer to six children per family inside expensive cities, and a sharp [Works in Progress piece](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/chabad-life/) locates the reason less in theology than in *scaffolding*: childcare woven into daily life, physical spaces built around young families, the quiet default that nearly every adult is caring for a small child. Whatever one thinks of the end, it's the week's best case study in a Hayekian truth — you change behavior at scale not by decree but by re-engineering the dense local conditions people actually live inside. (Jul 21)

**Strong opinions, held wrong** — Cedric Chin has a bracing [corrective](https://commoncog.com/strong-opinions-weakly-held-is-bad/) to Silicon Valley's favorite epistemic slogan. "Strong opinions, weakly held," he argues, mostly produces loud conviction and social posturing; what expert decision-makers actually do, per Gary Klein's research on how professionals really think, is commit to a reading of a situation while holding explicit, *named* expectations whose violation is the trigger to change their minds. The difference between sounding open-minded and being correctable turns out to be a matter of machinery, not attitude. (2026 update)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

**The Gray Area — Ian Bogost on "The Small Stuff"** (Aug 3, 2026)
The Atlantic writer and game scholar argues that the relentless optimization of daily life — the dematerializing of stick shifts, door keys, paper tickets, the friction we've engineered away in the name of convenience — has quietly severed us from the sensory texture of being alive. **Queue it.** It's the human-scale mirror of this issue: every visible hand that smooths a process also removes a friction someone was living inside, and Bogost is the rare critic who makes you feel the cost of the frictionless world rather than just argue it. ([The Gray Area](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-gray-area-with-sean-illing/id1081584611))

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The reflex to grab the wheel is not, in itself, a vice. Some coordination problems genuinely require a hand: a housing market that has failed to build for a generation, a cartel rigging the price of the thing that moves the world, a technological shift whose distributional violence no invisible process will gently smooth. Hayek is sometimes read as a libertarian talisman against ever acting, and that reading is too cheap; his essay is not a command to do nothing but a warning about a specific and permanent kind of blindness. The planner, the legislator, the lab, the strategist — each reaches for the controls armed with real knowledge, and each is missing the same thing Hayek named: the dispersed, tacit, local, perishable knowledge that lives only in the millions of people who will actually inhabit the outcome, and that no model, statute, or frontier system has ever successfully gathered in time. The mark of wisdom in an age of visible hands is therefore not the courage to steer — that part is easy, and everywhere — but the humility to know how much of the road you cannot see, and to build that blindness into the plan. Anthropic's own paper, to its credit, does exactly this: it offers scenarios, not a prophecy, three futures held open rather than one future declared. That is the right posture for all of it. Reach for the wheel when you must. But keep both hands light, watch for the thing you were sure you'd accounted for and didn't, and never mistake the clarity of your model for the opacity of the world it is trying to move. The hand is visible again. The road, as ever, is not.
