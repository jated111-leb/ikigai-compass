# The Daily Compass #068 — Not too hot, not too cold

*Wednesday, October 7, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On Sunday in New Delhi, India's foreign minister, S. Jaishankar, offered the most compact statement of a strategy that much of the world now follows. Speaking to a Munich Security Conference gathering on emerging and middle powers, he said that what countries like India want from the rivalry between the United States and China is a ["Goldilocks scenario"](https://www.aninews.in/news/world/asia/you-don8217t-want-it-too-hottoo-good-jaishankar-calls-for-a-goldilocks-scenario-to-navigate-us-china-rivalry20261004173247/): not too hot, not too cozy, somewhere at the right temperature. He also said the United States has "deliberately become a lonelier power." From India to Brazil, Turkey to Indonesia, and especially across the Gulf, the ambition of the moment is to avoid choosing: to buy from one great power, sell to the other, take security from whoever offers it and keep every door open. This issue asks whether that ambition is wisdom or wishful thinking. It sets the sharpest case against hedging beside the best case for it, then looks at the region where the strategy is being tested hardest, and in real time.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The case against: hedging is a fair-weather strategy

**What happened.** In January, Canada's prime minister, Mark Carney, told the World Economic Forum in Davos that states caught between Washington and Beijing had to stop negotiating alone: "If we're not at the table, we're on the menu." The speech set off a season of talk about a "middle-power moment." The political scientist Michael Beckley answered it in [*Foreign Affairs*](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/middle-power-delusion) with an essay whose title gives away the argument, "The Middle Power Delusion," and whose subtitle is blunter still: "Not Choosing Is Not an Option." Middle powers look more prominent today, he argues, not because they are stronger but because they are more exposed. For three decades they enjoyed unusual conditions: shelter under American hegemony, a fast-growing world economy, and the ability to trade freely with rival powers without picking sides. Those conditions are disappearing. Growth has slowed, globalization has turned into a contest over chokepoints, and both great powers now seek control over finance, technology, critical minerals, energy, shipping and data. In that world, Beckley argues, keeping options open does not protect a middle power. It exposes it, because any coalition big enough to matter is visible enough to be punished. His advice is to choose deliberately: decide which great power poses the greater threat to your security, prosperity and autonomy, then build national strength and use it to bargain from inside that camp.

**Why it matters.** Beckley's argument is about timing as much as strategy. Hedging was cheap when the great powers were mostly cooperating and the price of friendship with both was low. As rivalry intensifies, each side increasingly treats ambiguity as a choice against it. Hal Brands of Johns Hopkins makes a related point in the current [*Foreign Policy*](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/09/09/hal-brands-rules-based-order-great-power-politics-geopolitics/), arguing that in a world where rules are in retreat, self-help matters more, and that a grand coalition of middle powers is unlikely to hold the line by itself, because countries such as Brazil, India and Saudi Arabia see as much opportunity as danger in a fragmented order. Read together, the two essays issue a warning to any state, or company, that has grown used to straddling. Strategies that work in calm weather can fail precisely when they are needed. Beckley writes from an American vantage point, and his prescription leans toward alignment with Washington. But the question he poses has no national bias: what is your plan for the day both sides demand an answer?

*Source: Beckley, Foreign Affairs (May 2026), ~20 min; Brands, Foreign Policy, Fall 2026 issue (Sep 9, 2026), ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. The case for: great powers court the pivotal

**What happened.** The academic literature offers a sharper and more conditional answer. In ["Better Safe Than Sorry,"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/review-of-international-studies/article/better-safe-than-sorry-why-great-powers-accommodate-highvalue-hedgers/FE4550A8910E862B2BD03CF3C88B3791) published in the *Review of International Studies* (in print this year), Luis Simón and Fabio Figiaconi of the Vrije Universiteit Brussel ask when hedging survives great-power pressure. Their answer turns on what they call "high-value hedgers": secondary states whose location or capabilities could tip the balance in a war between the great powers. Rival great powers, they argue, tend to accommodate such states rather than pressure them to choose, because the chance and cost of pushing them into the other camp are too high. Better to be safe than sorry. They test the idea on two cases a century apart. Before the First World War, Britain and Germany both chose to accommodate the Netherlands' hedging rather than force its hand. Today, the authors argue, the United States and China are doing the same with Singapore, whose position on one of the world's busiest shipping lanes makes it too valuable for either to push away.

**Why it matters.** This is the necessary corrective to Beckley. Hedging is not one strategy that either works or fails. It is a bet whose odds depend on what you hold. A country that controls a strait, a port, a pipeline route, a critical mineral or a pool of capital that both sides need can sustain ambiguity far longer than one that holds nothing either side wants. The practical lesson follows: the precondition for a durable hedge is to become indispensable, and to make that value hard to substitute. That applies with unusual force to a region like the Gulf and Iraq, where location and energy have made states valuable to every great power, and where the question is whether that value still protects them when the shooting starts. The paper's limits are worth noting too. It deals mainly with small states and two cases, and it says more about why great powers hold back than about what happens when they stop.

*Source: Simón & Figiaconi, Review of International Studies 52(5) (2026), first published online 2024. ~40 min; the BISA blog has a short summary.*

### ◆ 3. The test case: hedging under fire

**What happened.** The Gulf is where these arguments are being tested hardest. Writing in [*Foreign Policy*](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/09/25/gulf-states-iran-trump-oil-energy-hormuz/) under the headline "Gulf States Are Hedging and Suffering," Shawn A. Howard, a former CIA analyst who spent more than two decades on the Middle East, argues that the war with Iran has caught the six members of the Gulf Cooperation Council in an acute paradox. They still need American protection against Iranian strikes, but that protection has shown its limits and has also made them targets. So they are doing everything at once. They keep cooperating militarily with the United States, they are diversifying their regional security relationships, they are pursuing diplomacy, and they are talking directly to Tehran. Howard goes through the approaches of Saudi Arabia, Oman, the UAE, Qatar and Kuwait in turn, and his verdict is stark: none of these strategies, alone or together, is insulating the region from the war's consequences.

**Why it matters.** Howard's essay is the empirical check on the first two briefs, and the result is uncomfortable for both. The Gulf states are high-value in exactly the sense Simón and Figiaconi describe: they sit on energy, shipping lanes and capital that every major power needs. That value has bought them attention and options, but not immunity, because in a war the most valuable territory is also the most exposed. At the same time, Beckley's simple remedy, choose a side, does not obviously help either. The states most closely aligned with Washington have not been spared. What the Gulf case suggests is that hedging is less a strategy for avoiding danger than a way of managing it: keeping several channels open so that some still work when others fail. For readers in the region, and for any business whose plans assume calm in the Gulf, that is a more sober and more useful framing than either "middle-power moment" or "middle-power delusion."

*Source: Howard, Foreign Policy (Sep 25, 2026). ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the island that asked to be neutral** — in 416 BC, in the middle of the Peloponnesian War, Athens landed troops on Melos, a small island in the Aegean that had tried to stay out of the conflict, and demanded its submission. Thucydides' reconstruction of the negotiation, the [Melian Dialogue](https://public.wsu.edu/~hughesc/melian_dialogue.htm), contains the most quoted sentence in the study of international politics: "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians asked to be friends with Athens while allied to neither side. The Athenians refused, reasoning that tolerating a neutral would look like weakness to their own subjects. Melos held out. When it fell, Athens killed the men of military age, enslaved the women and children, and resettled the island with colonists. Twenty-four centuries later, it remains the founding text of the case against neutrality, and a reminder that what decided Melos's fate was not its argument but its irrelevance to Athens' rivals. Value, as brief 2 suggests, is what makes a hedge survivable. (Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book 5)

**🃏 WILDCARD: a script in a cave** — scholars have long debated whether Teotihuacan, the great city of ancient central Mexico, had true writing or just a rich set of symbols. A study in [*Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/painted-teotihuacan-glyphs-of-cave-19-chalcatzingo-morelos-mexico/536A5E711BB8DFED14BE189F4F1B9259) led by Christophe Helmke of the University of Copenhagen re-examined three faded red glyphs in a small cave at Chalcatzingo, far from the city itself, first recorded in the 1980s. Using image processing, the team identified a place name that also appears in Teotihuacan murals and a possible warrior title, painted during rituals in roughly the fourth to sixth centuries CE. They argue it adds to growing evidence of a genuine script, with a known repertoire now approaching 120 signs. A provincial outpost, it turns out, wrote in the great power's language. (Antiquity, Sep 2026)

**Breakout capacity, not breakout** — the one AI item this week turns hedging into a technology doctrine. In a new Carnegie paper, ["AI Breakout Capacity,"](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/artificial-intelligence-ai-development-breakout-germany-india-japan-uk-middle-power) Anton Leicht and Sam Winter-Levy argue that AI middle powers such as Germany, India, Japan and the UK cannot afford to race the United States and China to build frontier models, but should not accept total dependence either. Their model is nuclear latency: Japan has no nuclear weapon, but could build one quickly. Middle powers, they argue, should keep the ability to sprint to the frontier if they are ever cut off, by onshoring compute, securing chip-purchasing rights, keeping pre-training expertise alive and creating the legal and corporate vehicles for a rapid build. Their summary: build the capacity for a frontier sprint while hoping never to use it. It is the Goldilocks hedge applied to the most strategic technology of the age. (Carnegie, Sep 28, 2026)

**The dollar's slow drift** — for all the talk of de-dollarization, the IMF's latest data on the currency composition of central banks' reserves, [released Sep 30](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/economics/data/7977210/dollars-share-of-foreign-reserves-fell-slightly-in-q2-imf), shows the dollar's share at 56.7% at the end of June, down only modestly from 57.2% at the end of March. The shift is real but slow, and the renminbi's share remains small. Hedging in finance, as in geopolitics, tends to happen at the margin rather than in a single break. (IMF COFER, Sep 30, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1940, with France defeated and occupied, the philosopher Simone Weil wrote an essay on Homer that she published under a pseudonym to slip past the censors. ["The Iliad, or the Poem of Force"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Iliad_or_the_Poem_of_Force) argues that the true hero of the *Iliad* is force itself, the power that turns people into things, and that it degrades not only those who suffer it but those who wield it. No one in the poem possesses force for long; it passes from hand to hand, and the victor of one book is the corpse of another. Her essay ends with a sentence worth carrying into any discussion of power politics: "Only he who has measured the dominion of force, and knows how not to respect it, is capable of love and justice." Read beside the Melian Dialogue, Weil offers something the realists leave out. The Athenians were right that the strong do what they can. They were wrong to think that strength was a stable possession; within a few years their expedition to Sicily had ended in catastrophe. Today's briefs are about how to survive among giants: whether to choose, when to hedge, how to become too valuable to discard. Those are the right questions for any state, or any person, caught between larger forces. Weil's addition is that survival is not the whole of it. The middle position has its own dignity, which is the ability to see force clearly without worshipping it, and to remember that the giants, too, are temporary.
