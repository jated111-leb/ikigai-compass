# The Daily Compass #034 — No one at the wheel

*Thursday, August 20, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

We are bracing, collectively, for AI to take the wheel of our lives — and spending almost all our fear on the one wheel our hands are still on. The genuinely driverless things are elsewhere, and quieter. This issue is about who is actually steering. An AI agent can now hold assets and run a business, and when one does harm there may be no one the law can find to answer for it. Two wars grind on with no actor able to end them, having quietly stopped trying to win. And your job — the runaway everyone is watching for — turns out, on the data, to be the place humans still steer best. Goethe wrote the whole predicament into a poem in 1797: the apprentice who enchants a broom to haul his water and then cannot, for the life of him, make it stop. *The spirits I summoned,* he wails, *I now cannot rid myself of.* We keep summoning brooms. The only question that matters is which ones we built an off-switch for.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The agent with no address

**What happened.** As AI agents begin to hold assets and run businesses — Argentina's Milei has floated letting them own and operate corporations, while several US states move to bar AI "personhood" — Tyler Cowen and Sonia Farrell Pearson argue the whole personhood debate is a distraction: ["Capitalizing Untethered AI Agents."](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/capitalizing-untethered-ai-agents.html) Legal personhood, they write, is neither necessary nor sufficient. The real question is enforcement. For two thousand years, nonhuman legal persons — corporations, estates, ships — have acted in law through human officeholders the law could locate, summon, prosecute, and, if need be, replace. Agentic AI breaks that chain: it exercises practical agency with no locatable human principal behind it.

**Why it matters.** The entire architecture of accountability quietly assumes you can find the responsible party. Personhood was never the mechanism that made a corporation answerable — *findability* was; there was always a person to serve the subpoena on. An agent that transacts, persists, and adapts to you (test-time training, last issue) but has no address, no officer, no one to summon isn't a legal person. It's a legal vacuum, and the law has never had a working move against a defendant it cannot serve. The question of the decade isn't the philosophical one everyone reaches for — *are the agents people?* It's the procedural one nobody wants: when one of them does real harm, whose door does the sheriff knock on? Right now, honestly, the answer is nobody's. That's not a science-fiction problem. It's a jurisdiction problem, and it's already here.

*Source: Cowen & Pearson, Marginal Revolution (Aug 19). ~10 min.*

### ◆ 2. The war that runs itself

**What happened.** In War on the Rocks, Alex Kolbin argues that two wars which look nothing alike operationally — Ukraine, a mass ground invasion; Iran, something far more limited — are converging on the same political logic: ["Managed Instability."](https://warontherocks.com/managed-instability-why-the-wars-against-iran-and-ukraine-can-go-on-and-on-and-on/) Once the hope of a quick, decisive victory dies, the belligerents stop trying to *win* and settle instead into a self-sustaining, indefinite, low-to-mid-intensity conflict that resolves nothing and ends never — because no actor has both the power and the will to force a conclusion.

**Why it matters.** This is brief 1's problem wearing camouflage: a system running with no one able to stop it — not for lack of a person, but for lack of a *decisive* one. The twentieth-century grammar of war (declaration, battle, victory, treaty — a clean beginning and end, with signatories who could be held to it) is giving way to war as a chronic condition, managed like a disease rather than settled like a dispute. It rhymes with two threads this newsletter keeps pulling: issue #023's paradox that cheaper, less-lethal war lowers the bar to *start* one, and issue #029's larger point about drift — that the absence of a clean ending isn't a pause on the road to resolution, but increasingly the destination itself. Wars used to be events, with an author and an armistice. Some are now climates, with neither.

*Source: Kolbin, War on the Rocks (Aug 19). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. The panic that misfires

**What happened.** Against those two genuinely driverless systems, here is the one everyone is actually panicking about — your career — and the data says the alarm is pointed at the wrong target. A new working paper (via [Cowen](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/declining-occupations-and-career-outcomes-in-the-united-states.html)) links the 2000 Census to administrative earnings through 2020 across 2.4 million workers: starting your career in an occupation that later contracts by 25% or more costs roughly 5% in cumulative lifetime earnings — real, but far milder than the catastrophe the discourse implies — while mobility turns out to be substantial, and workers' children's outcomes are barely tied to the parent's occupational decline at all.

**Why it matters.** It's the counterweight the issue needs, and it carries the sharper point. We funnel our control-anxiety into the one system where humans demonstrably keep the wheel — a career is a thing a person can see coming, adapt to, and exit, and the evidence is that they do, at a modest and survivable cost. Meanwhile the truly driverless things (an agent no one can serve, a war no one can end) glide past under-alarmed, because they don't have a face to fear. Human adaptability is real and chronically underrated; the AI-jobs apocalypse keeps being forecast and keeps arriving as a manageable reroute. The danger was never the runaway we're all bracing for. It's the ones we've stopped watching precisely because we can't picture whom to blame.

*Source: via Cowen, Marginal Revolution (Aug 19). ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — the sharpest idea in this week's AI cycle is a contrarian one from Jack Clark's [Import AI 469](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-469-science-ai-rsi-simulator): that Zuckerberg's "distribute superintelligence to everyone" manifesto (issue #025) is best read not as optimism but as *technological pessimism* — a vision that treats AI as a tool for empowering individuals while flinching from what genuinely invention-capable AI would actually mean. A lab insider inverting the whole manifesto debate. (Zvi's next weekly and any framework movement stayed quiet.)

**Data scarcity** — Byrne Hobart on the constraint least discussed in the trillion-dollar buildout: the frontier is running out of high-quality training data, and scarcity changes the economics of everything downstream. If compute is the engine and capital the fuel, data is the road — and the road, it turns out, is finite. ([The Diff](https://www.thediff.co/archive/data-scarcity/), Aug 18; likely paywalled)

**The last unmanned mile** — a War on the Rocks piece on "contested logistics": the US military can no longer assume it can quietly resupply a dispersed force in a Pacific fight, and must learn to sustain troops under fire and degraded comms. The unglamorous constraint that has decided more campaigns than any battle — and the operational underside of brief 2's forever-wars. ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/cogs-of-war/contested-logistics-and-the-last-unmanned-mile/), Aug 19)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Sorcerer's Apprentice** — Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, 1797. The apprentice, left alone, enchants a broomstick to haul water so he needn't — and then, having never learned the word that stops it, watches it fetch and fetch and flood the house, splitting into two brooms when he takes an axe to it. His cry is the whole of this issue: *the spirits that I summoned, I now cannot rid myself of.* Why now: brief 1's ownerless agent and brief 2's endless war are both enchanted brooms — processes we knew how to start and never built the spell to stop. Goethe's real subject wasn't magic; it was the specific, recurring human failure to install the off-switch before the demonstration. ([Univ. of Pittsburgh / Ashliman](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0325ast.html), 1797)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the discipline of the off-switch** — Quanta on the painstaking art of quantum error correction: building a reliable computer out of physically fragile qubits by wrapping the delicate thing in layers of engineered control that catch and fix its errors faster than they accumulate. It's the exact opposite of an enchanted broom — the slow, unglamorous work of making a runaway-prone system steerable *before* you let it run. The most important engineering of the age may be the least cinematic: putting the brakes in first. ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/building-a-quantum-computer-one-fragile-qubit-at-a-time-20260819/), Aug 19)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The reassuring thread and the frightening one are the same thread, pulled in opposite directions. Where a human is genuinely in the loop — choosing a career, rerouting when a field contracts, adapting at a survivable cost — we steer far better than we fear; the occupations data is quietly good news about ourselves. The danger lives exactly where we removed ourselves from the loop on purpose: the agent we set up to act without us, the war we let slide past the last exit, the process we launched without ever writing the line that halts it. Goethe's apprentice wasn't undone by ambition; plenty of masters enchant plenty of brooms. He was undone by sequence — he built the fetching before he learned the stopping, and by the time the water was at his knees it was too late to go looking for the word. The quiet lesson under a loud week is the one the quantum engineers keep relearning by hand: the off-switch is not a feature you add at the end. It is the first thing you build, or it is the thing you never had. Summon carefully. And learn the second spell before you cast the first.
