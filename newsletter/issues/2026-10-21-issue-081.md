# The Daily Compass #081 — The right size

*Wednesday, October 21, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1926 the British biologist J.B.S. Haldane published an essay called "On Being the Right Size", which contains one of the most memorable images in science writing. "You can drop a mouse down a thousand-yard mine shaft; and, on arriving at the bottom, it gets a slight shock and walks away, provided that the ground is fairly soft. A rat is killed, a man is broken, a horse splashes." The reason is geometry. When a creature doubles in length, its surface area grows four times but its weight grows eight times, so air resistance protects a mouse but not a horse. Size, Haldane showed, is not a detail. Every animal's shape, skeleton and way of life is dictated by how big it is, and a giant built like a small creature would collapse under its own weight. This issue applies his question to human things: whether big teams or small ones produce more original ideas, why companies that rush to grow often break, and whether AI gains as much from being made bigger as people gain from being given more time.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Do big teams kill originality?

**What happened.** In 2019 a widely cited paper in *Nature* by Lingfei Wu, Dashun Wang and James Evans, "Large teams develop and small teams disrupt science and technology", analysed tens of millions of papers, patents and software projects and reached a striking conclusion. Small teams were more likely to produce work that disrupted a field and sent it in new directions; large teams tended to refine and develop existing ideas. The finding became a staple of management advice: if you want breakthroughs, keep teams small. A new preprint challenges it directly. In ["Large scientific teams are more, not less, disruptive"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07329) (arXiv, Oct 5, 2026), Junming Huang and Yu Xie argue that the earlier result was largely a measurement artefact. The standard "disruption index" falls mechanically as a paper's reference list gets longer, and larger teams tend to cite more references. Once the authors correct for this, and use a new measure they validate against recognised breakthroughs, including Nobel-winning work, disruption rises with team size in all four fields they study and in every decade from the 1970s to the 2010s.

**Why it matters.** The paper is a preprint and not the last word; other researchers have argued that the small-team advantage survives such corrections, so this is a live debate rather than a settled reversal. But it carries two useful lessons whichever side turns out to be right. The first is about size: the intuition that bigness smothers originality is less certain than it seemed, and large, well-organised teams may be able to combine more ideas than small ones. The second is about measurement: a single choice in how a metric is built can create an apparent effect of size that is not really there. Before reorganising a company around a finding about team size, it is worth asking what exactly was measured, and whether the measure itself depends on size.

*Source: Huang & Xie, arXiv 2610.07329 (Oct 2026); Wu, Wang & Evans, Nature 566 (2019). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Measure twice, scale once

**What happened.** Startup culture celebrates speed: raise money, hire fast, grab the market before anyone else. A study by Ron Tidhar, Benjamin Hallen and Kathleen Eisenhardt, ["Measure Twice, Cut Once: Unit Profitability, Scalability, and the Exceptional Growth of New Firms"](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2021.15970) in *Organization Science*, suggests the companies that grow largest often do the opposite. The authors compared matched pairs of promising young ventures in new markets, following them over several years. The firms that achieved exceptional growth went through a distinct sequence. First they focused on making each unit of their business profitable, not on growing; then they learned broadly about their market; and then they actively delayed growth until the model was sound. Only after that did they shift to growing profitably and building the capabilities to support it. Their peers that focused on growth from the start, the authors write, "ironically fail to achieve it". The paper offers a precise definition of premature scaling and introduces the idea of "product-market-profit fit".

**Why it matters.** Haldane's point applies to companies too: scaling up does not simply make a firm bigger, it magnifies whatever is already there. A business that loses a little money on each customer loses a lot more at ten times the size, and the systems, hiring and culture built to support a flawed model become harder to change as they grow. The sample is small and the study is qualitative, so its lessons are a guide rather than a law. But the sequence, profit per unit first, learning second, growth third, is a useful discipline for any company tempted to treat growth as proof of success. A related OECD study of about 190,000 innovative start-ups this year found that European firms take somewhat longer than American ones to reach billion-dollar valuations, and that American scale-ups can grow within a large home market while European ones lean more on cross-border deals and foreign investors. The size of the market shapes the path to size.

*Source: Tidhar, Hallen & Eisenhardt, Organization Science 36(1) (2025); OECD STI Working Paper 2026/08. ~40 min.*

### ◆ 3. Does AI scale like we do?

**What happened.** The AI industry has been built on scaling: bigger models, more data and more computing power produce better results. Recently, much of the progress has come from a new kind of scaling, letting models "think" longer by generating more reasoning before they answer. The philosopher Toby Ord of Oxford, who has been writing a series of essays on the economics of AI scaling, asks a simple question about it in ["AI Scaling vs Human Scaling"](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/g6sdhN39so8rWBuYk/ai-scaling-vs-human-scaling) (October 2026): does an AI system gain as much from extra thinking as a person gains from extra working time? Drawing on data from METR, a research group that compares AI agents and human experts on research-engineering tasks, he finds that people have so far improved considerably more with each doubling of time than AI systems have with each doubling of computation. He is careful about the limits of the data: no human in the study worked longer than eight hours, so the human curve beyond that point reflects several people working in parallel rather than one person working longer. Newer models trained for long chains of reasoning may be narrowing the gap, and on his reading AI appears to be catching up.

**Why it matters.** Ord's question reframes the debate about scaling. The issue is not only whether AI keeps improving as it gets more resources, but how steeply: a system that needs vastly more computation to achieve what a person achieves with a little more time is powerful but expensive, and that cost shapes which tasks it makes sense to automate. It also echoes Haldane. Different kinds of system scale in different ways, and simply making something bigger does not guarantee it gets proportionally better. For companies deciding where to deploy AI, the practical lesson is to watch the shape of the curve, the cost of each additional step of improvement, rather than assuming that more computation will always buy a corresponding gain.

*Source: Ord, "AI Scaling vs Human Scaling" (Oct 2026); METR data. ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: more admirals than ships** — on 19 November 1955 *The Economist* published an anonymous comic essay by the historian C. Northcote Parkinson that began: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." His evidence came from the Royal Navy. Between 1914 and 1928, the number of capital ships in commission [fell from 62 to 20](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1994/december-1/challenging-parkinsons-law), while the number of officials at the Admiralty rose from 2,000 to 3,569, an increase of more than 78%. Administration, Parkinson argued, grows by its own internal logic, because officials want to multiply subordinates rather than rivals and then create work for one another. It was satire, but it became one of the most quoted laws of organisational life, and a reminder that a bureaucracy can grow while the thing it manages shrinks. (The Economist, 1955)

**🃏 WILDCARD: how the smallest flower got so small** — duckweeds, which float on ponds and include the world's smallest flowering plants, are only a few millimetres across. A study published on October 7 in *Current Biology* by a University of Nottingham team led by Anthony Bishopp used gene-expression analysis and advanced microscopy to find out how they shrank. Surprisingly, duckweeds have not thrown away the parts of a normal plant. They keep the same basic building blocks as a larger plant's shoot, [compressed and fused together](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/news/scientists-uncover-how-the-worlds-smallest-flowering-plant-became-so-tiny) into a tiny body. Miniaturised animals usually shrink by losing organs; duckweed shrank by packing the whole blueprint into less space. (University of Nottingham / Current Biology, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

William Blake, writing around 1803 in a poem not published until decades after his death, offered the opposite of Haldane's lesson, or perhaps its completion. "To see a World in a Grain of Sand / And a Heaven in a Wild Flower / Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand / And Eternity in an hour." Haldane taught that size determines what a thing can be. Blake suggests that it does not determine what a thing can mean. A duckweed holds the whole design of a flowering plant in a few millimetres; a small team can carry a large idea; a single hour of real attention can matter more than a year of distraction. Growth is often necessary, and sometimes it is the right goal. But the work of being the right size begins with knowing what you are for, and that question has the same answer whether you are a mouse, a horse or a company of thousands.
