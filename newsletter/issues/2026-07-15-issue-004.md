# The Daily Compass #004 — What's actually scarce

*Wednesday, July 15, 2026 · ~18 minute read*

OpenAI answered Apple and we finally learned what the fight is over: a screenless companion in your living room. Meanwhile two very different essays converged on the same unsettling idea — that AI is making the once-scarce things (diligence, code, even follow-through) abundant, which quietly repoints the scarcity somewhere else: theory, judgment, retention. Today's issue is about spotting what's actually scarce.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Apple v. OpenAI, act three: the answer, and the object

**What happened.** Tuesday, OpenAI made its first substantive response to Apple's suit — "we're not aware of any evidence that this complaint has merit… we believe in fair competition and allowing people the freedom to work wherever they choose" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/14/openai-pushes-back-on-apple-trade-secret-lawsuit/)) — signaling it will fight on California's employee-mobility doctrine rather than settle. The same day, Bloomberg revealed what the contested hardware actually is: a **mobile, screenless smart speaker with moving mechanical elements**, built as an in-home AI companion with input from ex-iPhone and Mac engineers. And Ben Thompson's Tuesday Stratechery article ([paywalled; its own summary line](https://stratechery.com/2026/apple-sues-openai-apples-real-problem/)) frames the suit bluntly: "there is one guilty employee, but this mostly feels like lashing out."

**The excerpt** (Bloomberg's device description, via TechCrunch):

> "The device reportedly has no screen, includes moving mechanical elements, and is being built with input from several former Apple engineers who worked on the iPhone and Mac."

**Why it matters.** The suit now has an object, and the object explains the panic: a post-smartphone form factor built by Apple's own alumni, aimed at the ambient-computing turf Apple has repeatedly failed to claim. Thompson's "lashing out" read matches the deeper pattern — litigation is what a platform owner does when it fears becoming the commoditized layer (see today's ⏳ timeless pick for the 24-year-old theory of exactly this). Axios adds the practical stake: discovery and injunctions could pause OpenAI's device program during the year that decides the form factor. Caveats where due: the device description is single-outlet sourcing, and Thompson's full argument sits behind his paywall.

*Sources: TechCrunch (Jul 14); Bloomberg via TechCrunch (Jul 14); Stratechery (Jul 14). ~10 min.*

### ◆ 2. The tower keeps rising: nobody holds the theory anymore

**What happened.** Armin Ronacher published ["The Tower Keeps Rising"](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/7/13/the-tower-keeps-rising/) — a rereading of Babel as a story about coordination rather than pride. A team's real shared language is its common understanding of concepts, boundaries, and invariants, maintained partly by the friction of working together. Agents remove the friction; codebases "become Babel not because nobody can communicate, but because nobody needs to."

**The excerpt:**

> "At Babel, the loss of common language stops construction whereas in AI-assisted engineering, construction can continue after shared understanding has already collapsed. The lack of an immediate failure is what makes it curious and a bit disorienting. The tower does not fall, and so we do not notice what was lost. It just keeps rising."

**Why it matters.** This is Peter Naur's 1985 classic ["Programming as Theory Building"](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/Naur.pdf) stress-tested against 2026: a program is not its source code but the living *theory* held by the people who built it — and agents let us ship code no one holds a theory of, with no immediate failure to warn us. The career translation comes from Byrne Hobart's Tuesday Diff, ["AI as a Conscientiousness Prosthetic"](https://www.thediff.co/archive/ai-as-a-conscientiousness-prosthetic/) (paywalled; the title carries the thesis): if models supply the diligence and follow-through that used to differentiate careers, what's left scarce is exactly what Naur said — theory, judgment, and the agency to form your own goals. On that last piece, Henrik Karlsson's ["On agency"](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/agency) is the essay to read: "agency is almost gentle — an attunement to the world and the self… like the force of a river being pulled toward the sea."

*Sources: Ronacher (Jul 13); Naur (1985); Hobart, The Diff (Jul 14); Karlsson. ~30 min for the full thread.*

### ◆ 3. The AI colander: churn is structural, and buyers know it

**What happened.** Tomasz Tunguz published the retention data behind the AI business-model anxiety: [model-layer customer retention](https://tomtunguz.com/the-ai-colander) lands between high single digits and ~40%, versus ~90% for SaaS and ~80% for social networks. The frontier crown changes hands every ~41 days, and the price of any given level of intelligence falls roughly 10× per year.

**The excerpt:**

> "AI is like a colander. Customers pour in. Many pour out. … Unlike the instability of the Middle Ages, this war of the weights benefits the community. Startups build on whichever model wins this month & re-route next month. Customers gain negotiating leverage every 41 days."

**Why it matters.** This completes the "who pays for intelligence" thread from issue #002: Evans argued models trend toward commodity; Tunguz now shows the retention numbers that prove buyers are behaving accordingly — routing to whoever wins the month. It's why labs are buying institutional stickiness with $10B of forward-deployed engineers, and why Anthropic's Fable-access rationing (extended again through July 19, while OpenAI advertises abundance) is strategically risky: in a colander market, *access uncertainty is churn fuel*. The management corollary comes from First Round's altitude-shifting roundup: as agents absorb execution, the core leadership skill becomes knowing when to drop into the details — "I'm not a micromanager, but I'm microinterested" (Matt MacInnis) — whether the executors are people or models.

*Sources: Tunguz (Jul 13); First Round Review; Willison on Fable access (Jul 12). ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**Claude goes to school** — Anthropic launched free premium Claude for verified US K-12 teachers: standards-mapped teaching skills, FERPA terms, a Detroit Public Schools pilot with a pre-registered impact study — and agentic recurring tasks ("hand off a task once… and it runs every school day at 4pm. Claude works while you drive home"). The classroom is now a lab-vs-lab distribution battleground. ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-teachers), Jul 14)

**Silicon Valley has a science fiction problem** — Aeon's new essay on "reactionary futurism": tech billionaires strip-mining sci-fi's aesthetics while discarding its politics — Meta named for a satire, Palantir for devices of corruption. "The dystopia has not been avoided; it has been made comfortable enough to sign up for." (Ali Rıza Taşkale, [Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/silicon-valley-has-a-science-fiction-problem), Jul 14)

**Perfect machines, tolerated carnage** — a neurosurgeon on why frozen Waymos made news during San Francisco's blackout while the ~115 Americans killed that day by human drivers didn't: illusory superiority makes us benchmark machines against an idealized self. "That double standard allows people to die by slowing the adoption of things that would save them." (Jonathan Slotkin, [Noema](https://www.noemamag.com/why-we-demand-perfect-machines-yet-tolerate-human-carnage/), Jul 14)

**The 90-minute kill switch** — The Hill's reported piece on how Washington's interventions (Anthropic given 90 minutes to pull Mythos after a competitor's complaint; GPT-5.6 delayed under pressure) are driving enterprises toward open weights — with 80% of open-source developers worldwide reportedly building on Chinese models. The reporting behind Lambert's "6 months to live" thesis. (Miranda Nazzaro, [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5952253-trump-administration-ai-restrictions-opens/), ~Jul 5)

**Appropriations are the strategy** — the White House issued two sweeping AI directives in June, but the operations-and-maintenance accounts that actually buy command-level AI software were drained by the Iran war, and the $87.6B supplemental has no AI line. Grand strategy is free; funding is the strategy. (Disclosure: the author sells such software — an unusually transparent advocacy piece.) (Adam Lackey, [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/cogs-of-war/the-pentagons-ai-strategy-has-a-funding-problem/), Jul 9)

**Three ladders of leverage** — the clearest maturity model yet for AI-native product work: personal (AI assists → completes tasks), product (prototypes → agent-shipped PRs), systems (self-checking workflows). "At the top of the ladder, AI completes multi-step tasks and checks its own results." (Colin Matthews, [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-top-pms-increase-their-leverage))

**CRISPR takes aim at cancer's most-wanted target** — Doudna's lab built a Cas12a2 system that detects mutant-p53 RNA (mutated in ~half of all cancers) and switches into destruction mode, shredding the cancer cell's DNA. A genuinely novel angle on "something of a holy grail in cancer therapy." (McCarty & Dattani, [Works in Progress](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/whats-new-in-biology-july-2026), Jul 9)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Strategy Letter V** — Spolsky, 2002: "Smart companies try to commoditize their products' complements." IBM funding Linux, Microsoft commoditizing the PC — and now every move in this week's news: Apple suing when AI threatens to commoditize *it*, China open-weighting the model layer, labs racing to own the layer that doesn't commoditize. Twenty-four years old and it reads like this morning's briefing. (Joel Spolsky, [Joel on Software](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/06/12/strategy-letter-v/))

**🃏 WILDCARD: Five graphs on heatwaves** — from 3QD's Tuesday curation: a data walk through how heatwaves are getting longer, hotter, and more frequent, anchored to Europe's brutal 2026 run — record May, hottest-ever June, and a third wave developing now.
