# The Daily Compass #109 — A good name

*Wednesday, November 18, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On the morning of April 18, 1906, an earthquake struck San Francisco, and the fires that followed burned for days and destroyed much of the city. Insurers faced a ruinous bill, and many of them looked for ways out. Fire policies often excluded earthquake damage, so companies argued over whether each building had been destroyed by the shaking or by the flames, and some settled for a fraction of what they owed. In London, a leading Lloyd's underwriter named Cuthbert Heath took the opposite view. He cabled his agent in San Francisco with an instruction that has become part of the market's folklore: ["Pay all of our policyholders in full, irrespective of the terms of their policies."](https://www.lloyds.com/about-lloyds/history/catastrophes-and-claims/san-francisco-earthquake) By Lloyd's own account, the earthquake ended up costing it more than $50 million. It also made Lloyd's name in America: a market that paid when it did not strictly have to was a market worth buying from, and the United States went on to become one of its largest sources of business. Heath had spent a fortune to buy something that could not be bought any other way. This issue is about reputation: what it is worth, how leaders win and lose it, and what AI is doing to the signals we use to judge who to trust.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The reputation bond

**What happened.** Why should a firm keep its promises when cutting corners would be cheaper? Economists answered the question in 1981. In ["The Role of Market Forces in Assuring Contractual Performance"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:89:y:1981:i:4:p:615-41) (*Journal of Political Economy*), Benjamin Klein and Keith Leffler showed that a price premium can act as a bond. If a firm earns more than its costs on each sale, it has something to lose by cheating: the stream of future premiums it would forfeit once customers stopped buying. Carl Shapiro ([*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 1983](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/premiums-high-quality-products-rents-reputation/wp043.pdf)) added that the premium also repays the investment it took to build the reputation in the first place. Online marketplaces later made it possible to measure that premium. In a randomised field experiment on eBay, Paul Resnick and colleagues sold matched lots of vintage postcards under an established seller's identity and under new ones; buyers paid [8.1% more](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/abs/value-of-reputation-on-ebay-a-controlled-experiment/610EA11BA19583907061AFB677BE4338) to the established seller (*Experimental Economics*, 2006). Losing it is costly. Luís Cabral and Ali Hortaçsu found that after an eBay seller received its first negative rating, weekly sales growth fell from about +5% to about −8% ([*Journal of Industrial Economics*, 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jindec:v:58:y:2010:i:1:p:54-78)). Michael Luca found that a one-star increase in a restaurant's Yelp rating was associated with a [5–9% rise in revenue](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hbs:wpaper:12-016) for independent restaurants, but not for chains, whose brand already carried the reputation. Where reputation pays, people fake it: Luca and Georgios Zervas found that Yelp's filter flagged about 16% of Boston restaurant reviews as suspicious, and that restaurants with weak reputations or more competition were [more likely to commit review fraud](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/22836596) (*Management Science*, 2016). A reputation can also be destroyed in a single sentence. On April 23, 1991, the jewellery retailer Gerald Ratner told an audience of business leaders that one of his firm's products was cheap ["because it's total crap"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Ratner). Customers stayed away, the company's value collapsed, and within two years Ratner was gone and the group had changed its name.

**Why it matters.** A reputation is not a feeling; it is an asset with a measurable value, and like any asset it can be built, borrowed, faked and destroyed. The Klein–Leffler logic has a practical edge. If your business depends on customers trusting quality they cannot check before buying, your margins are partly the price of that trust, and anything that tempts you to cut quality is a bet against your own future revenue. Two lessons follow. First, reputation is asymmetric: it is built slowly, sale by sale, and lost fast, so the first visible failure deserves an outsized response. Second, the more valuable reputation becomes, the more people will try to fake it, so the signals customers use, reviews, ratings and badges, need constant defence.

*Source: Klein & Leffler, JPE 89(4) (1981); Shapiro, QJE 98(4) (1983); Resnick, Zeckhauser, Swanson & Lockwood, Experimental Economics 9(2) (2006); Cabral & Hortaçsu, JIE 58(1) (2010); Luca, HBS WP 12-016 (2016); Luca & Zervas, Management Science 62(12) (2016). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. The leader's reputation

**What happened.** Leaders' reputations follow rules of their own. The first is that not all failures are equal. In a classic experiment, Peter Kim, Donald Ferrin, Cecily Cooper and Kurt Dirks asked participants to evaluate a job candidate accused of an accounting error at a previous job ([*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2004](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/686)). When the mistake was framed as a matter of competence, apologising repaired trust better than denying; when it was framed as a matter of integrity, denying worked better, because an apology for dishonesty confirms the worst. Research on how impressions form explains why. We weigh negative information more heavily when judging morality, because even dishonest people sometimes behave well, and positive information more heavily when judging ability, because incompetent people cannot fake a brilliant performance ([Skowronski & Carlston](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3196814)). A second rule is that fame can be dangerous. Ulrike Malmendier and Geoffrey Tate studied chief executives who won prestigious business awards and found that their firms [subsequently underperformed](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14140) similar firms whose bosses had not, while the winners earned more, wrote more books and joined more outside boards (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2009). The effects were concentrated in firms with weak governance. A third is that a good reputation can be rented out. David Hsu found that start-ups accepted offers from high-reputation venture capitalists [at a 10–14% discount](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/29) on valuation, paying for the investor's name (*Journal of Finance*, 2004). And AI has introduced a new reputational risk. In four experiments with more than 4,400 participants, Jessica Reif, Richard Larrick and Jack Soll found that people who used AI at work were [judged lazier and less competent](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12088386/) than those who got the same help from a colleague (*PNAS*, 2025), though the penalty disappeared among evaluators who used AI themselves.

**Why it matters.** For leaders, the practical lesson is to know which kind of reputation is at stake. Competence failures are forgivable and should be owned quickly, with an apology and a fix. Integrity failures are far more damaging, and the only reliable defence is not to commit them; a public reputation for honesty takes years to build and can be lost in one revealed lie. Malmendier and Tate's finding is a warning about the opposite problem: a glowing reputation can distract the person who has it, and the boards that should check them. And as AI use spreads, leaders should be open about how they and their teams use it. The reputational penalty fades where AI use is normal, and secrecy turns an efficiency into a question of honesty.

*Source: Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks, JAP 89(1) (2004); Skowronski & Carlston, Psychological Bulletin (1989); Malmendier & Tate, QJE 124(4) (2009); Hsu, Journal of Finance 59(4) (2004); Reif, Larrick & Soll, PNAS 122(19) (2025). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. Reputation in the age of AI

**What happened.** AI is changing reputation from both ends: it makes reputational signals cheaper to fake, and it is becoming the reader that judges them. On the faking side, the US Federal Trade Commission's rule banning fake reviews, including AI-generated ones, has been in force since October 2024. Its enforcement has been uneven. On December 22, 2025, the FTC sent warning letters to ten companies under the rule, and on the same day [set aside](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/in-rare-move-ftc-sets-aside-rytr-order-for-burdening-ai-innovation-and-failing-to-plead-violations) a 2024 order against Rytr, a company whose AI tool could generate reviews, arguing that the order placed an undue burden on AI innovation. Faked people are an even bigger risk. On November 5, prosecutors in New York charged three men with [using AI to clone the voices and images of chief executives](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-11-06/3-charged-in-scheme-to-use-ai-to-mimic-ceos-to-steal-129-million), and of lawyers, to persuade finance staff to wire money; the alleged scheme took about $129 million from companies including hospitals, a bank and a fund manager. The charges have not been tested in court. On the judging side, AI assistants are becoming the place where many customers ask which product, firm or service to trust. A University of Toronto study ([arXiv, September 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919)) found that AI search engines draw overwhelmingly on "earned" sources such as reviews, press coverage and comparison sites, rather than on what brands say about themselves: for American car queries, about 82% of AI-cited sources were third-party, compared with about 45% for Google. Their recommendations are also unstable. Researchers at SparkToro and Gumshoe ran the same product prompts repeatedly through leading AI assistants and found that the [same list of brands came back](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ai-recommendations-change-with-nearly-every-query-sparktoro/) less than 1% of the time (January 2026; not peer reviewed), although how *often* a brand appeared at all was far more consistent.

**Why it matters.** For decades, companies could shape their reputations partly through what they said about themselves. AI tilts the balance towards what others say about them. That raises the value of genuine third-party validation, independent reviews, credible press coverage and satisfied customers, and lowers the value of polished self-promotion. It also means a company's reputation is increasingly summarised by systems it cannot see and that do not give the same answer twice. Leaders should do three things. Check regularly what the major AI assistants say about the company, and fix the underlying sources rather than chasing rankings. Treat any unexpected request from an executive, by voice, video or email, as unverified until confirmed through a separate channel. And remember the oldest lesson of the reputation economy: the most durable protection against faked signals is a record that others will vouch for.

*Source: FTC Consumer Review Rule, 16 CFR Part 465 (2024); FTC (Dec 22, 2025); US Attorney, SDNY (Nov 5, 2026), via AP/ITV; Chen, Wang, Chen & Koudas, arXiv 2509.08919 (2025); SparkToro & Gumshoe (Jan 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: why traders keep their word** — in his Glasgow lectures, recorded by a student in 1766, Adam Smith asked why the Dutch, the most commercial people in Europe, were also "the most faithfull to their word." It was not national character, he argued, but self-interest. ["A dealer is afraid of losing his character,"](https://cafehayek.com/2025/06/quotation-of-the-day-5056.html) and when a person makes perhaps twenty contracts a day, "the very appearance of a cheat would make him lose." People who seldom deal with each other, by contrast, are more tempted to cheat, because a clever trick can gain more than the damage to their character costs. Repeated dealing, not virtue, makes honesty pay, which is why reputation systems work best where customers come back. (Smith, Lectures on Jurisprudence, 1766)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the fish that behaves when watched** — on coral reefs, small cleaner wrasse eat parasites off larger "client" fish. But the cleaners would rather eat their clients' protective mucus, which is a form of cheating. In a study in *Nature*, Redouan Bshary and Alexandra Grutter found that client fish watched how a cleaner treated others and preferred to visit cleaners they had seen behave well, and that cleaners [fed more cooperatively](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04755) when a potential client was watching (2006). A fish with a brain the size of a grain of rice manages its reputation. (Nature, 2006)

**Reputation goes digital** — by the end of this year, every EU member state is due to offer citizens a digital identity wallet, a phone app that can prove who you are, and share verified credentials such as qualifications or age, without handing over more data than needed. Regulated businesses, including banks, will have to accept it roughly a year later. Readiness is patchy, and the Commission has doubted that all 27 states will be ready in time ([Euronews, Sep 2026](https://www.euronews.com/2026/09/15/would-you-trust-one-app-with-all-your-digital-identity)). If it works, verified credentials could become a counterweight to AI-generated fakery: a way to prove that a person, a review or a qualification is real. (European Commission; Euronews)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Shakespeare gave the most famous defence of reputation to the least deserving speaker. In *Othello*, Iago tells the general: ["Good name in man and woman, dear my lord, / Is the immediate jewel of their souls. / Who steals my purse steals trash; 'tis something, nothing; / 'Twas mine, 'tis his, and has been slave to thousands; / But he that filches from me my good name / Robs me of that which not enriches him / And makes me poor indeed."](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/othello/read/3/3) In the previous act, the same Iago had told Cassio that reputation was "an idle and most false imposition, oft got without merit and lost without deserving." Both speeches are true, which is why Iago is so dangerous. A good name is precious, and it can be stolen by someone who knows how to manipulate what others believe. The tools for that kind of theft have never been cheaper. The best defence is still the one Cuthbert Heath chose in 1906: a record that speaks for itself.
