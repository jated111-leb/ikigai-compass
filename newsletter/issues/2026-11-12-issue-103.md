# The Daily Compass #103 — Bundles of habits

*Thursday, November 12, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

For most of his writing life, Anthony Trollope held a full-time job at the British Post Office. He wrote 47 novels anyway, and the reason was a habit. He rose at 5:30 every morning and wrote for three hours before going to work, with a watch on the desk in front of him. "It had at this time become my custom," he explained in his *Autobiography*, "to write with my watch before me, and to require from myself 250 words every quarter of an hour." He even had the words counted as he went, because words, "if not watched, will have a tendency to straggle." When the [*Autobiography*](https://www.entrepreneur.com/article/250835) was published after his death in 1882, readers were dismayed. Critics sneered at his "clock-work methods", and his reputation suffered for years: a great novelist was not supposed to work like a clerk. (Some modern readers doubt that anyone could keep up that pace for three hours; it is his own claim.) But Trollope's point was a serious one. Inspiration was unreliable; routine was not. A small, daily, almost mechanical habit, kept up for decades, produced a body of work that few writers of his era could match. This issue is about habits: how long they really take to form, why organisations run on them, and what happens when using AI becomes one.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. How long a habit really takes

**What happened.** The popular claim that a habit takes 21 days to form turns out to have no scientific basis. The best-known study is ["How are habits formed"](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) (*European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2010), in which Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick a new daily habit, such as drinking water with lunch or running before dinner, and to rate how automatic it felt each day for 12 weeks. The median time to reach peak automaticity was 66 days, with an enormous range, from 18 days to an estimated 254. Exercise habits took longer than eating or drinking ones, and, encouragingly, missing a single day made little difference. A [2024 systematic review](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/23/2488) in *Healthcare* found typical medians of about two months, with individual estimates from four days to nearly a year. A larger study used machine learning on real behaviour. In ["What can machine learning teach us about habit formation?"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500) (*PNAS*, 2023), Anastasia Buyalskaya, Colin Camerer and colleagues analysed more than 12 million gym check-ins from about 60,000 people and more than 40 million handwashing observations in hospitals. Gym habits took months to form; handwashing habits took weeks. And the people with the most predictable gym routines responded least to an intervention designed to increase attendance. Strong habits, in other words, are hard to change in either direction. Habits are also triggered by context more than intention. In a [study of cinema-goers](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211419863) (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 2011), David Neal, Wendy Wood and colleagues found that people with a habit of eating popcorn at the movies ate almost as much week-old stale popcorn as fresh, but only in a cinema. In a meeting room, they left the stale popcorn alone.

**Why it matters.** The science has two practical lessons for anyone trying to change behaviour, in themselves or in an organisation. First, be patient and plan for months, not weeks: a new habit is fragile long after the initial enthusiasm fades, and a missed day is not a failure. Second, change the context, not just the intention. Habits are tied to places, times and cues, which is why changes in context are such powerful opportunities. In a [field experiment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2015.11.008) (*Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 2016), Bas Verplanken and Deborah Roy found that a sustainability campaign worked better on households that had recently moved home than on those that had not, with a "window of opportunity" of about three months after a move. For leaders, this means that reorganisations, new offices, new systems and new years are not just disruptions to manage. They are rare chances to build better habits, while the old cues are gone.

*Source: Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle, EJSP 40(6) (2010); Singh, Murphy, Maher & Smith, Healthcare 12(23) (2024); Buyalskaya et al., PNAS 120(17) (2023); Neal, Wood, Wu & Kurlander, PSPB 37(11) (2011); Verplanken & Roy, J. Environmental Psychology 45 (2016). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Organisations run on routines

**What happened.** Organisations have habits too. In *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* (1982), the economists Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter argued that routines are a firm's "genes": the stored know-how that lets it function, and the reason it struggles to change. Laboratory work by Michael Cohen and Paul Bacdayan ([*Organization Science*, 1994](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v5y1994i4p554-568.html)) showed that routines are stored as a kind of procedural memory, fast and reliable but slow to adapt. Yet routines are not as rigid as they look. Martha Feldman and Brian Pentland ([*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3556620)) pointed out that every routine has two sides: the procedure as written, and what people actually do each time, which varies and slowly reshapes the procedure. The strongest evidence that routines can trap organisations, and individuals, comes from London. In ["The Benefits of Forced Experimentation"](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/1464166) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2017), Shaun Larcom, Ferdinand Rauch and Tim Willems used travel-card data to study a two-day strike in 2014 that closed many Underground stations. Forced to find new routes, about one in twenty affected commuters kept using their new route after the strike ended: they had found a better way to work that their habits had kept them from trying. The time they saved over the following years outweighed the time lost in the strike. People, the authors concluded, "under-experiment in normal times."

**Why it matters.** Routines are an organisation's memory and efficiency, but they also hide better options. The Tube strike shows that many people, and many teams, are travelling a familiar route that is not the best one, and will never find out unless something forces them to look. Some companies now create that disruption deliberately. In January 2023, Shopify [deleted around 10,000 recurring meetings](https://fortune.com/2023/01/03/shopify-cutting-meetings-worker-productivity/) from employees' calendars and asked teams to add back only those they really needed. Changing habits at scale is hard, however. In a [megastudy](https://bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=2660) of more than 61,000 gym members (*Nature*, 2021), Katherine Milkman and 29 colleagues tested 54 different programmes designed to increase exercise. Almost half worked during the four-week programme, but only a handful produced effects that clearly lasted beyond it. The lesson for leaders: periodically break routines on purpose, to discover what you are doing out of habit rather than choice. Then expect the new behaviour to need support for months, not weeks, before it becomes the new routine.

*Source: Nelson & Winter (1982); Cohen & Bacdayan, Organization Science 5(4) (1994); Feldman & Pentland, ASQ 48(1) (2003); Larcom, Rauch & Willems, QJE 132(4) (2017); Fortune (Jan 2023); Milkman et al., Nature 600 (2021). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. When AI becomes a habit

**What happened.** For many people, using AI is turning from an experiment into a habit. In its [Generative AI and News Report 2026](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2026-standalone-tools-information-infrastructure), published on October 29, the Reuters Institute at Oxford found that across six countries it has tracked since 2024, daily use of generative AI tools rose from 7% in 2024 to 16% in 2025 and 28% in 2026, while weekly use reached 51%. In the United States, a [Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/about-a-quarter-of-u-s-adults-use-chatbots-daily/) conducted in February found that about a quarter of adults use chatbots daily, including some who say they use them almost constantly. Habits shape skills, and the evidence on what a habit of AI does to skills is beginning to accumulate. In a study of four endoscopy centres in Poland ([*Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology*, 2025](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250813/AI-use-in-colonoscopy-may-weaken-doctors-detection-skills-over-time.aspx)), Krzysztof Budzyń and colleagues found that after doctors became used to an AI system that flagged polyps during colonoscopies, their detection rate in procedures done without AI fell from about 28% to 22%. The study was observational, but it was among the first real-world signs of "deskilling". In a theoretical paper, ["The Augmentation Trap"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03501) (arXiv, April 2026), Michael Caosun and Sinan Aral of MIT showed how a manager can rationally choose heavy AI use, because the productivity gains come quickly while the loss of skill comes slowly, and end up with a workforce that is less capable than if AI had never been adopted.

**Why it matters.** Most debates about AI focus on what it can do in a single task. The bigger question for organisations is what happens when using it becomes a daily habit, repeated thousands of times. Habits are powerful precisely because they run without thinking, which is also what makes them risky: a habit of accepting the first answer, or of never practising a skill unaided, will shape people's abilities whether anyone intends it or not. The research suggests a few defences. Decide deliberately which tasks should be done with AI and which without, rather than letting the habit decide. Build regular practice without the tool for skills that matter, much as pilots still fly manually despite autopilot. And measure skills directly, not just output, so that any erosion shows up before it becomes a problem. As the Caosun and Aral model suggests, the time horizon is crucial: the benefits of an AI habit appear in this quarter's numbers, while the costs may not appear for years.

*Source: Reuters Institute, Generative AI and News Report 2026 (Oct 29, 2026); Pew Research Center, Americans and AI 2026 (Jun 2026); Budzyń et al., Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology 10 (2025); Caosun & Aral, arXiv 2604.03501 (2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Aristotle never said it** — "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit." The line is quoted endlessly, usually attributed to Aristotle. In fact it was written by the American historian Will Durant in [*The Story of Philosophy*](https://checkyourfact.com/2019/06/26/fact-check-aristotle-excellence-habit-repeatedly-do/) (1926), as his own summary of Aristotle's ethics. The idea, though, is genuinely Aristotle's: in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he argued that we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones. Benjamin Franklin took the idea literally. In his *Autobiography*, he describes a little book with a chart of thirteen virtues, one row for each, and a black spot for every lapse. He focused on one virtue a week, cycling through all thirteen four times a year. He never achieved perfection, he admitted, but he was "a better and a happier man" for the attempt. (Durant, 1926; Franklin, Autobiography)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the brain's bookends** — as a habit forms, the brain seems to package it into a single unit. Studying rats learning to run mazes and press levers, the MIT neuroscientist Ann Graybiel and her colleagues found that neurons in a region called the striatum fire strongly at the [beginning and end of a well-learned routine](https://news.mit.edu/2018/distinctive-brain-pattern-helps-habits-form-0208) and go quiet in the middle, a pattern they call "task-bracketing". In a 2018 study in *Current Biology*, the bracketing appeared only around sequences that had been rewarded, not around the same movements performed without reward. The brain, it seems, marks where a habit starts and stops, and leaves the middle to run on autopilot. That is why habits are so efficient, and so hard to interrupt once begun. (Current Biology, Feb 2018)

**The habit-changing drug** — weight-loss drugs such as Ozempic and Wegovy are changing more than appetite. In a study published in the *Journal of Marketing Research* last December, Sylvia Hristakeva, Jūra Liaukonytė and Leo Feler used purchase data from a large panel of American households and found that those who started GLP-1 drugs [cut grocery spending by about 5%](https://www.news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/12/ozempic-changing-foods-americans-buy) within six months, with savoury snacks down about 10% and yoghurt and fresh fruit up. But many users stopped taking the drugs, and when they did, their spending largely drifted back. For food companies, it is a vivid case of a habit disrupted from the inside, and of how quickly old habits can return. (JMR, Dec 2025)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1890 the American psychologist William James published *The Principles of Psychology*, and its fourth chapter, on habit, remains one of the most quoted pieces of psychology ever written. Habit, he wrote, is "the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent". It keeps people in their stations and makes life possible, because we cannot think through every action from scratch. But he also saw its warning. ["Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits,"](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/09/25/william-james-on-habit/) he wrote, "they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state. We are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone." James's point is not that habits are bad. It is that they are being made all the time, whether we choose them or not. Trollope chose his: a watch, a desk and 250 words. The question for each of us, and for every organisation adopting new tools, is the same. Which habits are we building, one small repetition at a time?
