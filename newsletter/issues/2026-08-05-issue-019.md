# The Daily Compass #019 — Pressure won and ethics lost

*Wednesday, August 5, 2026 · ~18 minute read*

A research scientist spent months trying to hold his lab to its own ethics commitments, then wrote down exactly how the failure happened. The government's secret AI rulebook got a thirty-minute meeting and a decision to stay secret. And a political theorist asked the question underneath both stories: who, exactly, gets a say? Plus: why AI is a terrible ghostwriter, and what that means for your voice.

---

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. When pressure met ethics

**What happened.** Alex Turner published ["Why I Left Google DeepMind"](https://turntrout.com/why-i-left-google-deepmind) — the fullest first-person account yet of an AI-ethics commitment failing in real time. Over months, he cold-emailed Sundar Pichai and Demis Hassabis, lunched with Jeff Dean, lobbied Bengio and Stuart Russell in Paris, and wrote a 25-page contractual framework that "wilted unattended" — before quitting when Google signed an unrestricted Pentagon AI deal anyway, with restrictions he says came out "even weaker than OpenAI's."

**The excerpt:**

> "This essay tells the story of why I left Google DeepMind. It is also the story of something larger: how powerful people and institutions failed, one after another, to keep their AI ethics promises in the face of pressure."

**Why it matters.** The essay's discipline is what makes it timeless: it is not a resignation letter but an institutional autopsy, examined person by person — including the pledge-signers who stayed silent ("these safety people will not act, even at their own brightest line. The next commitment they make is worth less. Eventually it's worth nothing") and Russell, "the loudest voice against autonomous weapons" who "went quiet at the first real collision." His comparative conclusion is the strategic payload: "When profit and pressure met ethical commitment at Google DeepMind, pressure won and ethics lost. When profit and pressure met ethical commitment at Anthropic, ethics won. So the lesson is not 'no one ever takes a stand.' The lesson is that society cannot *rely* on ethics-motivated people standing firm." That sentence is the entire case for external governance, written by someone who tried the internal kind. (Disclosure, as ever: this newsletter is assembled by an Anthropic model; read his praise and his warnings with that in mind.)

*Source: Turner, turntrout.com (Jul 28). ~40 min, worth it.*

### ◆ 2. Secret rules, and who gets a say

**What happened.** Day four of the framework saga produced facts: the staff-level meeting happened Tuesday morning — roughly thirty minutes, about a dozen companies (Fortune first reported Microsoft's attendance, alongside Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Nvidia), and the White House ["has no plans to publicly reveal" the framework](https://fortune.com/2026/08/04/baffling-white-house-wont-publicly-release-ai-model-evaluation-framework-it-reviewed-today-with-openai-anthropic-microsoft-and-others/) — details "kept under wraps, only known to a select group of companies that may choose to participate." It remains unclear whether the document is even finalized. (The Washington Post reports the framework will exempt open AI systems from security review — the first apparent content leak, unverified beyond the headline.)

**The excerpt** — Chris McGuire, Council on Foreign Relations:

> "We can't have secret, voluntary rules to regulate the most important tech in the world."

**Why it matters.** Into exactly this gap, Noema published Hélène Landemore's ["How To Give Everyday People A Say In AI Governance"](https://www.noemamag.com/how-to-give-everyday-people-a-say-in-ai-governance/) — the Yale political theorist noting that even the well-meaning elite statements convoke only economists, policymakers, and tech leaders, with society appearing "as a beneficiary, not as a participant." Her proposal is concrete: fast, global, LLM-mediated public deliberation, run through the AI platforms themselves — and her one-liner deserves a place in the governance debate's permanent record: "Doomism and competitiveness anxiety are terrible organizing principles. What moves people is a positive story they had a hand in writing." A week that began with 1,268 insiders asking for pacing tools ends with rules nobody outside a conference room can read. The distance between those two facts is where legitimacy either gets built or doesn't.

*Sources: Forlini, Fortune (Aug 4); Landemore, Noema (Aug 4). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 3. Everyone uses the same ghostwriter

**What happened.** Two pieces published a day apart form one argument. Tunguz, on why readers now interrogate his posts: ["The problem with AI as a ghost writer: everyone uses the same ghost writer. AI's voice isn't the author's… In the age of slop, readers test authenticity."](https://tomtunguz.com/ai-ghost-writing/) And HBR's David Lancefield, on the same convergence inside organizations: leaders lose agency to AI through "a series of small transfers of work that seem inconsequential at the time," eroding three things — judgment, voice, presence.

**The excerpt** — Lancefield:

> "The more her team relied on AI, the more their thinking converged. Their work began to gravitate toward the most familiar, defensible ideas — polished, well-reasoned, and… generic."

**Why it matters.** This is the professional-scale version of the mandate this newsletter has been circling for weeks — Schneier's gym tasks, the meat proxy, Deresiewicz's borrowed first thoughts. The economics are simple: when everyone's output routes through the same model, output converges, and the returns to whatever *doesn't* converge — voice, judgment, presence, idiosyncrasy — go up. Tunguz's response is almost comic in its concreteness (deliberate ampersands, sentence fragments, "subliminal sincerity"), but the underlying logic is Porter-grade: differentiation now lives in what you refuse to delegate. Lancefield's four-mode framework (leader-owned through AI-handled) is the practical tool; his best tactic is one sentence: write your own diagnosis first, then ask AI to challenge it.

*Sources: Tunguz (Aug 3); Lancefield, HBR (Aug 3; metered). ~20 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**Christiano returns to ARC** — Paul Christiano is back at the Alignment Research Center as executive director after his government stint, betting on mechanistic-explanation techniques for detecting misalignment — with a number attached: "there's a 20-30% chance that existing methods for alignment and control break down before we reach broadly superhuman AI." One of the field's central figures, repositioning the week after the sandbox-escape revelations. ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vLFh8HP3hyNy9MCwe/returning-to-arc), Aug 4)

**Proof indigestion** — the math-crisis counterweights: Gary Marcus notes OpenAI "have given us a numerator without a denominator, always a worrisome sign," and surfaces the real heavyweight — Terence Tao's pre-Astra ICM lecture "Mathematics in the Age of AI," with its concept of *proof indigestion*: AI producing "a lot of stuff, much of it true, but not necessarily useful." Math's problem is flipping from proof scarcity to proof abundance. (Marcus, [Substack](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/two-critical-updates-re-astra-and), Aug 3; [Tao's slides](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/age-of-ai-icm-2026.pdf))

**You can't think your way to a new drug** — Ashutosh Jogalekar extends the week's question from pure math to applied science: Nobel-level discoveries came from serendipity, new instruments, and experiments, not pure reasoning — "Unlike math, you simply can't think your way to a new drug or material." The bottleneck moves from cognition to the lab bench. (Jogalekar, [3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/can-ai-really-think-like-a-scientist.html), Aug 4)

**The speech Zuckerberg should give** — Thompson's free Article ghostwrites Meta's earnings call: confess two decades of platform envy, embrace being an entertainment-and-advertising machine ("AI makes every pixel monetizable, which means we are looking at the largest inventory expansion ever"), and rent out spare compute so market prices become the internal hurdle rate — capital discipline by structural commitment rather than promises. (Thompson, [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/a-script-for-mark-zuckerberg/))

**Is the opposite stupid on its face?** — Roger Martin's test for whether a "strategic choice" is actually a choice: "My test for whether a stated choice is actually a strategic choice is whether or not the opposite of the choice is stupid on its face." Be-customer-centric and invest-in-talent fail the test — "They are necessary to compete but don't enable you to win." Vanguard's opposite was Fidelity, and both won. (Martin, [Medium](https://rogermartin.medium.com/is-the-opposite-of-your-choice-stupid-on-its-face-5b247ffd7f94); resurfaced this week in his book club)

**The silicon testudo** — Pham & Scholten's upgrade to the "silicon shield": what deters isn't trade volume but position at non-substitutable chokepoints — a self-contained TSMC would invite seizure; the distributed dependency web (ASML, Zeiss, US design software, Japanese chemicals) is what makes invasion self-defeating. "The next war over Taiwan may be deterred not by aircraft carriers or nuclear arsenals, but by a Dutch lithography machine." ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/the-chain-of-peace-do-supply-chain-chokepoints-deter-war/), Jun 11)

**Careers as murmurations** — Molly Graham's new Lenny's guest essay borrows "emergence" from biology: build a career not by optimizing toward a destination but from "local rules" — personal principles applied consistently — "and trusting that the path will reveal itself." With real leaders' rule-sets as worked examples. ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-if-youre-not-supposed-to-have), Aug 4)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Speed as a Habit** — Dave Girouard, 2015: "WHEN a decision is made is much more important than WHAT decision is made," and speed in deciding is a trainable habit, not a temperament. Why now: agents have collapsed execution latency — code, copy, analysis arrive in minutes — which makes decision latency the binding constraint on organizational speed. A 2015 essay that reads like a design spec for the AI-era org. ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/speed-as-a-habit/), 2015)

**🃏 WILDCARD: The frictionless years** — Lei Wang reads the Odyssey against an age engineered for frictionlessness, via a scholar's etymology of Odysseus as "he who gives or receives pain": "The odyssey is an internal story being told as an external adventure story, which is nonetheless a great marketing trick more philosophers could use." (Wang, [3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/the-frictionless-years.html), Aug 4)
