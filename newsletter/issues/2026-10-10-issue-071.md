# The Daily Compass #071 — The invisible infrastructure

*Saturday, October 10, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

You cannot see it on a balance sheet or a map, but it shapes almost everything that happens in an economy. It decides whether a manager hands a decision to a subordinate or keeps it on her own desk, whether a buyer pays before the goods arrive or insists on cash at the door, whether a company can grow beyond the circle of people its founder personally knows. Trust is the infrastructure that sits underneath the visible kind: the roads and grids and payment systems only work as well as the confidence people have in one another and in the rules. It is also oddly asymmetric. It takes years to build, a moment to destroy, and almost nobody budgets for it. This issue looks at what economists have learned about trust inside firms, why more of it is not always better, and how quickly it is being spent in workplaces going through change right now.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Trust draws the org chart

**What happened.** Why are some companies run by a handful of people at the top who approve every hire and every purchase, while others push decisions down to plant and branch managers? In ["The Organization of Firms Across Countries"](https://worldmanagementsurvey.org/wp-content/images/2014/11/QJE-2012-Bloom-1663-705.pdf) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2012), the economists Nicholas Bloom, Raffaella Sadun and John Van Reenen surveyed almost 4,000 firms in the United States, Europe and Asia about who makes decisions on investment, hiring, production and sales. Their strongest finding concerned trust. Firms headquartered in regions where people generally trust one another are significantly more likely to decentralize, and in their data trust accounts for about half of the variation in how decentralized firms are. To get closer to cause rather than correlation, they looked inside multinationals and found that subsidiaries are given more autonomy when the parent company's home country and the subsidiary's host country trust each other more, a result that holds even when trust is measured using religious similarities between the two countries.

**Why it matters.** The mechanism is simple and familiar to anyone who has worked in a low-trust environment. When an owner cannot trust managers to act in the firm's interest, she keeps decisions to herself. That works while the company is small. But one person's attention is limited, so a firm that cannot delegate cannot grow much beyond what its founder can personally oversee. The authors argue that this is one way trust raises productivity across a whole economy: it lets the best-run firms expand. For a manager in a low-trust society, the paper suggests that the habit of centralizing is not irrational, but it carries a hidden cost that compounds over time. The practical question is how to build pockets of trust deliberately, through clear decision rights, transparent metrics and a track record of delegation that works, so that the company can grow past the limits of its founder's attention.

*Source: Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, QJE 127(4) (2012). ~60 min for the paper; the introduction carries the argument.*

### ◆ 2. The right amount of trust

**What happened.** If trust is so valuable, should everyone simply trust more? Jeffrey Butler, Paola Giuliano and Luigi Guiso tested that idea in ["The Right Amount of Trust"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15344), first circulated as an NBER working paper and later published in the *Journal of the European Economic Association* (2016). Using data from the European Social Survey, they found that individual income is hump-shaped in how much people trust others. People with very low trust avoid being cheated but pass up profitable opportunities, partnerships and deals. People with very high trust take on too much social risk and get cheated more often. The best outcomes go to those in between. Their estimates are striking: the cost of trusting too much or too little is comparable to the income lost by not going to college, and roughly 10% of people hold beliefs miscalibrated enough to cut their income by more than 13%. They also found that the income-maximizing level of trust is typically higher than the average person's.

**Why it matters.** The paper turns trust from a virtue into a skill: calibration. The goal is not to be trusting or distrustful as a matter of temperament, but to match one's trust to how trustworthy the people and institutions around you actually are, and to update when evidence changes. That has an uncomfortable implication for low-trust societies. Where institutions are weak, low trust is often the rational response, and urging people to "just trust more" is bad advice. Yet the finding that optimal trust usually exceeds the average suggests that most people err on the side of too much suspicion, leaving deals and collaborations on the table. For a leader, the useful version is to trust specifically rather than generally: extend trust in measured steps, make it easy to verify, and expand it as people prove reliable.

*Source: Butler, Giuliano & Guiso, NBER WP 15344 (2009); JEEA (2016). ~45 min; the VoxEU summary takes 5.*

### ◆ 3. Trust is spent during change

**What happened.** The newest evidence on trust inside organizations comes from Edelman's ["Trust at Work"](https://www.edelman.com/trust/2026/trust-barometer/special-report-work) special report, released this week and based on a survey of more than 10,000 employees in ten countries, conducted in August. Trust in "my employer" is still high, at 74% globally, but it has fallen five points since 2024, its first decline since 2021. Trust in colleagues fell nine points, to 73%; trust in managers fell seven points, to 72%; and trust in chief executives fell four points, to 66%. Trade coverage describes all three as record lows for the series. The report links the decline to the pace of workplace transformation: most employees had been through at least one major change in the past year, with AI integration the most common, and 77% say they fear losing their jobs to automation, offshoring, insufficient training or other forces.

**Why it matters.** The Edelman data illustrate the asymmetry at the heart of this issue. During the pandemic, employers built up an unusual reserve of trust by communicating often and protecting their people. That reserve is now being drawn down by restructuring, AI rollouts and uncertainty about jobs, and the people closest to employees, colleagues and managers, are losing trust fastest. For leaders planning their own transformations, the lesson connects to the first two briefs: change spends trust, and organizations that do not replenish it will find delegation harder and resistance higher. Edelman's own prescription is to give employees more agency and clarity during change. Some caution is in order, since Edelman is a communications firm whose barometer also serves as a showcase for its services, and this report is a separate survey from its main annual barometer. But the direction of the numbers matches what many managers will recognize.

*Source: Edelman, Trust at Work special report (Oct 6, 2026). ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: an element of trust** — in 1972, the year he won the Nobel Prize in economics, Kenneth Arrow published an essay called ["Gifts and Exchanges"](https://philpapers.org/rec/ARRGAE) in *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, nominally a review of Richard Titmuss's book on whether blood donors should be paid. It contains one of the most quoted sentences in the economics of trust: "Virtually every commercial transaction has within itself an element of trust, certainly any transaction conducted over a period of time." Arrow went on to suggest that much of the world's economic backwardness could be explained by a lack of mutual confidence. Half a century of research, including the briefs above, has largely confirmed his intuition: trust is not a soft extra layered on top of markets but part of what makes markets possible at all. (Arrow, 1972)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the lighthouse on a ring** — in the sunken royal quarter of ancient Alexandria, underwater archaeologists from Franck Goddio's European Institute for Underwater Archaeology, working with Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities, have recovered a [bronze seal ring engraved with an image of the Pharos](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/mena/2026/10/07/new-image-of-an-ancient-wonder-divers-find-ring-showing-lighthouse-of-alexandria-at-sunken-palace/), the great Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. Egyptian officials say it is the first known seal ring to show the lighthouse. It was found on Antirhodos, a submerged island in the Eastern Harbour. The image resembles depictions on Alexandrian coins of the second century AD, though the ring's exact date and owner are not known. A seal was the ancient world's signature: a way to prove that a message or a sealed jar really came from who it claimed. Trust technology, lost under the sea for nearly two thousand years. (The National / Heritage Daily, Oct 7, 2026)

**Trust what you can check** — the one AI item this week. Stack Overflow's [2026 Developer Survey](https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/10/06/the-results-of-the-2026-developer-survey-are-here/), released Oct 6 with more than 30,000 responses from 169 countries, found that 83% of developers use AI tools in some way. But their trust is conditional: about 48% say they trust AI output when they can easily verify it, while only about 7% would trust it with important decisions, and nearly 79% say attribution of sources matters when they judge AI-generated answers. It is brief 2's calibration principle in practice. The people who use these tools most have learned to trust them in proportion to how easily they can be checked. (Stack Overflow, Oct 6, 2026)

**The escrow that beat eBay** — when eBay entered China in the early 2000s by buying the leading auction site EachNet, it looked unbeatable. Alibaba's answer, Taobao, won in large part by solving a trust problem rather than a price problem. In a market where few shoppers had credit cards and strangers had little reason to trust each other, Alibaba launched Alipay, which [held the buyer's money in escrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taobao) until the buyer confirmed the goods had arrived. By March 2006, according to Forbes, Taobao had about 67% of China's online-shopping users to eBay EachNet's 29%. It is a lesson with obvious local force. In Iraq, according to central bank figures [reported by Shafaq News](https://en.hathalyoum.net/articles/295402), roughly nine-tenths of the dinars in circulation sit outside the banking system, and only about a fifth of adults hold a bank account. In markets like that, the clearest lesson in platform strategy still holds: sell trust first, and the transactions follow. (Taobao history; Forbes, 2014; Shafaq News, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1986 the philosopher Annette Baier published an essay called ["Trust and Antitrust"](https://philpapers.org/rec/BAITAA) that offered a definition still worth carrying around. Trust, she wrote, is accepted vulnerability to another's possible but not expected ill will. To trust someone is to hand them a power over you that they could misuse, while expecting that they will not. That is why betrayed trust stings in a way that a broken machine does not: we were not merely relying on someone, we had made ourselves vulnerable to them. Sixteen years later, the philosopher Onora O'Neill used her BBC Reith Lectures, *A Question of Trust*, to warn that societies trying to restore lost trust often reach for more audits, rules and controls, which can deepen suspicion rather than cure it. Her first lecture opened with an older thought, from Confucius: a ruler needs weapons, food and trust, and if he cannot keep all three, he should give up the weapons first, then the food, and hold on to trust until the end. Today's briefs are, in a sense, footnotes to that ancient priority. Firms that cannot trust cannot delegate. People who cannot calibrate their trust leave value on the table or get cheated. Organizations that spend trust during change without replenishing it find their people quietly withdrawing. The lesson is not to trust blindly. It is to be worthy of trust first, to extend it deliberately, and to remember that the vulnerability it requires is not a weakness but the price of doing anything together at all.
