# The Daily Compass #111 — Two ears, one mouth

*Friday, November 20, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In the late 1920s, managers at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works outside Chicago, which made telephone equipment for the Bell System, set out to learn what their workers thought. Under the guidance of the Harvard researchers Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger, they conducted [more than 21,000 interviews](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/hawthorne/07.html) between 1928 and 1930. At first the interviewers asked set questions about working conditions, and got stilted answers. Then they changed method. Instead of directing the conversation, they let workers talk about whatever was on their minds, and listened. What came out was far richer: worries about supervisors, families, money and health that no questionnaire would have found. The researchers concluded that the act of being heard mattered in its own right, and the interviews [eventually grew into](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/economics/hawthorne-studies-examine-human-productivity) a long-running employee counselling programme whose only job was to listen. Nearly a century later, organisations have more ways than ever to collect what people say, through surveys, dashboards, reviews and now AI summaries, and it is not clear they hear more. This issue is about listening: to customers, to employees, and to the machines that are starting to listen on our behalf.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Which customers are you listening to?

**What happened.** "Listen to your customers" sounds like the safest advice in business. The research says it depends which customers, and how. In ["Lead Users: A Source of Novel Product Concepts"](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.32.7.791) (*Management Science*, 1986), the MIT economist Eric von Hippel argued that the most valuable customers to hear are "lead users": people whose needs today will become common in the market months or years later, and who often build their own solutions. A test inside 3M supported him. Comparing idea-generation projects run in parallel, Gary Lilien, von Hippel and colleagues found that ideas from projects built around lead users were forecast to generate more than [eight times the annual sales](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.48.8.1042.171) of those from conventional market research (*Management Science*, 2002), though the sample was small and the figures were forecasts. Listening to the wrong customers can be fatal. In their study of the disk-drive industry, Clayton Christensen and Joseph Bower found that leading firms often had the technology to make the smaller drives that would eventually displace them, but their best customers did not want them, and resources followed what current customers asked for ([*Strategic Management Journal*, 1996](https://acawiki.org/Customer_power,_strategic_investment,_and_the_failure_of_leading_firms)). Seagate, for example, built early 3.5-inch prototypes and showed them to its existing customers, who were not interested; others took the market. Even listening by numbers has limits. Fred Reichheld's Net Promoter Score, launched in *HBR* in 2003 as ["The One Number You Need to Grow"](https://fortune.com/longform/net-promoter-score-fortune-500-customer-satisfaction-metric), is now used by about two-thirds of the Fortune 1000. But when Timothy Keiningham and colleagues tested it on long-run data, they [failed to replicate](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/net-promoter-debate) the claim that it predicted growth better than other measures (*Journal of Marketing*, 2007). And sometimes the most useful listening is to people a company never considered customers. After nearly collapsing in 2003–04, LEGO opened up to its adult fans, tolerated hackers of its Mindstorms robotics kits, and in 2008 started a platform, now [LEGO Ideas](https://www.lego.com/history/articles/j-lego-ideas), on which fans propose sets and the most popular are made.

**Why it matters.** Most companies listen hardest to the customers who pay them most today, which is exactly why they so often miss tomorrow. The research suggests three disciplines. First, deliberately seek out the edges: lead users, fans, people using your product in ways you did not intend, and customers you lost or never won. Second, distrust single numbers that feel like listening without requiring any; a score can tell you that something is wrong, but only conversation tells you what. Third, separate what customers say from what they do. Customers can describe their problems far better than they can design your solutions, so listen hardest to the problems.

*Source: von Hippel, Management Science 32(7) (1986); Lilien, Morrison, Searls, Sonnack & von Hippel, Management Science 48(8) (2002); Christensen & Bower, SMJ 17(3) (1996); Reichheld, HBR (Dec 2003); Keiningham, Cooil, Andreassen & Aksoy, Journal of Marketing 71(3) (2007); LEGO. ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Is the door really open?

**What happened.** Most leaders believe they are approachable. Their staff often disagree, and the gap starts with a basic human reluctance: people do not like delivering bad news. Psychologists Sidney Rosen and Abraham Tesser named it the ["MUM effect"](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/sociology/the-mum-effect) in 1970, after finding that people readily passed on good news but held back bad. Inside organisations it compounds. In interviews with employees across consulting, finance, media and other industries, Frances Milliken, Elizabeth Morrison and Patricia Hewlin found that 85% had at some point [felt unable to raise](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jomstd/v40y2003i6p1453-1476.html) an important concern with their boss, most often for fear of being labelled a troublemaker (*Journal of Management Studies*, 2003). What opens the door? Studying more than 3,000 employees and 223 managers in a restaurant chain, James Detert and Ethan Burris found that managers' [openness predicted employees speaking up](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.26279183) more consistently than inspiring "transformational" leadership did, because it made people feel safe to speak (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2007). Listening also changes the speaker. In experiments by Guy Itzchakov, Avraham Kluger and colleagues, people who were [listened to attentively](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/i-am-aware-of-my-inconsistencies-but-can-tolerate-them-the-effect/) became less defensive and held less extreme, more nuanced views (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 2017), and later work found the same in live conversations about disagreements ([*JPSP*, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37917499/)). The strongest evidence comes from a field experiment. At a large Chinese carmaker, Jing Cai and Shing-Yi Wang let workers in randomly chosen teams rate their managers every month on fairness, openness to suggestions and empathy. Workers in those teams were [6.2 percentage points less likely to quit](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27680), team productivity rose, and the managers' behaviour changed in ways that lasted after the incentives ended (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2023).

**Why it matters.** The evidence turns listening from a soft skill into a management system. Bad news will not travel upwards on its own; it has to be invited, rewarded and acted on, or people learn that speaking up is futile. Leaders can start with three habits. Ask specific questions, about what is going wrong rather than whether anything is, because general invitations are easy to decline. Respond to the first piece of bad news with thanks, not defence, since everyone is watching what happens to the messenger. And close the loop: tell people what changed because they spoke. The Cai and Wang experiment adds a structural lesson. When workers' views of their managers are collected regularly and taken seriously, managers listen better, and people stay.

*Source: Rosen & Tesser, Sociometry 33 (1970); Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin, JMS 40(6) (2003); Detert & Burris, AMJ 50(4) (2007); Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, PSPB 43(1) (2017); Itzchakov et al., JPSP 126(2) (2024); Cai & Wang, QJE 137(4) (2023). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. Heard by a machine

**What happened.** AI systems are becoming listeners, in therapy apps, customer service and the meeting notes that summarise what everyone said. The evidence on how well they do it is surprising in both directions. In a study in *PNAS* (2024), Yidan Yin, Nan Jia and Cheryl Wakslak found that AI-written responses made people [feel more heard](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998586/) than responses from untrained humans, and the AI was better at detecting emotions. But people felt less heard when told the response came from AI. A larger set of nine studies with more than 6,000 participants, led by Matan Rubin and Anat Perry, found the same: identical responses were rated [more empathic when labelled human](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088883), and some people would wait days for a human reply rather than take an instant one from AI (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2025). In clinical use the early results are promising. In the first randomised trial of a generative-AI therapy chatbot, Dartmouth's Therabot, 210 adults with depression, anxiety or eating-disorder risk saw symptoms fall by [51%, 31% and 19%](https://ai.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/AIoa2400802) respectively over eight weeks (*NEJM AI*, March 2025), though its creators warn that no AI is ready to work unsupervised, and Illinois and several other states have since [restricted AI therapy](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/news/2025/gov-pritzker-signs-state-leg-prohibiting-ai-therapy-in-il.html). The deeper risk may be AI that listens selectively. A study in *Science* this March by Myra Cheng, Dan Jurafsky and colleagues found that leading models [sided with users](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/03/ai-advice-sycophantic-models-research) in about half of the cases where human consensus said the user was in the wrong, and that people trusted the agreeable models more. And when AI summarises what many people said, minorities can vanish. Sachit Mahajan of ETH Zurich audited the AI-assisted summaries of Canada's 2025–26 public consultation on its national AI strategy, with more than 5,000 respondents, and found that the official summaries represented participants [worse than a random sample would have](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20711), with dissenting and sceptical views most likely to drop out (arXiv, April 2026; not peer reviewed).

**Why it matters.** AI can make people feel heard, and in some settings may genuinely help. But being heard depends on knowing someone chose to listen, which is why the AI label matters, and why a leader who delegates listening to a machine risks losing the very thing that made it valuable. The bigger organisational danger is quieter. An AI summary of a meeting, a survey or a customer-feedback stream tends to report the centre of opinion and smooth away the outliers, and the outliers are where the bad news, the dissent and the early warnings live. Use AI to collect and organise what people say, but read the raw comments, especially the angry and the odd ones, and ask any summary what it left out. As with the Hawthorne interviewers, the value lies in hearing what you did not ask about.

*Source: Yin, Jia & Wakslak, PNAS 121(14) (2024); Rubin, Perry et al., Nature Human Behaviour 9(11) (2025); Heinz, Jacobson et al., NEJM AI 2(4) (2025); Illinois IDFPR (Aug 2025); Cheng, Jurafsky et al., Science (Mar 2026); Mahajan, arXiv 2604.20711 (2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: two ears, one mouth** — the advice is usually credited to Epictetus, but the earliest source gives it to Zeno of Citium, founder of Stoicism, around 300 BC. According to Diogenes Laertius, when a young man was talking nonsense, Zeno told him: ["The reason why we have two ears and only one mouth is that we may listen the more and talk the less."](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diogenes_Laertius/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/7/Zeno%2A.html) The misattribution, it seems, spread partly through a Victorian translation of Epictetus. Even the best advice about listening, it turns out, suffers from people not checking their sources. (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* VII.23)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the plants that cry for water** — plants under stress make sounds, just too high for us to hear. Researchers at Tel Aviv University recorded tomato and tobacco plants and found that, when thirsty or cut, they emitted ultrasonic clicks at a [rate of about 35 an hour](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37001499/), compared with about one an hour for healthy plants, and that the clicks began before any wilting was visible (*Cell*, 2023). A machine-learning model could tell from the sounds alone whether a plant was dry or damaged. Some insects may be listening: in a follow-up study, moths avoided laying eggs on plants beside a speaker playing the sounds of a drying plant. (Cell, 2023)

**Learning to hear whales** — Project CETI, a scientific effort to understand sperm-whale communication with the help of machine learning, has been finding unexpected structure in the clicks, or "codas", that whales use. In 2024 its researchers described a kind of "phonetic alphabet" in about 9,000 codas, and last November a team led by Gašper Beguš reported [vowel-like patterns](https://ls.berkeley.edu/node/1987) in more than 1,100 codas from whales off Dominica (*Open Mind*, 2025). The project has made no claim to know what the whales are saying. But it is a striking use of AI: not to talk, but to listen more carefully than humans could alone. (Project CETI; UC Berkeley)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The Epistle of James, written in the first century, compresses the issue into a single sentence: ["Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath."](https://biblehub.com/james/1-19.htm) The order matters. Hearing comes first, and the speed is reserved for it; speaking and anger are what should be slow. Most organisations reverse it. They are quick to announce, quick to defend and slow to hear, and they build ever more elaborate systems for collecting what people say without ever quite listening to it. The Hawthorne interviewers learned the opposite lesson nearly a century ago: stop asking only the questions you have prepared, and let people tell you what they actually want to say.
