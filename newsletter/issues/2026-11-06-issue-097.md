# The Daily Compass #097 — A palace in time

*Friday, November 6, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1880 the young French mathematician Henri Poincaré spent about two weeks struggling, at his desk in Caen, with a problem about a class of functions he would later name after Lazarus Fuchs. Then he set it aside to join a geological field trip organised by the School of Mines, and, as he later recalled, the travel made him forget his mathematical work. At Coutances, in Normandy, the group boarded an omnibus to go somewhere or other. "At the moment when I put my foot on the step," Poincaré wrote in his 1908 essay on mathematical invention, the idea came to him, "without anything in my former thoughts seeming to have paved the way for it", that the transformations he had been using were identical to those of non-Euclidean geometry. He did not check it then; he simply carried on his conversation, and verified the result later at leisure. Poincaré's account is one of the best-documented examples of something many people have experienced: the breakthrough that arrives not during the effort, but after it, in the pause. This issue is about the value of not working: the evidence that long hours stop paying off, why leaders' sleep matters to their teams, and why AI, sold as a way to save time, seems so far to be filling it instead.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The flat part of the curve

**What happened.** The best historical evidence on hours and output comes from Britain's munitions factories in the First World War, when the government studied workers, mostly women, putting in 60 to 100 hours a week. Analysing those records in ["The Productivity of Working Hours"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp8129) (*Economic Journal*, 2015), the Stanford economist John Pencavel found that output rose in proportion to hours up to about 49 hours a week, and then at a decreasing rate. According to [press accounts](https://qz.com/313397/you-really-can-work-longer-hours-without-killing-your-productivity) of the study, output at 70 hours differed little from output at 56, and the absence of a weekly rest day was especially costly: six eight-hour days produced slightly more than 70 hours spread over seven. More recent experiments point the same way. In a study published in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2025, Wen Fan and Juliet Schor of Boston College followed six-month four-day-week trials at 141 organisations with nearly 2,900 employees in six countries; burnout fell and job satisfaction and mental health rose, with the gains partly explained by fewer sleep problems and less fatigue. In the 2022 British pilot, 56 of the 61 participating companies chose to continue afterwards. Now governments are acting. On October 14 Brazil's Senate voted 75 to 1 for a constitutional amendment that [ends the six-day "6x1" schedule](https://en.mercopress.com/2026/10/15/brazil-approves-a-40-hour-week-and-two-days-off-ten-days-before-the-runoff), guaranteeing two weekly rest days and phasing the maximum working week down from 44 hours to 40, without a pay cut. Business groups warned of higher costs for small companies.

**Why it matters.** The idea that rest raises productivity is not new. When Henry Ford moved his factory workers to a five-day, 40-hour week in 1926, he argued that "we can get at least as great production in five days as we can in six", because the pressure would bring better methods. The evidence since suggests he was broadly right, with caveats: the four-day-week trials involved self-selected firms, and much depends on whether the work itself is redesigned rather than merely compressed. But the core finding is robust. Beyond a certain point, extra hours buy little extra output and cost a great deal in fatigue, errors and turnover. For leaders, the practical question is not how to get more hours from people but where the flat part of the curve begins for their kind of work, and how to stop the organisation drifting past it. Protected rest days, realistic workloads and a culture that does not reward visible overwork are not perks. They are a way of buying output at a better price.

*Source: Pencavel, Economic Journal 125 (2015); Fan & Schor, Nature Human Behaviour (2025); Autonomy / 4 Day Week Global UK pilot (2023); Brazil Senate, via MercoPress (Oct 15, 2026); Ford, World's Work (1926). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Your bad night is your team's bad day

**What happened.** The management researcher Christopher Barnes has spent much of his career studying what sleep does to leaders. In ["You Wouldn't Like Me When I'm Sleepy"](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/4353) (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2015), Barnes, Lorenzo Lucianetti, Devasheesh Bhave and Michael Christian tracked supervisors and their teams over ten working days. On days after a leader had slept badly, the leader had less self-control, behaved more abusively towards subordinates, and the whole team was less engaged that day. Notably, the quality of the leader's sleep mattered more than its quantity. Founders are not exempt. In a study in the *Journal of Business Venturing* in 2019, J. Jeffrey Gish, David Wagner, Denis Grégoire and Barnes asked more than 700 entrepreneurs to evaluate business pitches that experts had already ranked. Those who were short of sleep were less able to recognise the most promising ideas, sometimes ranking a poor idea above better ones; when the same entrepreneurs slept at least seven hours, they [consistently picked the pitches](https://www.ucf.edu/news/study-sleep-is-essential-for-business-leaders-seeking-next-successful-venture/) the experts rated best. Recovery during the day matters too, though less than people hope. A meta-analysis led by Patricia Albulescu in *PLOS ONE* (2022) found that micro-breaks of ten minutes or less reliably increased energy and reduced fatigue, but improved performance only on less demanding tasks; harder work seems to need longer recovery. And the benefits of holidays fade fast: research by Jessica de Bloom and colleagues suggests that well-being typically returns to its pre-holiday level within about a week of returning to work.

**Why it matters.** A leader's sleep is not a private matter. Because leaders set the emotional tone for their teams and make decisions that others must live with, a poor night's sleep is quietly transmitted to everyone who reports to them. The research also overturns a familiar assumption: that rest is a reward to be earned after the work is done. Recovery works more like a battery than a bank account. It needs regular recharging, through sleep, breaks and evenings genuinely away from work, rather than an annual holiday that drains away within days. A meta-analysis of studies on "psychological detachment" from work adds an uncomfortable twist: the people with the heaviest workloads, who most need to switch off, are the least able to do so. That is a design problem, not a personal failing, and it is one leaders are in a position to solve, starting with their own example.

*Source: Barnes, Lucianetti, Bhave & Christian, AMJ 58(5) (2015); Gish, Wagner, Grégoire & Barnes, JBV (2019); Albulescu et al., PLOS ONE 17(8) (2022); de Bloom et al. (2009, 2023); Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah, Frontiers in Psychology (2017). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. The time that AI saves goes somewhere

**What happened.** AI tools are sold as a way to give people time back. Two datasets released last week suggest that, so far, the time is being reinvested in more work. On October 29 the workforce-analytics company ActivTrak published an analysis of digital activity data from 9,140 knowledge workers at 467 companies between April and September. Workers who used AI tools had [workdays up to 90 minutes longer](https://www.cio.com/article/4243615/does-ai-actually-save-employees-time-data-is-contradictory.html) than those who did not, with more time spent after hours and more switching between applications. ActivTrak itself cautions that the data show correlation, not cause: people who already work long hours may simply adopt AI more readily. Two days earlier the software company Hubstaff released a survey of 1,068 American office workers: 46% of AI users said the amount of work expected of them had risen since they started using AI, against 5% who said it had fallen, and 69% of all respondents reported experiencing burnout in the past year. Both companies sell workplace software, and the findings should be read with that in mind. But they echo a field study at an American technology company by Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye of UC Berkeley, published in *Harvard Business Review* in February, which concluded that AI tools "didn't reduce work, they consistently intensified it", as people took on broader tasks and filled natural pauses with quick AI queries.

**Why it matters.** Every major productivity technology raises the same question: will the gains come back as time, or as more output? Historically the answer has depended less on the technology than on the choices of employers and societies. Without a deliberate decision, the default is that saved time is absorbed by rising expectations, as these early data suggest. For leaders, this means that AI's benefits to wellbeing will not arrive on their own. If the goal is sustainable performance rather than a short burst of output followed by burnout, organisations need to decide explicitly how much of the time AI saves will be returned as slack, recovery or shorter hours, and to protect the pauses that AI makes it so easy to fill. Even AI researchers seem to be reaching a similar conclusion about their machines (see Rapid Fire).

*Source: ActivTrak Productivity Lab (Oct 29, 2026), via CIO; Hubstaff State of the Workweek (Oct 27, 2026); Ranganathan & Ye, HBR (Feb 2026). ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Darwin's thinking path** — Charles Darwin, who transformed biology from his house in rural Kent, kept a working routine that would alarm most modern managers. According to the reminiscences of his son Francis, he did his concentrated work in the morning and was often heard to say "I've done a good day's work" around midday. The rest of the day went to letters, reading, rest and walks around the Sandwalk, a gravel path at the edge of his garden that he called his "thinking path". The writer Alex Soojung-Kim Pang, in his book *Rest* (2016), estimates from this timetable that Darwin did about four hours of focused work a day, and notes that the mathematician G.H. Hardy believed four hours of creative work a day was about the limit for a mathematician. Darwin's output, including *On the Origin of Species*, suggests the limit was not a handicap. (Francis Darwin, Life and Letters, 1887; [Darwin Correspondence Project](https://darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/curious/darwin-and-working-home))

**🃏 WILDCARD: spiders that make up lost sleep** — researchers at the University of Bonn led by Daniela Rößler filmed 92 jumping spiders of the species *Portia fimbriata* through the night and found that they appear to sleep in [two distinct stages](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261026225555.htm): a quiet stage and a stage resembling REM sleep, with eye movements, twitching and leg curling. The spiders reacted more slowly to noise while in these states, and after a disrupted night they spent more time in the REM-like stage, a hallmark of genuine sleep. The study, published in *PNAS* in late October, did not measure brain activity, so it cannot say whether spiders dream. But it adds to evidence that even small-brained animals pay back their sleep debts. (PNAS, Oct 2026)

**Machines that need to sleep** — on October 27 Google Research described a ["sleep" framework](https://research.google/blog/the-sleep-framework-a-new-way-for-llms-to-retain-knowledge-in-continual-learning/) for large language models, based on a paper by Ali Behrouz, Farnoosh Hashemi, Adel Javanmard and Vahab Mirrokni. Instead of trying to learn everything continuously, the model alternates between a "wake" phase, in which it takes in new information as short-term memory, and an offline "sleep" phase, in which it consolidates what matters into its long-term parameters, including a "dreaming" stage in which it generates its own practice material. The aim is to let models keep learning without forgetting what they already know. The results are the authors' own and await independent testing, and the biological language is a metaphor. But it is a striking one: even for machines, taking in information and consolidating it appear to be different jobs. (Google Research, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1951 the rabbi and philosopher Abraham Joshua Heschel published a short book, *The Sabbath*, about the ancient practice of setting aside one day in seven for rest. Heschel argued that modern civilisation had become obsessed with space, with acquiring things and conquering territory, and had forgotten how to sanctify time. The Sabbath, he wrote, was an answer to that: "The seventh day is a palace in time which we build. It is made of soul, of joy and reticence." One need not share Heschel's faith to see the force of the image. Rest, in his account, is not an absence or a gap between periods of usefulness. It is something built, deliberately and in advance, with walls that keep the rest of the week out. That is exactly what the evidence in this issue suggests organisations need. The munitions workers needed their rest day; the leader needs the night's sleep; Poincaré needed the excursion; and the time that AI saves will become rest only if someone decides to build it. The working week fills whatever space it is given. A palace in time has to be built on purpose.
