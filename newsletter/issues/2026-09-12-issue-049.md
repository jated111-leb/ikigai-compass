# The Daily Compass #049 — What it is like to be

*Saturday, September 12, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

The great intellectual achievement of the twentieth century was to drain the living world of mind. Purpose was demoted to illusion, "goals" to loose talk, the organism to a very complicated machine and behavior to mechanism; to speak of what an animal *wanted* or what a life was *for* was to reveal yourself as soft, pre-scientific, a mystic in a lab coat. It was a spectacularly productive austerity — it built molecular biology and most of modern medicine — but it always carried the faint scandal of leaving out the most obvious thing about living things, which is that it is *like something* to be one. This issue is about a quiet reversal now underway on three fronts at once: purpose is being readmitted to biology as a real phenomenon, mind is being found in places we'd declared mindless, and we are beginning to decode the speech of a genuinely alien intelligence in the ocean. And it is about the wall all three of them run into — the oldest, hardest problem there is — which no amount of data has ever climbed: you can map a mind completely from the outside and still not know what it is like to be inside it.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Purpose comes back to biology

**What happened.** For most of a century, biologists have flinched at any talk of goals, intentions, or purpose in living things — such language was tolerated only as shorthand, a "convenient fiction" to be cashed out, eventually, in pure mechanism. In a substantial Aeon essay, the science writer Philip Ball reports on a bold research program arguing that the flinch was a mistake: ["The biological research putting purpose back into life"](https://aeon.co/essays/the-biological-research-putting-purpose-back-into-life) surveys scientists working at the intersection of information theory, thermodynamics, and biology who now suspect that *agency* — genuine goal-directedness — is a real natural phenomenon, not a story we impose on indifferent chemistry. Organisms don't merely behave *as if* they have ends; on this view they actually have them, and that capacity is something the physics of living matter produces and the old machine metaphor systematically hid.

**Why it matters.** This is a deep reversal, and it cuts against the grain of the moment. Just as we are busy building artificial systems and arguing about whether *they* have goals, the science of actual life is concluding that goal-directedness was never the metaphor we thought — that the most reductive account of biology left out something structurally real. It matters for how we think about AI (we are trying to engineer, in silicon, a property we are only now admitting we don't fully understand in carbon), and it matters for the larger picture this issue is drawing: the austere twentieth-century move of subtracting mind and purpose from nature is being partially reversed by nature's own students. The universe, it increasingly seems, is not as empty of insides as we made it. Which raises the question the next two briefs press harder: if purpose is real and widespread, where exactly does it stop — and how would we ever know from the outside?

*Source: Ball, Aeon (Jan 29, 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. Mind beyond the animal

**What happened.** Push the question outward and it gets stranger fast. An Aeon essay on the frontier of plant science — ["Beyond the animal brain: plants have cognitive capacities too"](https://aeon.co/essays/beyond-the-animal-brain-plants-have-cognitive-capacities-too) — argues that talk of plant *learning* and *memory* is, in many well-studied cases, not metaphorical but matter-of-fact. Plants sense, integrate dozens of variables, distinguish kin from stranger, anticipate, and adjust — they simply do it on timescales and through mechanisms too slow, too fast, or too diffuse for animals like us to read as behavior. The essay's sharpest point is about *us*: because we are animals, we recognize animal-like activity as intelligent and lean on anthropomorphic prototypes, so a mind that doesn't move like ours, has no face, no brain, no center, slips beneath our notice entirely.

**Why it matters.** The plant case is a mirror held up to our whole concept of mind. We have been looking for cognition in the shape of *our* cognition — centralized, fast, brain-based — and calling everything else insensate. If that prototype is a parochial accident of being the kind of creature we happen to be, then the map of "where mind is" has been drawn by the mapmaker's own reflection, and it is far too small. This is the intellectual bridge to the age's most disorienting questions, about minds in silicon and minds in the sea: our confidence that we can *tell* whether something has an inner life rests on a template we did not choose and rarely examine. The plant, rooted and silent and demonstrably doing many of the things we call thinking, is the nearest available lesson that intelligence need not look anything like the thing doing the looking.

*Source: Aeon. ~14 min.*

### ◆ 3. Decoding the alien next door

**What happened.** And then there is the case that puts a technology behind the philosophy. Project CETI and allied researchers are using machine learning to analyze the click-patterns ("codas") of sperm whales, and the results keep suggesting a far more structured, combinatorial communication system than anyone expected — enough that the UC Berkeley linguist Gašper Beguš describes sperm whales as "an alien intelligence living in the ocean next door." The legal scholar César Rodríguez-Garavito, in an [NYU analysis](https://www.law.nyu.edu/news/ideas/cesar-rodriguez-garavito-animal-communication-ai), takes the next step seriously: if AI genuinely brings us within reach of the *content* of whale communication, it forces live questions of rights and even consent — whether we could one day be obliged to bring the interests of a decoded species into the human decisions that affect them.

**Why it matters.** Here the reversal reaches its most vivid and its most humbling. The same tools we've spent this whole newsletter watching — pattern-finding machine learning — may hand us the *syntax* of another mind, a genuine feat. But syntax is exactly where brief 1's purpose and brief 2's mirror collide with the wall: we might decode every coda, chart the entire grammar, predict what a whale will "say" next — and still not have the faintest access to what it is *like* to be the whale saying it. Decoding the signal is not inhabiting the experience. The danger, and it is a real one as these claims accelerate, is that fluency in the code will be mistaken for entry into the mind — that we will confuse a perfect map of the outside for the inside it can never quite reach. What we owe a mind we can translate but not enter is one of the genuinely new moral questions of the decade, and it is being opened not by philosophers but by a microphone array in the Caribbean.

*Source: Beguš / Project CETI; Rodríguez-Garavito, NYU. ~11 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: What Is It Like to Be a Bat?** — Thomas Nagel, *The Philosophical Review*, 1974. The wall itself, named once and for all. Nagel argued that an organism is conscious if and only if there is "something that it is like" to be that organism — and that this *subjective character of experience* can never be captured by any amount of objective, third-person description. You can know everything about a bat's echolocation, every neuron and frequency, and still not know what it is like to perceive the world as sonar, because knowing-that and being-it are different in kind. Why now: it is the exact wall all three briefs run into. As we readmit purpose to life, find cognition in plants, and decode the speech of whales with AI, Nagel's fifty-year-old caution is the one that governs all of it — the map, however complete, is not the territory of another's experience, and no instrument yet built even points in that direction. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_It_Like_to_Be_a_Bat%3F), 1974)

**The indifferent planet** — a bracing corrective to all this talk of mind and meaning, from the other end of the scale. An [Aeon essay](https://aeon.co/essays/how-does-the-deep-earth-see-humanity) on the deep Earth notes how thoroughly the Anthropocene framing flatters us — casting humanity as the planet's main character — when our actual reach is almost comically shallow: the deepest mine goes about 4 km down, the deepest borehole ever drilled (the Kola "Superdeep") just 12 km, against nearly 6,400 km to the core. The crust, mantle, core, ocean and atmosphere form a linked system that made us and does not need us. A useful humility to set beside the re-enchantment: mind may be everywhere in the thin film of life, and the rock beneath it turns regardless. (Jun 8)

**Consciousness, the closer you look** — the science writer Michael Pollan has a new book, *A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness*, whose animating finding is that the harder you stare at consciousness, the *weirder* it gets — the role of the body and raw feeling, the evidence nibbling at the edges of plant sentience, the live and unsettling possibility that consciousness may not be generated "inside" us in the way the folk picture assumes. A good companion to this issue's spine, and a reminder that the one phenomenon we know most intimately from the inside is the one we understand least from the outside. (2026)

**🃏 WILDCARD: another way to build a mind** — if you want proof that intelligence needn't look like ours, [Nautilus reports](https://nautil.us/how-the-rule-breaking-octopus-is-rewriting-the-evolution-of-intelligence-1282633) on how the octopus is rewriting the evolution of intelligence itself. The dominant story held that big brains evolve to manage big social groups; the octopus — solitary, short-lived, and staggeringly clever — took a different road entirely, and it doesn't keep its mind in its head: its neurons are distributed down its arms, so that each limb does something close to thinking on its own. A genuinely alien intelligence, no ocean-crossing required — just a reef, and a creature that solved the problem of mind on a completely different plan. (Jul 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

There is a temptation, reading all this together, to feel the world re-enchanting — purpose flooding back into biology, cognition turning up in the roots and the tide pools, the speech of whales resolving into grammar under our instruments — and to call it a homecoming, the correction of a cold century's error. Some of that is real and worth the joy. But the deeper lesson of putting these stories beside Nagel's bat is stranger and more disciplining than a homecoming. It is that mind, wherever it turns out to live, is the one thing in the universe that does not fully yield to the view from outside. We are getting extraordinarily good at the outside: the information-theoretic signature of agency, the behavioral repertoire of a plant, the combinatorial structure of a coda. Every one of those is a genuine and hard-won map. And every one of them stops at the same shoreline, the one Nagel drew — the place where description ends and *being* begins, where you can know everything about a creature except the only thing it knows for certain, which is what it is like to be itself. This is not a counsel of despair; it is a counsel of humility and, oddly, of respect. The right response to a mind you can chart but not enter is not to declare it solved, and not to declare it empty, but to treat the gap itself as sacred — to extend the benefit of the inside to things that plainly have one, even when, *especially* when, we cannot climb in to check. We are surrounded, it turns out, by insides we will never see. The task was never to abolish that mystery by mapping around it. It is to live decently among all the other ways there are to be someone.
