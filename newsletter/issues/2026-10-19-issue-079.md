# The Daily Compass #079 — The second mouse

*Monday, October 19, 2026 · ~14 minute read*

In 1979 two young programmers, Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston, released VisiCalc, the first electronic spreadsheet for personal computers. It was a genuine revolution: for the first time, a business could change one number and watch an entire financial model update in seconds, and many people bought an Apple II simply to run it. Within a few years VisiCalc had been overtaken by Lotus 1-2-3, which ran on the new IBM PC and did more. A decade later Lotus in turn was overtaken by Microsoft Excel. The company that invented the category did not win it, and the company that eventually dominated it was the third to arrive. There is an old saying that the early bird gets the worm, but the second mouse gets the cheese. This issue is about the second mouse: the evidence that being first is overrated, the art of intelligent imitation, and what it means for AI, where the gap between the leaders and their imitators is now measured in months.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The legend of the pioneer

**What happened.** By the late 1980s, "first-mover advantage" had become an article of faith in business strategy, supported by studies showing that the first companies into a market tended to hold the largest shares. In 1993 Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis challenged that belief in a paper with a pointed title: ["Pioneer Advantage: Marketing Logic or Marketing Legend?"](https://people.duke.edu/~moorman/Marketing-Strategy-Seminar-2015/Session%205/Golder%20and%20Tellis.pdf) (*Journal of Marketing Research*). Earlier studies, they argued, had a fatal flaw: they relied on surveys of surviving companies, so pioneers that had failed and disappeared were never counted, and firms that were not truly first often described themselves as pioneers. Golder and Tellis went back to the historical record instead, searching hundreds of periodicals and books to identify the real first entrants in some 50 product categories. Their findings reversed the conventional wisdom. Almost half of the true market pioneers failed, and the survivors held much lower average market shares than earlier studies had suggested. The long-term winners were usually "early market leaders", which entered on average about 13 years after the pioneers.

**Why it matters.** The study is a classic warning about survivorship bias: if you look only at the companies still standing, being first looks like a great idea. It also changes the strategic question. Pioneers bear the costs of educating customers, proving that a market exists and making the early mistakes, and later entrants can learn from all of it at little cost. What seems to matter most is not timing but vision and persistence: the willingness to invest heavily and keep improving once the market's shape becomes clear. For any company tempted to rush into a new category, whether streaming in a new country, a new AI product or a new financial service, the lesson is that arriving first is not a strategy. Arriving with the right product, at the moment the market is ready, and staying long enough to win is.

*Source: Golder & Tellis, Journal of Marketing Research 30(2) (1993). ~30 min (paper).*

### ◆ 2. The case for intelligent imitation

**What happened.** If pioneering is overrated, imitation may be underrated. The Harvard marketing professor Theodore Levitt made that argument in an essay titled "Innovative Imitation" in the mid-1960s, later reprinted in his book *The Marketing Imagination*. Much of what companies call innovation, he observed, is actually imitation, and given the cost and risk of original research, a sensible company should balance its own R&D with deliberate, well-executed copying of what works elsewhere. In 2010 Oded Shenkar of Ohio State University expanded the idea into a book, [*Copycats: How Smart Companies Use Imitation to Gain a Strategic Edge*](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/copycats/9781422160633/). Imitators, he argued, can save on research and marketing, avoid the pioneers' mistakes and often capture more of the value than the innovators themselves. Among his examples: Honda and Toyota waited while Chrysler proved the market for minivans, then entered with their own versions.

**Why it matters.** Shenkar's point is not that copying is easy. Good imitation is a skill: choosing the right model to copy, understanding why it works rather than mimicking its surface, and adapting it to a different market. That is especially relevant in regions where many successful companies have been built by bringing a proven model from elsewhere and fitting it to local customers, languages and payment habits. Done well, this is not a lesser form of strategy. It is often the smartest one, because the market has already done the expensive research. The risk is the one Shenkar warns about: copying the visible features of a successful company while missing the hidden system, the culture, the economics, the operations, that made it work.

*Source: Levitt, "Innovative Imitation", Harvard Business Review (1960s), in The Marketing Imagination; Shenkar, Copycats (2010). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. AI's months-long lead

**What happened.** In artificial intelligence, the race between pioneers and followers is being measured with unusual precision. Epoch AI, an independent research group that tracks AI capabilities, compares the best "open-weight" models, those whose underlying parameters are published for anyone to download and run, with the best closed models from the leading labs. In an [analysis published in May](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-eci-gap), it found that the top open models had lagged the state of the art by an average of about four months on its general capabilities index, slightly more than the three months it had estimated last October. An [update this month](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-cyber-eci-gap) on cybersecurity capabilities found a larger gap: the best open model scored roughly where the closed frontier had been six to seven months earlier. Epoch has also estimated that Chinese models, many of them open, have trailed the US frontier by about seven months on average since 2023.

**Why it matters.** A lead measured in months is a fragile thing, and it raises a question every AI company has to answer: if followers can match your best product within half a year, often at a fraction of the cost, what is your lasting advantage? The answers being tested include distribution and customer relationships, proprietary data, specialised products built on top of models, and the speed to keep moving the frontier. For companies that use AI rather than build it, the gap is good news. Capabilities that were exclusive and expensive become widely available within months, which favours the fast, intelligent adopter over the one that waits for perfect tools. The Golder and Tellis lesson applies here too: being first to a capability matters less than being the one who turns it into a product customers keep using.

*Source: Epoch AI Data Insights (May and Oct 2026). ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the spreadsheet that nobody owned** — one reason [VisiCalc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VisiCalc) could be imitated so quickly is that its inventors never patented the core idea; at the time, software was generally thought not to be patentable, and Bricklin has said they were advised that the chances of getting one were slim. The concept of the electronic spreadsheet was free for anyone to build on, and others did, adding features and moving to new computers faster than the original could adapt. Bricklin has been philosophical about it, and the industry he started became one of the most valuable in software. It is a reminder that the most important inventions often enrich their imitators more than their inventors, and that the world is usually better for it. (Wikipedia; Bricklin's own accounts)

**🃏 WILDCARD: one rule for every black hole** — black holes come in very different sizes, from a few times the mass of the Sun to billions of times. A study in *Nature Astronomy* led by Andrew Mummery of the Institute for Advanced Study and Adelle Goodwin of Curtin University suggests they nevertheless follow the same rule when they launch jets of matter into space. By studying events in which supermassive black holes tear apart passing stars, the team found that jets appear when the black hole's feeding rate drops to about 2% of its theoretical maximum, the same threshold already seen in much smaller black holes in our own galaxy. The sample is small, ten events with good enough data, but if it holds, a rule that spans a millionfold difference in size would be one of astronomy's [more elegant unifications](https://ias.edu/news/ias-scholar-reveals-universal-rule-black-hole-burps). (IAS / Curtin University, Sep–Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Isaac Newton wrote in a 1676 letter to Robert Hooke that if he had seen further, it was by standing on the shoulders of giants. The phrase was not even his; versions of it go back at least to the twelfth-century scholar Bernard of Chartres, who described his generation as dwarfs perched on the shoulders of giants, able to see more and farther not because of their own sharp sight but because they were lifted up by those who came before. It is fitting that the most famous line about intellectual borrowing was itself borrowed. Every field advances this way: someone goes first, pays the price, makes the mistakes, and others climb up and see a little further. There is no shame in being the second mouse, or the dwarf on the giant's shoulders. The only real mistake is to climb up and fail to look.
