# The Daily Compass #100 — The hundred days

*Monday, November 9, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On July 24, 1933, Franklin Roosevelt sat down for the third of his radio "fireside chats" and looked back on what he called "the crowding events of the hundred days which had been devoted to the starting of the wheels of the New Deal". It had been a remarkable period: an emergency banking law, a flood of new agencies and programmes, and a Congress that passed almost everything he sent it. But the number was a later tidying. Roosevelt had not set out to do anything in a hundred days; one book published that summer called the period "The World's Greatest Ninety-Nine Days". The phrase itself was borrowed from history: the original ["Hundred Days"](https://www.npr.org/2017/04/23/525310208/the-first-100-days-presidential-benchmark-goes-back-to-fdr-and-napoleon) were Napoleon's, the stretch in 1815 between his escape from Elba and his defeat at Waterloo. Yet the round number stuck, and ever since, new presidents, chief executives and managers have been judged on their "first hundred days", a deadline nobody chose and everybody now uses. Round numbers have that power. They are arbitrary, but they change what people aim for, what they avoid and how they judge themselves. This is the hundredth issue of The Daily Compass, so it seems a fitting moment to ask why.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The lines that bend behaviour

**What happened.** Some of the cleanest evidence that round numbers matter comes from sport. In ["Reference-Dependent Preferences: Evidence from Marathon Runners"](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2417) (*Management Science*, 2017), Eric Allen, Patricia Dechow, Devin Pope and George Wu analysed nearly 10 million marathon finishing times. The times bunch sharply just under round numbers such as three, three and a half and four hours, and split times show why: runners near a round-number target speed up at the end. In ["Round Numbers as Goals"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/students-are-more-likely-to-retake-the-sat-if-their-score-ends-with-90.html) (*Psychological Science*, 2011), Pope and Uri Simonsohn found that baseball players change their behaviour late in the season to finish with a batting average of .300 rather than .299, and that students who score just below a round number on the SAT are more likely to retake it, even though a few points make little difference to admissions. Companies do the same with their accounts. In ["Earnings Management to Exceed Thresholds"](https://rzeckhauser.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/earnings-management-exceed-thresholds) (*Journal of Business*, 1999), François Degeorge, Jayendu Patel and Richard Zeckhauser found that reported earnings show too few small losses and too many tiny profits, as firms push to clear three lines: zero, last year's result and analysts' forecasts. Regulators draw lines too, and firms respond. In ["Firm Size Distortions and the Productivity Distribution"](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/66765) (*American Economic Review*, 2016), Luis Garicano, Claire Lelarge and John Van Reenen showed that the number of French firms drops sharply at 50 employees, the point at which a raft of labour regulations applies. Many firms stay at 49 rather than grow, at a cost they estimate at about 3.4% of GDP in their baseline model.

**Why it matters.** A threshold is never just a measurement. Once people know where the line is, they start managing to it, and the result is bunching: a pile-up just on the right side of the line and a shortage just beyond it. For leaders, this has two implications. The first is to design thresholds with care. Every bonus target, approval limit, sales quota and size-based rule creates its own bunching, and some of that behaviour (the sales pushed into the last week of the quarter, the project kept just under the sign-off limit, the team kept just below the headcount that triggers more process) costs more than it is worth. The second is to read your own data with suspicion. When results cluster just above a target, it may be a sign of effort, but it may also be a sign of timing and presentation. The economist Paul Oyer found exactly this pattern in fiscal year-end sales in 1998. The most useful question to ask of any target is not only whether it was hit, but what people did to hit it.

*Source: Allen, Dechow, Pope & Wu, Management Science 63(6) (2017); Pope & Simonsohn, Psychological Science 22(1) (2011); Degeorge, Patel & Zeckhauser, J. Business 72(1) (1999); Garicano, Lelarge & Van Reenen, AER 106(11) (2016); Oyer, QJE 113(1) (1998). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Fresh starts and small wins

**What happened.** Round numbers do not only distort; they also motivate. In ["The Fresh Start Effect"](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf) (*Management Science*, 2014), Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis found that Google searches for "diet", gym visits and commitments to personal goals all rise after "temporal landmarks": the start of a new week, month, year or semester, a birthday or a holiday. Such moments, they argue, let people draw a line under their past selves and start again. Decade birthdays seem to matter most. In a [study in *PNAS*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4260584) (2014), Adam Alter and Hal Hershfield found that people aged 29, 39, 49 and so on reported thinking more about the meaning of their lives, and were over-represented among first-time marathon runners, a healthy response to an approaching round number, though they found less healthy ones too. Progress towards a milestone can also be motivating in itself. In a classic study of a café loyalty card (*Journal of Marketing Research*, 2006), Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng found that customers bought coffee more often as they approached a free one, and that a 12-stamp card with two stamps already filled in was completed faster than a 10-stamp card, even though both required ten purchases. But the effects have limits. In a recent large trial in England, the Education Endowment Foundation and the Behavioural Insights Team [sent parents messages](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/bitup-updating-parents-on-number-of-school-days-missed-2022-23-trial) about their children's missed school days at the start of each half-term, explicitly to use the fresh-start effect. Attendance improved, but only slightly: about a fifth of a day per pupil.

**Why it matters.** Milestones are useful tools for leaders, but weaker than the folklore suggests. The research supports a few practical habits. Use natural landmarks, such as the start of a quarter, a new year or the anniversary of a team, to launch changes, because people are more open to new behaviour at those moments. Make progress visible: Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer's analysis of [nearly 12,000 daily diary entries](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html) from knowledge workers found that making progress in meaningful work was the single most powerful driver of a good day, even though managers they surveyed ranked it last among possible motivators. Breaking a long project into milestones gives people more of those days. And do not over-trust the round-number deadline. The [executive search firm Russell Reynolds](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2025/12/02/beyond-the-first-100-days-rhetoric-how-to-ensure-the-long-term-success-of-new-ceos/) argues, from its work with chief executives, that a real leadership transition takes 12 to 18 months, not 100 days. The first hundred days are a useful moment to listen and set direction, but the milestone that matters is whether the changes last.

*Source: Dai, Milkman & Riis, Management Science 60(10) (2014); Alter & Hershfield, PNAS 111(48) (2014); Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, JMR 43 (2006); EEF/BIT; Amabile & Kramer, HBR (May 2011); Russell Reynolds via Harvard Law School Forum (Dec 2025). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. The round numbers of AI

**What happened.** AI has its own relationship with round numbers, in its models, its tests and its rules. Start with the models. In ["Large Language Models Are Bad Dice Players"](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1051/) (ACL 2026), Minda Zhao, Yilun Du and Mengyu Wang tested 11 language models on drawing random numbers from 15 statistical distributions. Asked for many samples in one go, the models passed a median of only 7% of the tests; asked for one sample at a time, 10 of the 11 passed none. The failures carried into practical tasks, such as generating multiple-choice tests with answers spread evenly across positions. The authors conclude that the models lack a working internal sense of randomness, and that tasks needing it should use external tools. Next, the tests. In ["When AI Benchmarks Plateau"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16763) (ICML 2026), Mubashara Akhtar, Anka Reuel and colleagues studied 60 benchmarks used to evaluate language models and found that nearly half were highly saturated: they could no longer tell the best models apart. Finally, the rules. Regulators have drawn round-number lines to decide which models count as "frontier": the EU AI Act presumes systemic risk above 10^25 operations of training compute, while California's new law uses 10^26. A recent [policy paper from the think tank ITIF](https://itif.org/publications/2026/08/28/ais-frontier-is-moving-its-legal-definition-should-too/) argues that such fixed thresholds are going stale as technology improves. Even the most famous AI milestone, "artificial general intelligence", has proved hard to use: Microsoft and OpenAI's partnership once depended on when OpenAI declared it, but later amendments [tied key terms to the calendar year 2030](https://the-decoder.com/microsoft-gains-agi-rights-in-new-openai-deal-expert-panel-to-rule-on-milestone/) instead.

**Why it matters.** The pattern is the same one seen in marathons and earnings reports. A round number is a convenient focal point, but a poor measure of what we actually care about. Benchmarks are retired not when models hit a symbolic score, but when the test stops discriminating. Compute thresholds are easy to measure, but compute is only a rough proxy for capability and risk, and the line that made sense in 2023 may make little sense in 2027. And when two of the world's most sophisticated companies found an "AGI" milestone too vague to contract on, they fell back on a date. For leaders, the lesson is to be careful about the milestones that govern AI decisions in their own organisations: "80% accuracy", "human-level", "10% productivity gain". Choose them deliberately, revisit them as the technology moves, and pair any round-number target with a description of what it is supposed to represent. And where real randomness or fairness matters, such as sampling, testing or assigning, do not trust a language model's sense of chance.

*Source: Zhao, Du & Wang, ACL 2026; Akhtar, Reuel et al., ICML 2026 (arXiv 2602.16763); ITIF (Aug 28, 2026); The Decoder (Oct 2025). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the four-minute mile** — on May 6, 1954, at a cinder track in Oxford, the medical student Roger Bannister ran a mile in 3 minutes 59.4 seconds, the first person to break the round-number barrier. Just 46 days later, the Australian John Landy ran 3:57.9 in Finland. The story is often told as proof that a psychological barrier, once broken, collapses for everyone. Historians are more careful: the idea that four minutes was widely thought "physically impossible" was largely a sportswriters' myth, which Bannister himself disputed, and the barrier was already falling as training became more scientific. What the round number did was focus attention. Several of the world's best milers were chasing the same arbitrary line at the same time, and that competition, more than any change in belief, is why it fell. ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/five-things-know-about-roger-bannister-first-person-break-four-minute-mile-180968344/))

**🃏 WILDCARD: bees that understand nothing** — the idea of zero took humans thousands of years to develop. Honeybees seem to grasp something like it. In a study in *Science* (2018), Scarlett Howard, Adrian Dyer and colleagues trained bees to choose cards with fewer shapes on them. When the bees were then shown, for the first time, a card with [no shapes at all](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180607141031.htm), they treated it as having fewer than one, placing "nothing" at the low end of the number line. Bees joined a small club of animals, including monkeys, parrots and dolphins, shown to do this; later work found neurons in crows that respond most strongly to an empty set. (Science, Jun 2018)

**A hundred thousand hundreds** — in September, Japan's health ministry reported that the number of people in the country aged 100 or older had passed [100,000 for the first time](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02902/), reaching 107,677, almost nine in ten of them women. When records began in 1963, there were 153. It is a round number about a round number, and a reminder that the milestones societies plan around, from retirement ages to pensions, were set for lives much shorter than many now live. (Japan Ministry of Health via nippon.com, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

T. S. Eliot finished *Little Gidding*, the last of his *Four Quartets*, in 1942, in the middle of a war, and he wrote it about a small religious community in rural England and about the circling of time and history. Near the end, he wrote lines that have been quoted at graduations, retirements and anniversaries ever since: "What we call the beginning is often the end / And to make an end is to make a beginning. / The end is where we start from." Round numbers invite us to treat moments as either endings or beginnings: the hundredth day, the fortieth birthday, the year 2000. Eliot's point is that they are always both. A milestone is worth marking not because the number means anything, but because it gives us a reason to look back at what we have learned and forward at what we will try next. That is as true of a hundredth issue as of anything else. Thank you for reading. The next hundred start tomorrow.
