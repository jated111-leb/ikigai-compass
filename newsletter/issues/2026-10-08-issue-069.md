# The Daily Compass #069 — Where the money goes

*Thursday, October 8, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Every industry eventually learns that the thing it makes is getting cheaper. Mainframes became PCs, PCs became commodities, music became a stream, television became an app, and now intelligence itself, or at least the machine kind, is falling in price faster than almost anything ever measured. The natural reaction is alarm: if the product is becoming a commodity, where will the profits come from? The better question, and the one this issue is about, is where the profits are going. Money rarely vanishes from an industry when one part of it gets cheap. It moves, to the next scarce thing in the chain, often to a company that saw the shift coming while the incumbent was busy defending yesterday's margin. Today's briefs trace that movement through a classic essay on how profit migrates, a new paper on who really pays when big companies give things away, and a fresh argument about what AI agents will do to every app, including the ones that sell films and series for a living.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Skate to where the money will be

**What happened.** In the early 1980s IBM dominated computing. It owned around 70% of the mainframe market and took around 95% of its profits. When it built the personal computer, it outsourced two components, the processor to Intel and the operating system to Microsoft, and those two suppliers went on to capture most of the industry's profits while IBM entered a decade of decline. In ["Skate to Where the Money Will Be"](https://hbr.org/2001/11/skate-to-where-the-money-will-be) (*Harvard Business Review*, November 2001), Clayton Christensen, Michael Raynor and Matthew Verlinden turned that story into a theory. When a product is not yet good enough for its customers, the money goes to whoever integrates the whole thing, because performance depends on how the parts fit together. Once the product becomes more than good enough, customers stop paying for further improvements, the product becomes modular and standardized, and assembling it turns into a commodity business. Profit then moves to the parts that are still not good enough, often the components inside, where tight integration still matters. Christensen and Raynor later called this the "law of conservation of attractive profits": when one stage of a value chain becomes commoditized, attractive profits tend to reappear at an adjacent stage.

**Why it matters.** The essay's sharpest warning is about how incumbents respond. When a firm's main product commoditizes, investors and managers push it to lift returns by shedding asset-heavy, low-margin units, and those are sometimes exactly the parts where profit is about to move. The theory gives a practical question to ask about any business: which part of our chain is still "not good enough" for customers, and therefore still worth paying a premium for? For a streaming company, the answer is not obvious and keeps changing. Film libraries have become abundant. Sports rights, local-language originals, the billing relationship, and the trusted distribution partner may be the parts that still command a premium. The task is to stand where the money is going, not where it was.

*Source: Christensen, Raynor & Verlinden, Harvard Business Review (Nov 2001); developed in The Innovator's Solution (2003). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 2. Who pays for "free"

**What happened.** In 2002 the software entrepreneur Joel Spolsky wrote a short essay, ["Strategy Letter V,"](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/06/12/strategy-letter-v/) that gave the technology industry one of its most useful rules: commoditize your complements. Demand for a product rises when the things used with it get cheaper, so smart companies try to drive the price of their complements toward zero, often by giving them away. That, Spolsky argued, explains why large companies pay engineers to write open-source software. A new working paper puts the idea to an empirical test. In ["Commoditize Your Complement: Who Bears the Cost of Free Software?"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6502879), Peter Y. Won of the University of Utah studies 278 open-source releases by big technology firms. When a release happens, the firms that sell into the market being commoditized lose about 0.71% of their market value. The releasing firm gains about 1.37%. The firm's direct competitors barely react. Afterwards, the paper reports, the affected firms lose sales and cut back on investment, research and patenting.

**Why it matters.** The finding sharpens Spolsky's rule in an uncomfortable direction. The cost of "free" does not fall mainly on the giveaway firm's rivals, which is what most people would assume. It falls on the smaller companies whose whole business sat in the layer that was just made free. That is a pattern worth recognizing from the other side. Many firms, including most content and media businesses, sit on top of platforms (app stores, operating systems, smart TVs, telecom networks, and now AI assistants) whose owners have every reason to make the layer above or below them cheap. The defensive lesson is to know which layer you are in, to ask whether a bigger player would benefit from commoditizing it, and to build value in places they cannot easily give away. The paper is an unrefereed working paper, and the abstract is what is publicly visible, so its numbers should be read as early evidence rather than settled fact.

*Source: Spolsky, Joel on Software (Jun 12, 2002), ~10 min; Won, SSRN working paper (2026), ~45 min.*

### ◆ 3. The app behind the agent

**What happened.** The newest version of this story is about AI agents. In ["Apps, Agents, and Aggregation"](https://stratechery.com/2026/apps-agents-and-aggregation/) (Sep 28), Ben Thompson extends his decade-old "aggregation theory," which holds that in the internet era the biggest profits go to whoever owns the relationship with users and can make suppliers compete for their attention. His new claim is that AI agents are "the ultimate Aggregators." If you can simply ask an assistant to do something, the apps that used to do it become a means to an end rather than a destination. His example is personal: he asked a Meta agent to organize recipe videos he had saved on Instagram. The first result was an unusable document, but a few minutes later he had a working private interface for browsing them, built on the spot. Interfaces like that, he suggests, are becoming disposable. On his account, the biggest prize in technology is now providing the agent that sits in front of everything else.

**Why it matters.** If Thompson is right, the agent becomes the new layer that is "not good enough" and therefore worth paying for, in brief 1's terms, and many of today's apps become the commoditized complement, in brief 2's terms. That should concern any consumer business whose advantage is its app. A streaming service that people open by habit is in a strong position. A streaming service that an assistant chooses on the user's behalf, by comparing catalogues and prices, is in a much weaker one. The best defense is what agents cannot easily substitute: exclusive rights, a strong brand people ask for by name, and ownership of the customer and the bill. This week offered a regional illustration. India's JioHotstar entered the Middle East and North Africa [not with its own app but as a hub inside Starzplay](https://www.business-standard.com/entertainment/jiohotstar-enters-middle-east-north-africa-in-partnership-with-starzplay-126100700243_1.html), bundled into Starzplay's plans. A large content owner chose to rent a regional aggregator's subscription and billing rather than build its own. Thompson's essay is a single argument resting on a short personal experiment, and agents are far from reliable. But its direction is clear, and the strategic question it raises is worth asking now: in a world of agents, who owns your customer?

*Source: Thompson, Stratechery (Sep 28, 2026), paywalled, ~15 min; JioHotstar–Starzplay announcement (Oct 2026).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the razor that wasn't free** — every business-school student learns the "razors and blades" model: give away the handle, profit from the blades, as King Gillette supposedly did. In ["The Razors-and-Blades Myth(s)"](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclrev/vol78/iss1/12/) (*University of Chicago Law Review*, 2011), the legal scholar Randal Picker showed that the story is largely wrong. While Gillette's patents protected it, from 1904 to 1921, it sold its razor sets at a premium price of $5 and worked to stop dealers from discounting them; cheaper rivals held the low end. Only as the patents expired did Gillette launch a low-priced older-style razor, while introducing a new patented model at the traditional high price. The lesson is subtler than the myth. A company can afford to make one part cheap only when it controls another part that customers cannot get elsewhere. Without that, giving away the handle just gives away the business. (Picker, 2011)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the gun under the minefield** — in November 2023, South Korean soldiers checking a suspected minefield on Mount Geumseong in Naju, ahead of a hiking-trail project, found a small bronze tube about 10 centimeters under the soil beneath a rock face. After nearly three years of study, Korean heritage researchers announced last month that it is the [Hwangja Hwatong](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/lifestyle/koreanheritage/20260921/oldest-known-gunpowder-weapon-in-korea-discovered-near-minefield), the oldest known gunpowder weapon in Korea. The hand cannon, about 24 centimeters long and weighing roughly a kilogram, carries a 28-character inscription about its manufacture. It dates to either 1377 or 1437, at least 118 years older than the previous record holder. A weapon of war, found by soldiers clearing the weapons of a later war. (Korea Times, Sep 2026)

**The plunging price of thought** — the one AI item this week, and the data behind today's theme. The research group Epoch AI estimates in ["The plunging price of thought"](https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought) that the cost of reaching a fixed level of AI performance on its benchmarks has fallen by about 47% per quarter since 2023, roughly a 13-fold drop each year, faster than the historical declines in DNA sequencing, computing, lithium batteries or electricity. Newly achieved capabilities get cheaper fastest, at about 66% per quarter at first, slowing to around 32% two years later. When something gets cheaper that quickly, profits from selling it are unlikely to last. Brief 1 suggests they are already moving elsewhere: to chips, to data, to distribution, and perhaps to whoever owns the agent. (Epoch AI, Sep 22, 2026)

**The pass that closed** — Sling TV has quietly stopped selling its one-, three- and seven-day passes, which since August 2025 had offered short-term access to channels including ESPN for as little as $4.99 a day. Disney and Warner Bros. Discovery had both sued over the passes, and Disney failed to win an injunction, but [Sling has now withdrawn them](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/sling-tv-stops-offering-day-pass-espn-tnt/) without giving a reason. It is a small case study in today's theme: the most extreme form of unbundling ran into owners of scarce content, especially sports, who have every reason to protect the bundle. Whoever controls the scarce thing decides how small the unit of sale can be. (TheDesk, Oct 2, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In the third act of Oscar Wilde's [*Lady Windermere's Fan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Windermere%27s_Fan) (1892), a group of men trade epigrams late at night. "What is a cynic?" asks Cecil Graham. Lord Darlington answers with the line everyone remembers: "A man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing." Graham's reply is less famous and just as sharp: a sentimentalist, he says, is a man who "sees an absurd value in everything, and doesn't know the market price of any single thing." Between them the two lines describe the two ways businesses get commoditization wrong. The cynic sees only the falling price and concludes that nothing in the industry is worth anything anymore, missing the value that has simply moved elsewhere. The sentimentalist clings to the old product's worth, refusing to see that the market has stopped paying for it. Christensen's theory, Spolsky's rule and Thompson's warning all ask for a third stance: knowing the price, which tells you what has become ordinary, and knowing the value, which tells you what is still rare. The same discipline applies to a career. Skills, like products, become commodities, and the work that pays is the work that is not yet good enough to be done by anyone, or anything, else. The question for a company and for a person is the same: where is the money going, and are you skating there?
