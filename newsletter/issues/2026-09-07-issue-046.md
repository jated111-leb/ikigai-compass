# The Daily Compass #046 — Show your work

*Monday, September 7, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

"Show your work" is the oldest demand in education, and underneath it sits an entire theory of trust: that a conclusion is only as good as the reasoning you can follow to reach it, and that to believe an answer you must be able to *watch the thinking*. For all of human history this held for machines too — a calculation you couldn't audit was a calculation you couldn't trust. This week, two events detonated that premise from opposite directions, in the same seven days. A new frontier model started reasoning in a way that, by design, *cannot* show its work — its thinking happens in a place no human can read. And an AI produced a proof of one of mathematics' deepest theorems so vast that no human will *ever* read it — thirteen million lines — yet every single line has been mechanically verified as correct. The reasoning went dark and the proof went perfect in the same instant, and between them they mark the end of one age of trust and the beginning of another: the slow migration from *following the argument* to *checking the result.*

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The mind that won't show its work

**What happened.** OpenAI shipped GPT-6 "Astra," and the interesting thing isn't the capability jump — it's the architecture. As reported by [Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/04/2026/openais-new-astra-model-has-private-thoughts) and [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/02/openais-new-reasoning-technique-alarms-ai-safety-experts/), Astra uses "recurrent depth": instead of reasoning by emitting a visible chain of word-tokens, it loops internally, iterating on its own hidden state in a latent space, and only afterward emits a *paraphrased summary* of what it concluded. The raw reasoning is never rendered into readable language at all. Safety researchers flagged the consequence immediately: chain-of-thought monitoring — reading a model's step-by-step "thinking out loud" to catch it scheming or drifting, the single most relied-upon interpretability tool of the past two years — becomes not harder but *structurally impossible.* You cannot monitor a thought that was never put into words.

**Why it matters.** This is the exact inversion of "show your work," and it produces a genuinely new and unsettling shape: a model that may be simultaneously *more* aligned and *less* monitorable. The reasoning that made these systems auditable was, it turns out, a temporary artifact of how they happened to be built — text in, text out, thinking visible in between — and Astra shows it was never load-bearing to capability. Per reporting on UK AI Safety Institute testing, the window in which Astra can act on an unverbalized internal reasoning process stretched to roughly half an hour, up from a few minutes in the prior generation — a large expansion of the zone where the machine is doing something no human can read as it happens. We spent two years reassuring ourselves that we could always just *look at what it's thinking.* This week the lights went off in that room, and they are not coming back on.

*Source: Semafor, TechCrunch, OpenAI system card (Sep 2–4). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The proof no human can read

**What happened.** In the same week, from the opposite pole, [Anthropic reported](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formalizing-fermats-last-theorem) that Claude — working largely autonomously over about eleven days — produced the first end-to-end, machine-checked formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem in the proof language Lean. The scale is difficult to hold in the mind: roughly *thirteen million lines* of Lean, some 29,500 intermediate theorems proved along the way, more than five times the size of Mathlib (the entire community-built library of formalized mathematics). It completed the last unproven entry on Freek Wiedijk's twenty-year-old "100 theorems" formalization list. Kevin Buzzard, the mathematician who leads the human effort to do exactly this, reviewed it and called it "an extraordinary autoformalization achievement" that proves the theorem "with no assumptions other than the axioms of mathematics."

**Why it matters.** No human being will ever read those thirteen million lines — and it doesn't matter, which is the whole point. A formal proof in Lean is checkable by a small, simple, trusted program: you don't have to follow the reasoning, you only have to trust the checker, and the checker can confirm every step mechanically. So brief 1's problem has, sitting right beside it, brief 2's answer. Astra shows that reasoning is becoming unreadable; Lean shows that *reading was never the only route to trust.* When you cannot follow the argument, you can still verify the artifact — if the artifact is cast in a form a machine can independently check. This is the deepest shift the two stories jointly announce: trust is migrating off the human act of comprehension and onto mechanical verification. The unreadable mind (Astra) and the unreadable-but-unforgeable proof (Lean) are the two halves of a single new world, in which "I understood it" quietly stops being the standard and "it checks out" takes its place.

*Source: Anthropic (Sep 4). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. What is the mathematician for now?

**What happened.** The most human document of the week is a blog post by the man who got scooped. Kevin Buzzard has spent years, with EPSRC funding running to 2029, leading a painstaking community effort to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean by hand — and then an AI did a version of it in eleven days. His response, ["FLT: Anthropic has beaten me to it,"](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/09/04/flt-anthropic-has-beaten-me-to-it/) is not defensiveness but something rarer: a domain expert metabolizing displacement in real time, and turning it into the right question. If autonomous formalization of something this deep is possible *now*, he notes, then machine-checking huge swaths of the mathematical literature — and rigorously refereeing new AI-generated math — is suddenly on the table. The craft he built his career on just changed under him, mid-stride.

**Why it matters.** Buzzard's question — what is the mathematician *for* once formalization is cheap? — is the human form of this whole issue, and it has a surprisingly old answer. G. H. Hardy, in *A Mathematician's Apology* (1940), insisted that mathematics is not fundamentally about establishing that theorems are true; it is about which truths are *beautiful*, which are *worth* proving, which patterns are deep enough to deserve a life's attention. A machine can now, increasingly, supply the verification — the grinding, checkable *that-it-is-so.* What it cannot yet supply is the taste that decides *what is worth asking*, the judgment of significance, the sense (as issue #045 argued about intelligence generally) that the value was never in the raw computing but in the choosing. The mathematician's seat doesn't disappear when the proving is automated. It moves — from proving the theorem to knowing which theorem was worth the proof, and why it matters that it's true. That judgment is the last thing to show its work, because it was never work in the first place. It was meaning.

*Source: Buzzard, Xena Project (Sep 4). ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — Simon Willison's hands-on with Astra quietly documents the economic story under the architectural one — ["GPT-6 Astra."](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/3/gpt6-astra/) As "reasoning effort" becomes a dial you turn, the *same* prompt can swing wildly in price: he clocked one maximum-effort render at roughly 63 cents against about 1.5 cents for the same task on a lighter model — a ~40x per-task spread driven purely by how hard you let it think. The comfortable assumption that inference only ever gets cheaper is dead; test-time compute is now the dominant cost variable, and "how much thinking did you pay for?" is the new line item. (Sep 3)

**The hidden rule in the black box** — Matt Levine on a beautiful mechanism-design morality tale — ["Amazon Charged the First Price."](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-01/amazon-charged-the-first-price) Amazon's ad marketplace was sold to advertisers as a Vickrey second-price auction — win, but pay only just above the runner-up, so honest bidding is safe. The FTC alleges Amazon quietly ran a *first-price* auction instead, charging winners their full bid — overcharging some 1.2 million businesses by more than $20 billion since 2019. The elegant public mechanism and the actual behavior of the black box were two different things, and no bidder could see the gap — brief 1's unreadable reasoning, wearing a green eyeshade. (Sep 1)

**Fear isn't data** — a bracing correction from Tyler Cowen, flagging new work (Hartley, Jolevski, Melo & Moore): 35.9% of US workers had used generative AI by the end of 2025, and the average worker now estimates a ~20% chance of losing their job to AI within two years — yet across AI-exposed occupations there is still *no statistically significant* rise in layoffs or fall in job postings, and even small *positive* wage effects. The dread is real and the disruption, so far, mostly isn't. Worth taping to the monitor next to every AGI-jobs headline. ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/09/more-optimistic-results-on-ai-and-job-markets.html), Sep 1)

**What actually transforms** — Benedict Evans deflates the "everyone becomes a software builder, apps are dead" story — ["AI, tools and transformation."](https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/9/3/ai-tools-and-transformation) Being able to spin up a little tool in five minutes doesn't change how a company works, because most people don't want to build tools; they want the work done. Real platform shifts, he argues, never came from automating the tasks we already had — they came from the things that *weren't previously possible* and that nobody thought to ask for. A useful compass for telling a genuine transformation from a faster horse. (Sep 3)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Four-Color Theorem** — Kenneth Appel & Wolfgang Haken, 1976. The first time a major mathematical theorem was proved with essential help from a computer: to show that any map needs only four colors, the pair reduced the problem to 1,936 special cases and had a machine grind through them across more than a thousand hours of computer time. The result detonated a philosophical fight that has never fully closed — because the case-checking was "too protracted for human inspection," some mathematicians refused to accept it as a genuine proof at all. If no person can survey the argument end to end, is it knowledge or just a very confident rumor? Why now: that fifty-year-old controversy is this week's headline, scaled up a millionfold. Fermat's Last Theorem in thirteen million lines of Lean is the Four-Color Theorem's question made total — and the answer the field slowly reached in 1976 (trust the verification, not the reading) is the answer the whole culture is about to have to learn. ([AMS](https://blogs.ams.org/mathgradblog/2014/06/29/color-theorem/), 1976)

**🃏 WILDCARD: what is math even for** — fittingly, Quanta filed a dispatch from the International Congress of Mathematicians on exactly this question — "What Is Math For in the Age of AI?" — a live panel of working mathematicians asking what they actually *treasure* about doing mathematics once machines can prove and check. The answers keep circling back not to the theorems but to the understanding — the "aha," the reframing, the beauty Hardy defended — which is precisely the part no checker can supply. A hopeful counter-melody: the machine can take the proving; the wondering is still ours. ([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/), Sep 3)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

For most of the history of knowing things, understanding and trusting were the same act. You believed the proof because you could follow it; you trusted the reasoning because you could watch it unfold; "show your work" and "I believe you" were two ends of one gesture. What this week quietly announced is that those two things are coming apart, permanently. On one side, machines whose reasoning we can no longer read — not because they hide it, but because it never passes through words we could have read in the first place. On the other, proofs we also cannot read, that are nonetheless more certain than anything a human hand ever wrote, because a dumb and trustworthy checker has confirmed every step. Comprehension and verification, married since Euclid, are filing for divorce. This is not, on its own, a tragedy: the Lean checker is a genuinely new and marvelous kind of trust, and a civilization that can mechanically verify its own mathematics is stronger, not weaker, for it. But it asks something of us that we are not yet in the habit of doing. When you can no longer follow the reasoning, you have to get very serious, very fast, about the checkers — about what independently verifies the result, who built the verifier, and whether the standard it checks against is the one you actually care about. And you have to hold on, harder than ever, to the one move that was never mechanizable and never will be: not proving that a thing is true, but judging that it was worth proving. The machines can now show us the answer without showing their work. Our job, increasingly, is to know which questions were worth asking — and to keep asking them ourselves.
