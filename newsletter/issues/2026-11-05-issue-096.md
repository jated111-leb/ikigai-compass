# The Daily Compass #096 — The box already ticked

*Thursday, November 5, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

At five o'clock on the morning of Sunday, September 3, 1967, every vehicle in Sweden stopped, carefully crossed to the other side of the road, and waited. When traffic resumed, the whole country drove on the right. Sweden had driven on the left for centuries, even though its neighbours drove on the right and most Swedish cars already had the steering wheel on the left, so that drivers had to switch sides at the Norwegian and Finnish borders. The case for changing was overwhelming, and the public rejected it anyway: in a referendum in 1955, about 83% voted to keep driving on the left. Parliament overrode the vote in 1963, and the country then spent four years preparing for *Dagen H*, "H Day", from *högertrafik*, right-hand traffic. Some 350,000 road signs were changed, about 20,000 of them in Stockholm alone. In the days after the switch, accidents actually fell, as drivers took extra care, before returning to their usual levels. The episode is a near-perfect illustration of the power of defaults. People stick with the existing option even when they can see that another is better, and moving them takes far more than a good argument. This issue is about that power: how the pre-selected option shapes savings, health and markets, why a default can be worth $20 billion a year, and what happens when AI becomes both the default and the one choosing.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The option nobody chose

**What happened.** In 1988 the economists William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser gave the phenomenon a name. In ["Status Quo Bias in Decision Making"](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/status_quo_bias_in_decision_making.pdf) (*Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*), they showed that people choose an option far more often when it is presented as the status quo, in experiments and in real decisions about health and retirement plans. The evidence since has been remarkable. In ["The Power of Suggestion"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2001), Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a large American company that began enrolling new employees in its retirement plan automatically, while letting them opt out. Participation among new hires reached 86%, against 37% for a comparable group who had to sign up themselves, and most of the automatically enrolled stayed at the default contribution rate and fund. In ["Do Defaults Save Lives?"](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/health/can-defaults-save-lives-the-power-of-default-options-on-life-saving-decisions) (*Science*, 2003), Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein showed that consent to organ donation was about 12% in Germany, where people must opt in, and 99.98% in neighbouring Austria, where they are presumed to consent unless they opt out, though actual donation rates differ far less. In a study at Rutgers University published in *JAMA* in 2010, Gretchen Chapman and colleagues gave staff either a pre-assigned flu-shot appointment they could change or an invitation to book one: vaccination rose from 33% to 45%. A meta-analysis by Jon Jachimowicz, Shannon Duncan, Elke Weber and Eric Johnson in *Behavioural Public Policy* (2019), covering 58 studies and more than 73,000 participants, found that defaults have a substantial average effect, but one that varies enormously from setting to setting.

**Why it matters.** Defaults work for three reasons: they require no effort, they are read as an implicit recommendation, and they become the reference point against which other options feel like losses. A new study of Germany's pension system, by Tabea Bucher-Koenen, Luisa Wallossek and Joachim Winter ([ZEW, 2026](https://www.zew.de/PU96181)), adds a fourth: defaults work best on people who do not notice them. When the country automatically enrolled low-income "mini-job" workers in its public pension, participation rose by 23 percentage points, and the effect was strongest among workers who did not know their own enrolment status. For leaders, this means every default in a product, a policy or an organisation is a decision, whether or not anyone made it deliberately. The useful exercise is to list the defaults that shape customers' and employees' behaviour, from the pre-selected plan to the standard meeting length, and to ask of each one: who chose this, and would we choose it again today?

*Source: Samuelson & Zeckhauser, JRU 1 (1988); Madrian & Shea, QJE 116(4) (2001); Johnson & Goldstein, Science 302 (2003); Chapman et al., JAMA 304(1) (2010); Jachimowicz et al., BPP 3(2) (2019); Bucher-Koenen, Wallossek & Winter, ZEW DP 26-014 (2026). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. The $20 billion default

**What happened.** The most valuable default in the world is the search box. Court documents unsealed in America's antitrust case against Google showed that it paid Apple [$20 billion in 2022](https://9to5mac.com/2024/05/01/google-paid-apple-search-safari/) to be the default search engine in Safari, in exchange for a share of the resulting advertising revenue. In his remedies ruling in September 2025, Judge Amit Mehta barred Google from exclusive distribution deals but allowed it to keep paying for default placement. Both sides have appealed, and the case is now before the federal appeals court in Washington. Regulators elsewhere have tried a different tool: forcing people to choose. Under the EU's Digital Markets Act, smartphones must show a browser choice screen. Mozilla says Firefox has been picked millions of times. But an analysis published in October by the International Center for Law & Economics, [using browser-share data from 11 countries](https://laweconcenter.org/resources/we-measured-europes-browser-revolution-bring-a-magnifying-glass/), found that the combined share of the default browsers on mobile changed by less than one percentage point. A field experiment by Hunt Allcott, Juan Camilo Castillo, Matthew Gentzkow, Leon Musolff and Tobias Salz, ["Sources of Market Power in Web Search"](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/33410.html) (NBER, 2025), explains why. Requiring Google users to make an active choice of search engine raised Bing's share by only about one percentage point. But when users were paid $10 to try Bing for two weeks, 58% switched, and a third of them kept using it afterwards, having discovered it was better than they thought.

**Why it matters.** For incumbents, the lesson is that a default is a strategic asset worth paying for, and that defending it may matter more than winning head-to-head comparisons. For challengers, the evidence is sobering but useful. A choice screen, a comparison page or a better argument barely moves people off a default, because inertia is not mainly about information. What does move them is experience: getting them to actually use the alternative, for long enough to update their beliefs. That is why free trials, bundling, migration tools and partnerships that make a product the default somewhere new are often worth more than marketing that asks people to make a choice. Growth, in many markets, is less about persuading people to switch than about becoming the thing they never have to choose.

*Source: US v. Google court filings, via 9to5Mac (May 2024); ICLE / Truth on the Market (Oct 13, 2026); Allcott, Castillo, Gentzkow, Musolff & Salz, NBER WP 33410 (2025). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. When the default chooses for you

**What happened.** AI is now part of the battle over defaults. On September 23 Britain's Competition and Markets Authority [published strengthened proposals](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cma-strengthens-proposals-allowing-people-choice-over-their-search-service) requiring Google to show users of Android phones and the Chrome browser a choice of search provider when they set up a device, and then to prompt them to choose again every year. For the first time, AI assistants that meet the regulator's technical and security criteria could appear on those screens alongside traditional search engines. The consultation closed on October 9, and a decision is expected by the end of the year. Meanwhile, AI systems are themselves turning out to be highly susceptible to defaults. In ["A Dual-Process Perspective on Nudge Susceptibility in LLM-Based GUI Agents"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19843) (September 2026), Haya Halimeh, Sascha Kaltenpoth, Kevin Bösch and Oliver Müller of Paderborn University ran a simulated online shopping experiment with 3,600 AI agents across six frontier models. The agents were swayed both by pre-selected defaults and by social-influence cues. Giving them more time to reason reduced their susceptibility to defaults but increased their susceptibility to social cues: thinking harder changed how they were nudged, not whether. And AI suggestions can act as defaults for human thought. In a study published in *Science Advances* in March, [Cornell researchers](https://www.news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/03/ai-assistants-can-sway-writers-attitudes-even-when-theyre-watching-bias) including Mor Naaman found that people writing with a slanted autocomplete assistant shifted their opinions on social issues towards its position, mostly without noticing, and that warning them about the bias did not prevent it.

**Why it matters.** The power of defaults is about to be multiplied. Whichever AI assistant becomes the default on phones and in browsers will shape where billions of questions go, which is why regulators are already arguing over the screens. And as software agents begin to buy, book and compare on people's behalf, the defaults built into websites and interfaces will steer machines as well as humans, apparently even more reliably. For companies, two practical implications follow. Anyone selling to AI agents will need to think about choice architecture as carefully as they think about search ranking today. And anyone deploying AI to employees or customers should treat its default settings, from the suggestions it offers to the options it pre-selects, as decisions with real influence, to be made deliberately and reviewed, not left as they arrived.

*Source: CMA (Sep 23, 2026); Halimeh, Kaltenpoth, Bösch & Müller, arXiv 2609.19843 (Sep 2026); Williams-Ceci, Jakesch, Naaman et al., Science Advances (Mar 2026), via Cornell Chronicle. ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the footer that grew Hotmail** — when Hotmail launched its free web-based email service in 1996, its investor Tim Draper of the venture firm Draper Fisher Jurvetson pushed for an unusual feature: a line at the bottom of every outgoing message inviting the recipient to get their own free email at Hotmail. Users did not choose to add it; it was simply there, by default, on every email they sent. Every message became an advertisement delivered by a friend, and the service grew at a pace rarely seen before, reaching [millions of users](https://techcrunch.com/2009/10/18/ps-i-love-you-get-your-free-email-at-hotmail/) within about a year and a half. Microsoft bought it at the end of 1997 for a reported $400 million. It remains the textbook case of a default turned into a growth engine, long before anyone called it "viral". (TechCrunch; HBS)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the pigeons that kept their route** — what happens when a familiar path becomes dangerous? Researchers in Italy flew a remote-controlled, falcon-like robot near homing pigeons fitted with GPS trackers as they travelled routes they knew well. The pigeons cut their flying altitude by [about half](https://ecoevorxiv.org/repository/view/11964/) as they approached the "RobotFalcon", starting some 200 metres away. But they did not change their route to avoid the area, even on later flights when the robot was absent. The authors suggest that the efficiency of a familiar path outweighed the risk. The birds adjusted their behaviour in the moment but kept their default. Many organisations do the same. (Cerritelli et al., EcoEvoRxiv preprint, 2026)

**The end of the subscription trap** — some of the most profitable defaults are the ones that keep customers paying. From January 1, 2027, under Britain's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, subscription businesses will have to remind customers before a free or discounted trial turns into a paid plan and before long contracts renew, give them cooling-off periods, and make leaving easy. The government [brought the start date forward](https://www.stephensonharwood.com/insights/subscription-reforms-brought-forward-to-january-2027-is-your-business-ready/) in August, and the detailed regulations were laid before Parliament in October. The European Commission is expected to propose a Digital Fairness Act this month that could go further, possibly requiring customers' express consent before a free trial converts. For subscription businesses built on inertia, the default is about to change. (Stephenson Harwood; Baker McKenzie; MLex)

Stephen Dubner talks with Richard Thaler, the Nobel economist who, with Cass Sunstein, popularised the idea of nudges, about what defaults have achieved in practice, from automatic enrolment and automatic escalation in retirement plans to Eric Johnson's work on organ donation. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In May 2005 the novelist David Foster Wallace gave a commencement address at Kenyon College that has since become one of the most widely read speeches of its kind. It begins with a parable about two young fish who are asked by an older fish, "How's the water?" and who later ask each other, "What the hell is water?" Wallace's point was that the most important features of our lives are often the ones we no longer notice, and he used the language of software to describe them. The real value of an education, he suggested, is learning to do the work of "altering or getting free of my natural, hard-wired default setting", the habit of seeing everything from the self outward. His subject was attention and compassion, not product design or regulation. But the insight runs through everything in this issue. Defaults are powerful precisely because they are invisible: the savings plan nobody chose, the search engine nobody selected, the route the pigeons always fly. The first step in using them well, in a company or a life, is simply to see them, and then to ask whether we would choose them if we were choosing today.
