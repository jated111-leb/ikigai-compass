# The Daily Compass #080 — The peacock's tail

*Tuesday, October 20, 2026 · ~14 minute read*

The peacock's tail troubled Charles Darwin. In 1860 he wrote to a colleague that the sight of a feather in a peacock's tail made him sick, because it seemed to contradict his theory: a huge, heavy, conspicuous tail makes a bird easier for predators to catch and harder to keep alive. Why would natural selection produce something so wasteful? Darwin's own answer was sexual selection: peahens prefer showy males. A century later the Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi offered a sharper explanation. The tail is valuable precisely because it is costly. Only a genuinely healthy, strong bird can afford to grow and carry one, so the tail is an honest signal of quality that a weak bird cannot fake. This issue is about costly signals: the degrees, prices, gestures and effort that persuade others because they are hard to counterfeit, and what happens to them when a new technology makes a once-expensive signal suddenly cheap.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Why degrees work even if school teaches nothing

**What happened.** In 1973 a young Harvard economist named Michael Spence published ["Job Market Signaling"](https://www.sfu.ca/~allen/Spence.pdf) in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, a paper that helped win him a share of the 2001 Nobel Prize, alongside George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz, for work on markets in which one side knows more than the other. Spence's problem was simple. An employer cannot see a job applicant's true ability, and every applicant has an incentive to claim to be talented. How can the able ones make their claim believable? His answer was that they need a signal that is cheaper for them to acquire than for less able people. Education can play that role. If capable people find it easier to complete a demanding degree, then the degree separates them from the rest, and employers can rationally pay graduates more, even if, in the extreme case of the model, the education itself teaches nothing useful for the job.

**Why it matters.** Spence's idea explains a great deal of behaviour that otherwise looks irrational: expensive offices that reassure clients, warranties that only a confident manufacturer can afford, advertising campaigns whose cost is itself the message, and the long, unpaid hours that junior professionals put in to show commitment. It also carries an uncomfortable implication. If much of the value of a credential lies in what it signals rather than what it teaches, then some of the time and money societies spend on education is a costly sorting contest rather than an investment in skills. For anyone hiring, the practical question is always: what does this signal actually prove, and how easy would it be for the wrong person to acquire it?

*Source: Spence, Quarterly Journal of Economics 87(3) (1973). ~40 min (paper).*

### ◆ 2. The handicap principle

**What happened.** Zahavi first set out his idea in 1975 in the *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, in a paper on mate selection as "a selection for a handicap", and later developed it with Avishag Zahavi in their book *The Handicap Principle* (1997). Their argument was that many puzzling features of animal life, from elaborate tails to loud songs to gazelles leaping high in the air in front of predators, make sense as honest signals. A gazelle that wastes energy jumping, a behaviour called stotting, may be telling a lion that it is fit enough to escape, so the lion should not bother to chase it. The signal is believable because it is expensive: a sick or slow animal could not afford it. For years many biologists were sceptical, until the theorist Alan Grafen showed in 1990 with a formal model that such costly signals can indeed be stable in evolution.

**Why it matters.** The handicap principle is one of those ideas that, once learned, appears everywhere. A company that offers a generous money-back guarantee is signalling confidence in its product that a weaker rival cannot match. A leader who takes a personal risk to back a decision signals conviction more convincingly than any speech. A brand that spends heavily on quality where customers can see it is doing something similar. The underlying rule is the same in nature and in markets: when talk is cheap, trust flows to signals that would hurt to fake. The useful exercise for any organisation is to ask which of its own claims to customers, partners or staff are backed by something costly, and which are merely words.

*Source: Zahavi, Journal of Theoretical Biology (1975); Zahavi & Zahavi, The Handicap Principle (1997); Grafen (1990). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. When AI makes talk cheap

**What happened.** What happens when a costly signal suddenly becomes free? Generative AI is running that experiment on one of the oldest signals in the labour market: the well-written job application. In ["Making Talk Cheap: Generative AI and Labor Market Signaling"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08785), the economists Anaïs Galdin and Jesse Silbert studied Freelancer.com, a large online work platform, using a language model to measure how carefully each application was tailored to the job. Before AI writing tools were widely available, employers clearly valued customised applications, and customisation predicted who would do the job well. After the platform introduced its own AI writing tool, that relationship largely disappeared. Their model suggests the loss of the signal hurts the best workers: under their estimates, the most able fifth of workers are hired about 19% less often, while the least able fifth are hired about 14% more often. A separate Yale study by Cui, Dias and Ye on the same kind of platform found that the link between cover-letter quality and callbacks fell by about half after an AI writing tool appeared, and that employers shifted their attention to other evidence, especially past work experience.

**Why it matters.** This is Spence and Zahavi in reverse. A well-crafted letter used to be a signal because it took effort and skill; once a machine can produce one in seconds, it proves very little, and the market looks for something that is still hard to fake. That pattern is likely to repeat far beyond hiring: in marketing copy, pitch decks, student essays, grant applications and even personal messages. As written polish becomes cheap, value shifts toward signals AI cannot easily manufacture: verifiable track records, work samples, live conversations, references and reputation built over time. For job seekers and companies alike, the strategic response is not to compete on polish but to invest in the costly, checkable signals that still separate the able from the merely articulate.

*Source: Galdin & Silbert, arXiv 2511.08785 (2025); Cui, Dias & Ye, arXiv 2509.25054. ~45 min (papers).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the leisure class** — the original theory of costly signals in human society came from the eccentric American economist Thorstein Veblen. In *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) he coined the term "conspicuous consumption" to describe the wealthy spending lavishly not for comfort but to display their wealth, and "conspicuous leisure" for the display of not needing to work. Some goods, he observed, become more desirable as their price rises, because a high price is the point. Economists still call such products Veblen goods. More than a century later his insight explains luxury brands, extravagant weddings and the instinct to be seen paying more, and it remains one of the most quietly influential books in the social sciences. (Veblen, 1899)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the price of friendship, in jackdaws** — jackdaws, small and highly social crows, form close bonds that can last for years. A new study by researchers at the Universities of Exeter and Swansea, published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, followed wild jackdaws at feeding sites and found that close social partners help each other hold on to a good spot. But the support comes at a price: bonded birds sometimes wait longer before they get to eat. Friendship, it seems, is a [trade-off even for crows](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-social-bonds-wild-jackdaws.html): security in exchange for patience. (Proceedings of the Royal Society B / phys.org, Oct 2026)

**Knowledge at Wharton — "Less Is More: Redefining the Luxury Goods Market," with Pinar Yildirim and John Zhang** (from the archive)
Two Wharton marketing professors discuss their research on "minimalist luxury": why some wealthy consumers are abandoning loud logos for quieter, unbranded goods. Part of their explanation is a signalling story: when convincing counterfeits are cheap, a visible logo no longer proves much, so the signal moves elsewhere. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

There is an old idea, found in many traditions, that a gift means most when it costs the giver something: not necessarily money, but time, effort or attention that could have gone elsewhere. A handwritten letter says more than a typed one, not because the words are better but because the hand had to move across the page. The economics of signalling gives that intuition a precise form, and the arrival of AI gives it new urgency. As machines make words, images and polish nearly free, the things that remain costly, showing up in person, doing the slow work, keeping a promise over years, become the most credible messages we can send. The peacock carries its tail every day at some risk to itself. Perhaps the human equivalent is simpler: to spend, on the people and work that matter, the one resource no machine can produce on our behalf, which is our own time.
