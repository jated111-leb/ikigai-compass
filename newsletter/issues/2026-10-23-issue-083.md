# The Daily Compass #083 — The map of the Pyrenees

*Friday, October 23, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

There is a story that management professors have told for decades. A young lieutenant of a small Hungarian detachment in the Alps sends a reconnaissance unit into the icy wilderness. It begins to snow, and the unit does not return for two days. The lieutenant fears he has sent his men to their deaths. Then they come back, and explain that they had given up hope until one of them found a map in his pocket. Calmed by it, they made camp, waited out the storm and used the map to find their way down. The lieutenant asks to see this map. It is a map of the Pyrenees. The organisational theorist Karl Weick made the tale famous as a parable: in a crisis, any plausible story that gets people moving may be better than none. This issue is about the power of stories: how narratives shape strategy, move markets and change behaviour, and why the most influential story is not always the true one. Even this one has a twist, which you will find below.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Plans that read like stories

**What happened.** In 1998 three authors, Gordon Shaw, who ran strategic planning at 3M, and the scholars Robert Brown and Philip Bromiley, published ["Strategic Stories: How 3M Is Rewriting Business Planning"](https://hbr.org/1998/05/strategic-stories-how-3m-is-rewriting-business-planning) in the *Harvard Business Review*. Their diagnosis was that almost all business plans were written as bullet points, and that bullet lists tended to become lists of "good things to do" that hid the hard questions: what the market looks like, what competitors will do, why the plan will work and what must happen in what order. Shaw's alternative was to write plans as narratives that set the scene, introduce the dramatic conflict and explain how the company would win. The approach fitted 3M's culture, which already ran on stories about the inventions of Post-it Notes and masking tape. Six years later, Jeff Bezos reached the same conclusion at Amazon and banned PowerPoint from senior meetings, replacing slides with narrative memos of up to six pages that everyone reads in silence at the start of the meeting.

**Why it matters.** The case for narrative plans is not that stories are more pleasant to read. It is that they are harder to fake. Bullet points let a writer list goals without explaining how they connect; a narrative forces the writer to show cause and effect, to sequence events and to admit where the logic depends on an assumption. Readers can also see themselves in a story in a way they cannot in a list, which makes commitment more likely. For any team writing a strategy, a plan for a new market or a case for investment, the exercise is simple and revealing: rewrite the bullet points as a story with a beginning, a conflict and an end. If the story does not hold together, neither does the plan.

*Source: Shaw, Brown & Bromiley, Harvard Business Review (May–June 1998). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. Why bad news sticks

**What happened.** The Nobel laureate Robert Shiller has argued in his work on "narrative economics" that popular stories, spreading like epidemics, drive booms, busts and economic decisions as surely as interest rates do. A new paper puts one part of that idea to a careful test. In ["When Does Bad News Stick? Topic-Level Asymmetry in Narrative Dynamics"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35838) (NBER Working Paper 35838, October 2026), Isha Agarwal, Wentong Chen and Eswar Prasad used the entire *Wall Street Journal* archive from 1990 to 2025 to build monthly indices of positive and negative sentiment across twelve economic topics. In every topic, a shock of bad news had a larger cumulative effect than a comparable shock of good news. The gap was largest for security and human rights, for firms and corporate strategy, for health and public safety, and for real estate and financial stability. A steady flow of new information did not necessarily wash negative stories away.

**Why it matters.** The findings formalise something every communications team suspects: a bad story about a company outlasts a good one, and the asymmetry is especially strong for stories about corporate strategy. That has practical consequences. It argues for addressing negative narratives early and directly rather than hoping that good news will eventually drown them out. It also suggests that broad measures of "sentiment" can mislead, because the mood about one topic can be very different from the mood about another. For investors, it is a reminder that markets react not only to data but to the stories told about data, and that those stories decay at different speeds. The paper is a new working paper and not yet peer reviewed, but it gives Shiller's idea a useful, measurable edge.

*Source: Agarwal, Chen & Prasad, NBER Working Paper 35838 (Oct 2026). ~40 min (paper).*

### ◆ 3. Action without conviction

**What happened.** Can an AI system talk people into doing something, not just thinking something? A study by Kobi Hackenburg, Luke Hewitt, Caroline Wagner, Christopher Summerfield and Ben Tappin, ["AI can persuade people to take political actions"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2611368123), published this month in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, tested exactly that. In two preregistered experiments involving 14,779 adults in Britain, participants held conversations with leading AI chatbots that tried to persuade them to sign real petitions, on issues such as democratic reform and animal welfare, using their own names and email addresses. The conversations raised petition signing by [19.7 percentage points](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09200). The most striking finding was what did not happen: the AI's success in changing people's opinions was not correlated with its success in changing their behaviour. Providing facts and information, the strategy that earlier research found most effective at shifting opinions, was the least effective at getting people to act.

**Why it matters.** The study separates two things that are usually assumed to move together. We tend to imagine that persuasion works by changing beliefs, and that changed beliefs then change behaviour. Here, the route to action ran around belief rather than through it. That matters for anyone thinking about AI's influence on elections, consumer choices or public health, because tests of whether AI can change opinions may understate its ability to change what people do. Signing a petition is a low-cost action, and more demanding behaviours may be harder to move. But the result is a reminder that stories and conversations work on us in ways that are not purely rational, and that systems able to hold millions of such conversations at once are a new kind of force.

*Source: Hackenburg et al., PNAS (Oct 2026); arXiv 2604.09200. ~30 min (paper).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the map that was not what it seemed** — the twist promised above. Weick told the story of the lieutenant and the wrong map in his 1987 essay "Substitutes for Strategy", and it became one of the most repeated parables in management. But its source was a poem. The Czech poet and immunologist Miroslav Holub published "Brief Thoughts on Maps" in 1977, crediting the anecdote to the Nobel laureate Albert Szent-Györgyi. In a [note](https://ephemerajournal.org/node/4675) in the journal *ephemera*, Thomas Basbøll and Henrik Graham traced Weick's version back to Holub's poem and questioned how a literary anecdote of uncertain origin had become evidence in strategy research. Whether or not the event ever happened, the story did exactly what it describes: it gave people a map, and they followed it. (Holub, 1977; ephemera)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the book that was bound out of order** — the Voynich manuscript, written in an unknown script and illustrated with strange plants and stars, has defeated codebreakers for more than a century. A study published on October 19 in *Digital Medievalist* by Colin Layfield of the University of Malta and the manuscript historian Lisa Fagin Davis suggests one reason: its pages may be [in the wrong order](https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/undeciphered-voynich-manuscript-may-have-been-bound-out-of-order-study-finds). Davis noticed that some drawings run off one page and resume several pages later. The authors argue the sheets were originally separate folded leaves and were later sewn together incorrectly in the 15th century. Some independent researchers say the reading-order claim goes beyond the data. Either way, any story becomes harder to understand when its pages are shuffled. (Live Science / Digital Medievalist, Oct 2026)

**The AI story and the money behind it** — the IMF's latest *Global Financial Stability Report*, released on October 13, named a [correction in AI-linked assets](https://thenextweb.com/news/imf-circular-ai-deals-financial-stability-report) as one of three main near-term risks to the financial system, alongside a sharp rise in government bond yields and a major cyberattack. Coverage of the report highlights the "circular" deals in which large technology companies invest in AI developers that then buy computing capacity from them, with some $1.4 trillion of promised future revenue tied up in such arrangements. It is narrative economics in action: a shared expectation about the future is now financing itself, and the risk is that the story runs ahead of the earnings. (IMF GFSR, Oct 2026)

The Pulitzer-winning author of *Lords of Finance* discusses his new book on the crash of 1873, a railway mania that led to two decades of deflation. His striking argument is that the crisis did less lasting damage to growth than to politics, feeding populism and protectionism: the story people told about the crash outlived the crash itself. **Queue it**; about 45 minutes and a perfect companion to today's theme. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

"We tell ourselves stories in order to live." Joan Didion opened her 1979 essay "The White Album" with that sentence, and it has been quoted ever since as a celebration of narrative. But the essay that follows is darker and more honest. Didion describes a period in the late 1960s when the stories she relied on to make sense of events stopped working, when experience seemed to arrive as a series of images without a plot. Her point was not only that we need stories, but that we impose them, and that the imposed story can fail us. It is a useful double lesson for this issue. Stories are how organisations commit, how markets move and how people find their way down the mountain. They are also how we mislead ourselves, by following a map of somewhere else. The task is not to stop telling stories, which is impossible, but to keep checking the map against the mountain.
