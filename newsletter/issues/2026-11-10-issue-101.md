# The Daily Compass #101 — Gladly teach

*Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1789 a Scottish clergyman named Andrew Bell took charge of a new school in Madras for the orphaned sons of British soldiers. Bell was looking for a better way to teach the youngest children their letters. He found one by watching local schoolchildren in India, who learned to write by tracing letters with their fingers in sand. When his own ushers refused to adopt the method, Bell did something radical: he put one of the pupils, a young boy named John Frisken, in charge of teaching it to the beginners. It worked, and Bell gradually built the whole school around the idea, with older pupils teaching younger ones under the master's supervision. He described the results in [*An Experiment in Education*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Bell_%28educationalist%29) (1797), and the "Madras system", along with a rival version promoted by Joseph Lancaster in London, spread across Britain and beyond as a cheap way to educate the poor. Its critics later pointed out its flaws, and Lancaster's claims about one master teaching a thousand children were more publicity than fact. But Bell had noticed something that research has since confirmed: the children doing the teaching were learning too, often more than the children being taught. This issue is about that effect: why teaching is one of the most powerful ways to learn, what it means for leaders and mentors, and what happens to it when AI does the junior work.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The protégé effect

**What happened.** Psychologists have been testing the old idea that teaching helps the teacher, and the evidence is surprisingly strong. In ["Expecting to teach enhances learning"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-014-0416-z) (*Memory & Cognition*, 2014), John Nestojko, Dung Bui, Nate Kornell and Elizabeth Bjork asked students to study a passage either for a test or because they would have to teach it to someone else. In reality, nobody taught; everyone took the test. The students who expected to teach recalled more, organised what they recalled better and were better at remembering the main points. Merely changing their expectations had changed how they studied. Actually teaching adds more. In studies by Logan Fiorella and Richard Mayer (*Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 2013 and 2014), students who prepared to teach did better on an immediate test, but those who went on to teach the material on video retained more a week later. Why? One answer comes from a [study by Aloysius Koh and colleagues](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/learning-teaching-others-extremely-effective) (*Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 2018): students who taught from memory learned more than those who taught by reading from a script, suggesting that much of the benefit comes from having to retrieve and reconstruct what you know. A meta-analysis of 39 studies by Keiichi Kobayashi ([*Educational Psychology Review*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-024-09871-4)) found that teaching after studying produced a moderate improvement in learning when students had known in advance that they would teach. Teaching works partly because it exposes what we do not know. In a classic study of the "illusion of explanatory depth" ([*Cognitive Science*, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1)), Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil found that people who felt confident they understood how everyday devices worked lowered their ratings sharply once asked to explain them step by step.

**Why it matters.** Organisations usually treat teaching as a cost: time that experts spend away from their real work. The research suggests it is also an investment in the experts themselves. Explaining forces people to organise what they know, retrieve it and spot the gaps they did not know they had. Some companies have built this into their culture. Google's own guidance on its employee-to-employee learning programme says that [80% of its tracked trainings](https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/learning-development-employee-to-employee) are run through g2g, a network of employees who volunteer to teach their colleagues. For leaders, the practical lessons are simple. Ask people to prepare as if they will teach, even if they will not. Give experts regular chances to explain their work to colleagues outside their field. And when someone says they understand a plan, ask them to explain how it works, step by step. The answer will tell both of you how deep the understanding really goes.

*Source: Nestojko, Bui, Kornell & Bjork, Memory & Cognition 42(7) (2014); Fiorella & Mayer, CEP (2013, 2014); Koh, Lee & Lim, Applied Cognitive Psychology 32(3) (2018); Kobayashi, Educational Psychology Review 36 (2024); Rozenblit & Keil, Cognitive Science 26(5) (2002); Google re:Work. ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. What mentors get back

**What happened.** The same effect shows up in mentoring and advice. In ["Career benefits associated with mentoring for mentors"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2013.03.011) (*Journal of Vocational Behavior*, 2013), Rajashi Ghosh and Thomas Reio combined studies of what mentoring does for the people who give it, and found that mentors reported higher job satisfaction and organisational commitment than non-mentors, along with better career outcomes and job performance linked to different kinds of mentoring. Giving advice can also motivate the adviser. In ["Dear Abby: Should I Give Advice or Receive It?"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6728546) (*Psychological Science*, 2018), Lauren Eskreis-Winkler, Ayelet Fishbach and Angela Duckworth randomly assigned middle-school students either to give motivational advice to younger students or to receive advice from experts. Over the following month, the advice-givers spent more time on their schoolwork. In studies with adults trying to save money, lose weight, control their temper or find a job, most people said that giving advice motivated them more than receiving it, the opposite of what most people expect. The authors suggest that giving advice builds confidence: to advise someone else, you have to see yourself as someone who knows what to do. A follow-up field experiment with high-school students ([*PNAS*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6660764)) found that writing advice for younger students improved the advisers' own grades.

**Why it matters.** Leaders often see mentoring as something senior people do for junior ones. The evidence suggests it runs in both directions. Noel Tichy, who ran General Electric's leadership centre at Crotonville, built his influential book *The Leadership Engine* (1997) around the idea that great leaders are teachers who develop a "teachable point of view". In a later book, he corrected himself: the first book, he wrote, ["misses a point: Teachers learn too."](https://www.globalsources.com/knowledge/talk-to-noel-teach/) Writing works the same way. In his [2017 letter to shareholders](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/04/23/what-jeff-bezos-learned-from-requiring-6-page-memos-at-amazon.html), Jeff Bezos explained why Amazon replaced slide presentations with written memos: "The narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding." For founders and growth teams, mentoring also helps the mentees. In a study of investors mentoring early-stage technology start-ups ([*Management Science*, 2025](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05115)), Amir Sariri found that advice improved the start-ups' later performance, and that its core message was "do less and learn more". The practical lesson: treat teaching, mentoring and writing things down as part of senior people's development, not a distraction from it.

*Source: Ghosh & Reio, JVB 83(1) (2013); Eskreis-Winkler, Fishbach & Duckworth, Psychological Science 29(11) (2018); Eskreis-Winkler et al., PNAS (2019); Tichy, The Cycle of Leadership (2002); Bezos, 2017 shareholder letter; Sariri, Management Science 71(12) (2025). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Who teaches the juniors now?

**What happened.** Learning at work has long followed a simple loop: juniors draft, seniors correct, and both learn in the process. AI is disrupting that loop. In a [field study published in *Harvard Business Review*](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/shaping-early-career-success-in-the-age-of-ai/) in July, researchers from KPMG and the University of Texas at Austin asked 523 of KPMG's early-career professionals to complete business tasks with an AI agent, and compared their work with what the AI produced alone. About half improved on the AI's output. A quarter merely matched it. And a quarter did worse than the AI alone, even though they had the same foundational skills as the best group: their attempts to direct and correct the AI pushed it the wrong way. The difference was not knowledge but judgement about how to work with the machine, a skill the authors argue has to be taught on the job. (The study was run by KPMG on its own staff, so it is not independent research.) At the same time, a [McKinsey survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/building-expertise-in-the-age-of-ai-who-trains-the-next-generation) of about 1,500 executives found that around a third said AI had already reduced the basic tasks through which junior employees build skills. AI can also strengthen the teaching loop, if it is designed to. At EPFL in Switzerland, the computer scientist Ola Svensson [built a chatbot](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/ai-can-help-students-learn-better-3) for his algorithms course of more than 500 students that asks questions instead of giving answers, so that students have to explain the material, and it then critiques their explanations. And in a [randomised study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017) of about 900 tutors working with low-income students in the United States, a tool called Tutor CoPilot, which suggested better questions to the tutors in real time, made students 4 percentage points more likely to master a topic, and 9 points more likely among students of lower-rated tutors.

**Why it matters.** If AI does the first draft, juniors may lose the very work that used to teach them, and seniors may lose the act of correcting it, which taught them too. Nothing about this is inevitable, but it will not fix itself. The emerging answer looks like more deliberate teaching, not less. McKinsey borrows a model from medicine, the "preceptor", in which a senior person supervises a small group and pays close attention to what they accept, reject or misjudge from AI. The KPMG researchers recommend asking juniors to record why they accepted, changed or rejected AI output, which turns every task into a small lesson. And tools can be designed, as at EPFL, to make people explain rather than simply receive answers. One caution: a study presented at CHI 2026 found that explaining a concept to an AI "tutee" made people [work harder but did not measurably improve their learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16583) in a single session, so the design details matter. For leaders, the question to ask of every AI rollout is not only what work it saves, but who now learns from that work, and how.

*Source: KPMG & UT Austin McCombs, HBR (Jul 2026); McKinsey Quarterly (Jul 2026); EPFL (2026); Wang et al., Tutor CoPilot, arXiv 2410.03017; Xu et al., CHI 2026 (arXiv 2601.16583). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: "Men learn while they teach"** — around two thousand years ago, the Roman philosopher Seneca wrote a series of letters of advice to his younger friend Lucilius. In the seventh, he warned him against crowds and urged him to spend time with people who would make him better, and those he could make better in turn. "These things work both ways," [Seneca wrote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docendo_discimus): "homines, dum docent, discunt", "men learn while they teach". The line became the Latin motto *docendo discimus*, "by teaching, we learn", and it has decorated school crests ever since. Seneca was not writing about schools, though. He was describing friendship as a kind of mutual teaching, and suggesting that the best way to improve yourself is to help someone else improve. (Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, 7.8)

**🃏 WILDCARD: meerkat schools** — teaching was long thought to be almost uniquely human. Then, in 2006, two studies changed the picture. In *Science*, Alex Thornton and Katherine McAuliffe of the University of Cambridge [reported](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/wild-meerkats-school-their-young) that adult meerkats in the Kalahari teach pups to handle scorpions step by step: first offering them dead scorpions, then live ones with the stings removed, and finally intact prey, and nudging escaping scorpions back to the pups. In *Nature*, Nigel Franks and Tom Richardson [showed](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2006/879.html) that ants lead each other to food in "tandem runs", with the leader slowing down when the follower falls behind. In both cases teaching costs the teacher time and food, which is why biologists count it as true teaching. There is no evidence that meerkats or ants learn from teaching. That bonus, it seems, may be ours. (Science; Nature, 2006)

**Fifty million teachers** — on World Teachers' Day in October, UNESCO and its partners [reported](https://www.africanews.com/2026/10/06/world-needs-50-million-additional-teachers-by-2030-unesco-says/) that the world has about 107 million teachers and needs 50 million more by 2030 to give every child pre-primary, primary and secondary education. More than half of that demand comes from replacing teachers who leave the profession. Whatever role AI plays in classrooms, the shortage is a reminder that teaching is still overwhelmingly a human job, and one many people are walking away from. (UNESCO via Africanews, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In the General Prologue to *The Canterbury Tales*, written in the late fourteenth century, Geoffrey Chaucer introduces a group of pilgrims travelling to Canterbury. Most of them are satirised: the greedy, the vain, the hypocritical. One of the few treated with real affection is the Clerk of Oxford, a thin, poor scholar on a thin horse, who would rather have twenty books at his bedside than fine clothes or a fiddle. Chaucer ends his portrait with a line that has been quoted by teachers ever since: ["And gladly wolde he lerne and gladly teche."](https://tigerweb.towson.edu/duncan/chaucer/3ln208308n.htm) The order matters. The Clerk is not a teacher who has finished learning, but a learner who teaches, and who seems to do both for the same reason: he loves understanding things. That is perhaps the best summary of today's research. Teaching is not what we do once we know enough. It is one of the ways we come to know. Gladly learn, and gladly teach.
