# The Daily Compass #087 — Nothing left to take away

*Tuesday, October 27, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

A few years ago Leidy Klotz, an engineer at the University of Virginia, was building a Lego bridge with his three-year-old son. One of the columns holding up the bridge was shorter than the other, so the bridge wasn't level. Klotz, as he tells it, reached for a brick to add to the short column. His son reached over and took a brick off the tall one. The child's fix was simpler, cheaper and just as good, and the father, a trained designer, had not even considered it. Klotz began to wonder whether this was a quirk or a pattern, and with colleagues he set out to test it. The answer, published in *Nature* in 2021, was that people systematically overlook the option of taking something away. When we want to improve a plan, a product, a process or a life, we instinctively ask what to add, and the possibility of removing something often never occurs to us. This issue is about subtraction: the evidence that we neglect it, why removing things often goes unrewarded even when it works, and the curious finding that AI systems seem to share our addiction to adding.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The option we forget we have

**What happened.** In ["People systematically overlook subtractive changes"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y) (*Nature*, 2021), Gabrielle Adams, Benjamin Converse, Andrew Hales and Leidy Klotz ran eight experiments with 1,153 participants. Each problem was designed so that subtracting was the better solution: stabilising a Lego platform where each added brick cost money but removing one was free, improving a miniature golf course, making a grid pattern symmetrical, revising an overloaded travel itinerary. Again and again, most people added. The bias was not a matter of preference; people simply did not think of subtraction. It shrank when participants were reminded that removing things was allowed, when they had more chances to try solutions, and when they were not distracted, and it grew under cognitive load. Later work has added nuance. A 2024 study in *Scientific Reports* by researchers at Uppsala University found that the tendency varies with age, culture and the task: children neglected subtraction more than adults, and Swedish and American adults approached the problems differently.

**Why it matters.** The bias helps explain some of the most familiar frustrations of organisational life: calendars that only ever fill up, processes that gain steps and never lose them, products that accumulate features, rulebooks that only grow. Each addition looks sensible on its own; the cost is in the accumulation. The research also points to the remedy, which is surprisingly simple: prompt people explicitly to consider removal. Asking "what could we take away?" at the start of any review, rather than as an afterthought, changes what solutions people see. A useful habit for any leader is to require that every proposal to add something, whether a meeting, a report, a feature or an approval step, be accompanied by a candidate for removal. The point is not austerity for its own sake. It is to put the option back on the table that our minds quietly leave off.

*Source: Adams, Converse, Hales & Klotz, Nature 592 (2021); Juvrud, Myers & Nyström, Scientific Reports (2024); Klotz, Subtract (2021). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 2. Nobody thanks you for what's missing

**What happened.** Even when subtraction works, it often goes unrewarded, because people notice what is there more than what is not. A new study led by Diletta Acuti of the University of Bath, ["The more, the better? The sustainable additive bias"](https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/university-of-bath-study-identifies-harmful-paradox-behind-well-meaning-green-efforts/) (*Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, September 2026), shows the problem in the context of sustainability. Across eight online and laboratory studies and a field experiment, consumers judged companies more sustainable when they added eco-friendly items than when they removed unnecessary ones, even when removal saved more waste. In the field test, with 302 guests at a four-star European hotel, rooms stocked with extra green amenities were rated the most sustainable, at 5.05 on the study's scale against 4.63 for pared-down rooms, yet they produced the most waste. The authors trace the effect to perceived effort: an added green item looks like an investment, while a missing one gives customers nothing to notice. Their remedy is to make the subtraction visible, by explaining the effort behind it and keeping a clear signal of intent, such as one labelled refillable dispenser in place of a row of miniature bottles.

**Why it matters.** The study generalises well beyond hotels. Subtraction is often the better choice, but it is harder to sell, inside the company as well as outside it. When Procter & Gamble's chief executive A.G. Lafley announced in 2014 that the company would shed up to 100 of its brands, he made the case with numbers: the 70 to 80 brands it would keep had produced about 90% of sales and over 95% of profit in the previous three years. Cutting is easy to describe as loss and hard to describe as gain. Leaders who want to simplify a product line, a service or an organisation need to tell the story of what the removal makes possible, more focus, less waste, a clearer offer, and to leave a visible sign that something was done on purpose. Otherwise customers and colleagues will see only that something is gone.

*Source: Acuti et al., Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (Sep 2026), via University of Bath; P&G announcement, via Fortune (Aug 2014). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. Machines that would rather add

**What happened.** If people overlook subtraction, what about the AI systems trained on what people write? Two recent papers suggest they inherit the habit, and a third shows what happens when engineers subtract instead. In "To Add Is Machine, To Delete Is Human" (arXiv, July 2026), Ebrahimi and colleagues studied AI coding models fixing real software bugs. The models usually found the right file but often failed to delete the code that needed to go; about 29% of patches that passed a standard benchmark kept code the human developer had removed, wrapping it in a new check instead, a pattern the authors call "Guard-and-Go". An earlier study by Uhler and colleagues found that GPT-4, given the same kinds of puzzles as in the *Nature* experiments, showed an even stronger preference for adding than humans did, especially when asked to "improve" rather than "edit". On the other side of the ledger, researchers at Apple and EPFL asked ["How Much of a Harness Does a Strong Agent Need for Autonomous ML Engineering?"](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/harness-autonomous-ml-engineering) (September 2026). They stripped an AI agent down to three abilities, reading files, writing files and running commands, and compared it with four elaborate open-source systems that coordinate multiple agents, on the same model, hardware and time budget. On machine-learning engineering tasks drawn from Kaggle competitions, the minimal agent matched or beat the elaborate ones, and adding more coordination machinery often made results worse.

**Why it matters.** Together the papers point to a practical lesson. Left to themselves, AI tools tend to add: more code, more caveats, more structure. Software and documents written with AI may therefore grow longer and more tangled unless someone deliberately asks for deletion. At the same time, the systems built around AI are often more complicated than they need to be, and as the underlying models improve, scaffolding that once helped can become dead weight. For leaders deploying AI, two habits follow. Review AI-assisted work for what should have been removed, not only for what is wrong. And periodically test whether the elaborate workflows built around a model still earn their keep, by comparing them with the simplest possible version. As the Apple authors put it, performance comes mainly from the underlying model; much of the rest is machinery. Their minimal agent was not the cheapest option in compute, so the saving is in complexity, not necessarily in cost.

*Source: Ebrahimi et al., arXiv 2607.28887 (Jul 2026); Uhler et al., arXiv 2404.16692; Brilliantov, Hernández-Cano & Abbé, Apple ML Research (Sep 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the subtraction quotes nobody said** — the two most famous lines about the art of removal are probably apocryphal. Michelangelo is supposed to have explained his *David* by saying he simply removed everything that was not David; [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2014/06/22/chip-away/) found no evidence that he or any great sculptor said it, and traced the idea to an unattributed 1858 magazine anecdote. Coco Chanel's advice to look in the mirror before leaving the house and take one thing off is likewise unsourced, though *Time* magazine in 1960 did quote her saying one should "always take off, never add." The best-documented line is humbler. Near the end of the sixteenth of his *Provincial Letters* (1656–57), Blaise Pascal apologised to his reader: "I have made this one longer only because I did not have the leisure to make it shorter." It is often credited to Mark Twain. Removal, Pascal knew, takes more work than addition. (Quote Investigator; Pascal, 1656–57)

**🃏 WILDCARD: what the river did when the dams came out** — in 2024 four hydroelectric dams on the Klamath River, on the border of Oregon and California, were removed, the largest dam removal in history, reconnecting more than 640 kilometres of river. A study in *Scientific Reports* led by Damon Goodman of California Trout, with tribal, state and federal partners, found that Chinook salmon [recolonised 88% of their documented historical range](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/rewilding-upper-klamath-river-basin-rapid-recolonization-chinook-salmon-following) within two migration seasons, swimming 345 kilometres into newly reopened habitat. About 7,700 passed the old dam sites in 2024 and more than 13,000 in 2025. Water shortages and a remaining dam still threaten the recovery, but it is one of the fastest river comebacks ever recorded, achieved by taking something away. (USGS / Scientific Reports, 2026)

**The 300,000-hour meeting** — when the consultancy Bain analysed the calendars of 17 large companies, it found that a single weekly executive committee meeting at one firm, together with the preparation meetings it triggered down the organisation, [consumed some 300,000 hours a year](https://www.bain.com/insights/the-300000-hour-meeting-fm-blog/). In January 2023 Shopify tried the opposite: a bot deleted every recurring meeting with three or more people from employees' calendars, and the company's chief operating officer later told NPR it had "deleted 322,000 hours of meetings", a figure partly projected for the year ahead. Meetings tend to grow back, which is why the exercise has to be repeated. (Bain; NPR, Feb 2023)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1939 the aviator Antoine de Saint-Exupéry published *Terre des hommes*, known in English as *Wind, Sand and Stars*. In a chapter on the aeroplane, he described how engineers refine a machine until its lines seem not invented but found, and he wrote: "Il semble que la perfection soit atteinte non quand il n'y a plus rien à ajouter, mais quand il n'y a plus rien à retrancher." In Lewis Galantière's translation, perfection is attained "not when there is no longer anything to add, but when there is no longer anything to take away." The line is often quoted about writing or design, but Saint-Exupéry was describing something practical: a wing, a fuselage, a machine that must fly. Subtraction, for him, was not minimalism as a style. It was the long work of discovering what was truly necessary. That is a fair test for any plan, product or week. What would remain if we took away everything that is not needed, and would it fly better?
