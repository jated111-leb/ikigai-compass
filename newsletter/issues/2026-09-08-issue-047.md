# The Daily Compass #047 — The stories that hold the world up

*Tuesday, September 8, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Every map of world power is drawn twice: once in facts — who has the armies, the reserves, the chips, the sea lanes — and once in stories, the shared narratives that tell everyone what those facts *mean* and how to behave. Most of the time the story is invisible, which is exactly what lets it do its work; we mistake it for the territory. But stories are load-bearing, and when one cracks, the behavior it was quietly holding up lurches. This issue is about three such stories, all under strain in the same season, and none of them about a model or a benchmark. The first is the story that the world has gone multipolar. The second is the story that there was ever a neutral, above-the-fray international order to lose. The third is the oldest of all — that commerce and connection make peace — now curdling, in real time, into its opposite. Three comforting maps, three cracks, and underneath them a single question a Beirut reader knows in the bone: when the story that organized the room stops being believed, what actually holds?

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The multipolar delusion

**What happened.** From Washington to Beijing to Moscow to New Delhi, one proposition has hardened into consensus: the age of American primacy is over and the world is now multipolar, power dispersed across many centers. C. Raja Mohan — an Indian strategist, notably, not an American nostalgist — calls this a mirage in [*Foreign Affairs*](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/multipolar-delusion-mohan). His argument is uncomfortable for nearly everyone: only the United States actually holds the *full stack* of a genuine pole — military reach, economic depth, technological frontier, institutional weight, cultural pull, all at once. Russia has nuclear weapons and resources but a narrow economy; China is a peer in some columns and absent in others. A world with one full-spectrum power and several partial ones, Mohan insists, is not multipolar — and the danger of pretending otherwise is that the belief has *emboldened Washington to shed the responsibilities that dominance used to carry*, to behave like one power among many precisely because it is not.

**Why it matters.** This is the rare geopolitical claim that indicts hawk and dove alike, and it turns the usual anxiety inside out: the risk isn't that America is declining, it's that America has talked itself into acting as though it has, and is walking away from the burdens of a role it still, uniquely, occupies. For everyone living in the gap — the middle powers, the small states, the regions that were stabilized (for better and worse) by a hegemon that at least showed up — the "multipolar moment" can mean not liberation into a balanced world but abandonment into an ungoverned one, where the one actor big enough to underwrite order has decided the story says it needn't bother. The map that says "power is everywhere now" is the most consequential fiction in circulation, because states are setting real policy by it. If Mohan is right, half the chess players at the table are moving pieces on a board that doesn't exist — and the costs of that error land first, as always, on the squares nobody was defending.

*Source: Mohan, Foreign Affairs (Mar/Apr 2026). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The order that was never neutral

**What happened.** If the first story is about who holds power now, the second is about the thing everyone claims we are losing: the "rules-based international order," the neutral machinery of experts and institutions that supposedly floated above politics. In a bracing [*Aeon* essay](https://aeon.co/essays/are-we-witnessing-the-end-of-the-technocratic-international), the historian Jan Eijking argues that this order was never the view from nowhere it advertised. Tracing the nineteenth-century roots of modern international organizations — the technical bodies that built the Suez Canal, wired the world telegraph, standardized the rails — he shows they were, from the start, instruments of capitalist expansion and empire as much as of cooperation, "a gated community sheltered from its own conditions of existence." His sharpest line inverts the whole self-image of technocracy: the loudest champions of *independence from politics* were reliably the very people who set the terms of politics in the first place — "to soar above the mists of prejudice," he writes, "often was the privilege of none other than the mist-makers."

**Why it matters.** We are told daily that a neutral, rules-bound order is unraveling, and the grief is real — but Eijking's history reframes what is actually being lost. If the technocratic international was always partly a machine for laundering power into the language of expertise and neutrality, then its crisis is less a fall from grace than the exposure of a fiction that had grown too threadbare to maintain. This matters enormously for how you respond: mourn a lost neutrality and you'll try to restore something that never existed; recognize the "view from nowhere" as always having been a view from *somewhere*, wielded by those who could afford to call their own position objective, and you ask the sharper question — not *how do we get the neutral order back* but *whose order was it, and who should build the next one, on what terms.* For everyone who was administered by that order rather than authoring it, the difference between those two questions is the whole game.

*Source: Eijking, Aeon (Jul 31, 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. When connection becomes the weapon

**What happened.** The third story is the oldest and the most beloved: that trade, finance, and connection knit nations together and make war unthinkable. In 2026 it is visibly running in reverse. The interdependence that was supposed to pacify has become the arena of coercion — chokepoints, sanctions, export controls, the deliberate weaponizing of the pipes that link economies (the phenomenon Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman named "weaponized interdependence"). And the clearest window onto the new game is the Gulf. A recent [Observer Research Foundation study](https://www.orfonline.org/research/mapping-gulf-sovereign-wealth-funds-in-the-global-energy-transition-capital-technology-and-diplomacy) maps how Gulf sovereign wealth funds — now roughly 40% of all sovereign-fund assets on earth, six of the world's ten largest — are deploying patient, long-horizon capital into hydrogen, critical minerals, and clean-energy supply chains, absorbing first-mover risk to build positions in the *next* energy order. The point isn't the returns. It's the leverage: converting today's oil rents into structural stakes that make both Washington and Beijing need you, while binding you to neither.

**Why it matters.** Call it strategic optionality — the doctrine of the age for anyone who isn't a superpower. If the world has one real pole (brief 1) and the neutral order was always a fiction (brief 2), then the rational move for a middle power is neither to pick a patron nor to trust the rules, but to make yourself *load-bearing* in as many rival systems as possible, so no single actor can afford to crush you and none can fully own you. Capital becomes statecraft; a wealth fund becomes a foreign ministry. This is the affirmative strategy hiding inside the two prior deflations, and it has obvious resonance from Beirut, where the art of surviving among larger powers by being useful to all of them and captive to none is not a think-tank abstraction but ancient regional muscle memory. The dream of *doux commerce* — connection as the solvent of conflict — has not died. It has been repurposed. The same wires that were meant to carry peace now carry pressure, and the winners are the ones who own a length of every wire.

*Source: ORF (Feb 2026); concept: Farrell & Newman, "Weaponized Interdependence." ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**Jevons comes for the doomers** — the cleanest hype-deflation of the week, and it's about jobs. [Fortune reports](https://fortune.com/article/why-hasnt-ai-boom-displaced-call-center-workers-employment-offshoring-india-philippines/) that far from gutting offshore call centers, the AI boom has coincided with their *expansion* — Philippine call-center headcount has nearly doubled to about 2 million over the decade, and unemployment in both the Philippines and India kept falling through mid-2026. The economist Torsten Slok names the mechanism as Jevons' paradox for labor: "lower cost per interaction does not mean fewer interactions" — cheaper service opens markets, languages, and channels that were previously too costly to bother with, expanding the pie of work rather than shrinking it. The lump-of-labor intuition fails again, exactly where everyone was surest it wouldn't. (Aug 31)

**The folly of rewarding A** — a fresh study in [*Harvard Business Review*](https://hbr.org/2026/09/why-cash-rewards-dont-always-produce-better-ideas) finds that when firms paid cash for employee ideas, they got *fewer but better* submissions — the money narrowed the funnel instead of widening it. Which is the fiftieth-anniversary vindication of Steven Kerr's 1975 management classic "On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B": organisms seek out what is actually rewarded and do that, to the exclusion of what you merely hoped for. Whether the subject is OKRs, agent "performance goals," or a bonus pool, the failure mode is ancient and it is architectural — you get what you pay for, not what you wanted. (Sep 2026)

**Founder mode, deflated** — a useful contrarian jab from Ben Saltiel, ["Why Founder Mode Is Bad Management Advice."](https://www.serviceableinsights.com/p/founder-mode-is-stupid) His claim: the viral "founder mode" gospel said nothing new and got weaponized into permission to centralize control and indulge ego, when the actual variable behind great operators is willingness to take big swings — a trait plenty of *non-founder* CEOs (Slootman, Nadella, Narayen) have had in abundance. A small but bracing reminder that the most seductive business stories are often just flattery with a diagram. (2026)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Passions and the Interests** — Albert O. Hirschman, 1977. Long before capitalism was defended on grounds of growth or freedom, its most sophisticated early advocates sold it as *political therapy*: commerce, Montesquieu and others argued, would channel humanity's wild, violent passions into the cool, predictable pursuit of interest — making rulers calculable and war less rational. Hirschman's slim masterpiece reconstructs how a civilization talked itself into a new economic order by promising it would *tame politics.* Why now: it is the deep genealogy of this issue's third story. The doux-commerce dream that connection breeds peace wasn't naïve marketing — it was the founding argument of the modern economy — which makes its 2026 inversion, connection as coercion, not a betrayal of the idea but the return of the passions the interests were supposed to have caged. ([Public Books](https://www.publicbooks.org/albert-o-hirschmans-the-passions-and-the-interests/), 1977)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the essence of life is a motor** — from the world of grand strategy to the world of the very small: [Quanta's Natalie Wolchover](https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-physical-life-force-turns-biologys-wheels-20260420/) reports that biologists have, after roughly fifty years, finally worked out the bacterial flagellar motor — a genuine rotary engine, spinning a tail-like propeller at several hundred revolutions per second to drive a single cell toward food. She finds in it something close to the essence of aliveness: not a metaphor, an actual wheel, a nanoscale machine older than us by billions of years, quietly turning inside the simplest things that live. A palate cleanser, and a reminder that the deepest machinery on Earth was never ours to invent. (Apr 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The three stories in this issue are all cracking at once, and it is tempting to read that as decay — the end of order, the death of neutrality, the failure of the peace that trade was meant to buy. But there is another way to hold it. A story that breaks was doing work, and its breaking is also a revealing: the multipolar map cracks and shows us that one power still, uniquely, could choose to underwrite the world and is choosing not to; the neutral-order myth cracks and shows us the power that was always hiding inside the word "neutral"; the doux-commerce dream cracks and shows us that connection was never innocent, only useful. What is exposed underneath is not chaos but *choice* — the human decisions that the stories had made to look like nature. Maria Popova, writing this month about the art historian George Kubler and his idea of a life as ["a meteor of actuality,"](https://www.themarginalian.org/2026/08/15/george-kubler-actuality/) puts the deeper move plainly: we are, she writes, "aflame with aliveness only as we burn through the atmosphere of now" — and yet we constellate meaning out of the signals of what has already burned. That is the discipline these three fractures ask of us. Not to mourn the maps, and not to pretend the territory has no shape, but to read the signals honestly — to see that world order was always a thing people made and told themselves stories about, and is therefore a thing people can make again, differently, on purpose. The stories that held the world up are showing their seams. That is not the same as the world coming apart. It is the moment when the authorship becomes visible again — and authorship, unlike nature, is something you can pick up.
