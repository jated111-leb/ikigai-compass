# The Daily Compass #086 — Builders by building

*Monday, October 26, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In February 1936 an aeronautical engineer named Theodore Paul Wright, of the Curtiss-Wright company, published a paper in the *Journal of the Aeronautical Sciences* with an unglamorous title: "Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes". Wright had noticed a regularity in the factory data. Each time the cumulative number of aircraft of a given type doubled, the labour needed to build them fell by a roughly constant percentage, about 20% on average across the industry. The first plane was expensive; the second was cheaper; the hundredth was far cheaper again, not because anyone had invented a new machine but because the workers, the managers and the factory itself had learned. The pattern became known as the learning curve, or Wright's law, and it has since been found in everything from Liberty ships to solar panels. In 2013 Béla Nagy, J. Doyne Farmer, Quan Bui and Jessika Trancik tested six proposed "laws" of technological progress against data on 62 technologies and found that Wright's simple rule forecast falling costs best. This issue is about learning by doing: how it became a weapon of corporate strategy, why the knowledge often lives in the system rather than in individuals, and whether AI lets people skip the curve or quietly takes it away from them.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The curve as a weapon

**What happened.** Wright's observation became a strategy in the 1960s, when Bruce Henderson's young Boston Consulting Group studied the costs of a semiconductor maker and found the same pattern on a larger scale. BCG's "experience curve" claimed that total unit costs, not just labour, fell by 20% to 30% with each doubling of accumulated volume, and its [own industry studies](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2013/growth-business-unit-strategy-experience-curve-bcg-classics-revisited) found declines of 10% to 25%. Henderson drew a bold conclusion. If costs fall with cumulative output, then the company with the largest market share accumulates experience fastest, gains the lowest costs and can use them to win still more share. Market share was therefore worth buying, even at a loss, and the logic fed directly into BCG's famous growth-share matrix of "stars", "cash cows" and "dogs". A decade later William Abernathy and Kenneth Wayne published the warning that every strategist should read alongside it, ["Limits of the Learning Curve"](https://acawiki.org/Limits_of_the_learning_curve) (*Harvard Business Review*, 1974). Their central case was the Ford Model T, which rode the experience curve brilliantly: its price fell from about $850 to below $300 as production soared. But Ford's plants became so specialised around one design that when customers wanted something different, the changeover to the Model A in 1927 shut production for months, and Ford lost its leadership to General Motors.

**Why it matters.** The experience curve remains one of the most powerful ideas in strategy, and it explains a great deal of the modern economy, from the dominance of Chinese battery and solar makers to the economics of chips and AI. But Abernathy and Wayne identified the trap built into it. Driving down the curve means standardising, specialising and cutting variety, and each of those makes it harder to change when the market or the technology moves. The firm that wins on cost can lose on adaptability. For leaders, the question is not whether to pursue scale and experience, which is often essential, but how to keep some capacity for change alongside it: a second product line, a protected team working on the next design, or a willingness to accept higher costs in exchange for flexibility. The cheapest producer of yesterday's product is not always the winner.

*Source: Wright, Journal of the Aeronautical Sciences (1936); BCG Classics Revisited (2013); Abernathy & Wayne, HBR 52(5) (1974). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 2. The knowledge lives in the line

**What happened.** If organisations learn by doing, who exactly does the learning? Three studies point to the same surprising answer. In ["Toward an Understanding of Learning by Doing"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18017) (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2013), Steven Levitt, John List and Chad Syverson studied a car assembly plant making some 200,000 vehicles a year. Defect rates fell steeply as production accumulated, halving with each tenfold increase in cumulative output. When the plant added a second shift, the new workers did not start again at the top of the curve: their defect rates began below those of the first shift. Most of the knowledge, the authors concluded, had quickly become embodied in the plant's equipment, procedures and organisation rather than staying in the heads of individual workers. Studies of heart surgeons show the other side of that coin. Robert Huckman and Gary Pisano, in ["The Firm Specificity of Individual Performance"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v52y2006i4p473-488.html) (*Management Science*, 2006), examined surgeons who operated at more than one hospital and found that their patients' outcomes improved with the surgeon's recent volume at that particular hospital, not with operations performed elsewhere. Skill was partly tied to familiarity with a specific team and its systems. An earlier study by Pisano, Richard Bohmer and Amy Edmondson of 16 hospitals adopting the same new minimally invasive heart operation found that, despite identical training, the hospitals learned at very different rates, and that teamwork characterised the fastest learners.

**Why it matters.** These findings change how to think about talent and know-how. Much of what an organisation learns does not walk out of the door when a star leaves, because it is built into processes, tools and the habits of teams; equally, a star hired from elsewhere may not bring as much as their record suggests, because part of their performance belonged to their old team. For leaders, the practical lessons are to capture learning deliberately in the system, through checklists, tooling and documented changes, so that it compounds and can be transferred, and to treat stable, familiar teams as an asset. When a new shift, a new site or a new market starts up, the goal is the car plant's achievement: to let it begin partway down the curve rather than at the top.

*Source: Levitt, List & Syverson, JPE 121(4) (2013); Huckman & Pisano, Management Science 52(4) (2006); Pisano, Bohmer & Edmondson, Management Science 47(6) (2001). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Can you skip the curve?

**What happened.** Generative AI promises to let beginners produce expert-level work on day one. A new study asks the follow-up question: does that work build expertise, or replace it? In ["Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise?"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35720) (NBER Working Paper 35720, autumn 2026), David Autor of MIT and colleagues ran a pre-registered, three-month field experiment with 133 practising patent lawyers at 11 American law firms. About two-thirds were given an AI drafting assistant, a pre-release tool from Google Labs, whose researchers co-authored the study; the rest worked without it. Blinded senior patent attorneys graded all the work. With the tool, the quality of drafts rose by about a third of a standard deviation, and junior lawyers gained most. The revealing test came at the end, when everyone reviewed a patent application without AI. Lawyers who had used the assistant outperformed the control group, but the whole of that advantage came from senior lawyers. Junior lawyers showed no average improvement. Their results instead spread out: fewer mediocre scores, but more poor ones as well as more good ones.

**Why it matters.** The study suggests that AI can lift the quality of work immediately while having uneven effects on the learning that normally comes from doing that work. Experienced people seem able to use the tool as a sparring partner and absorb its lessons; for some beginners it was a springboard, for others a crutch. The authors conclude that foundational expertise may be a prerequisite for turning AI-assisted practice into durable skill. It is one study, in one profession, with a tool built by a company involved in the research, and three months is short. But the question it raises is central for any organisation that depends on developing junior people into senior ones. If the doing is handed to a machine, firms will need to design the learning back in: deliberate practice without the tool, review of AI-assisted work by experienced colleagues, and careful attention to which juniors are thriving and which are merely being carried.

*Source: Autor, Rodchenko, Martin et al., NBER WP 35720 (2026); VoxEU column "Better work, not always better workers". ~40 min (paper).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the ships that were built in days** — the Liberty ships of the Second World War are the textbook case of learning by doing. Some 2,700 were built in American yards, and building times collapsed: the first took about 245 days, while later ships took weeks. In November 1942 a Richmond, California yard launched the SS *Robert E. Peary* four days and 15 hours after laying its keel, a publicity stunt that was never repeated. But in a careful study using newly available records from the Calship yard, ["How Much Did the Liberty Shipbuilders Learn?"](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wpa/wuwpdc/9712001.html), the economist Peter Thompson found that rising output per worker went hand in hand with more capital per worker and with falling quality, measured by the risk of a ship developing serious hull fractures. Together with changes in technology and capacity use, these explained "virtually all" of the productivity gain. Not every downward curve is learning; some of it is investment, and some is cutting corners. (Thompson, Journal of Political Economy, 2001)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the chimpanzee who hands over the tool** — camera traps at the Dindefelo reserve in Senegal, studied by researchers from the University of Barcelona and the Jane Goodall Institute Spain, captured 33 occasions on which wild chimpanzees passed tools to others, used for dipping for ants, gathering algae and fishing for termites, along with four transfers of fruit. Most went from mothers to their own offspring, and in one sequence a mother appears to show her daughter how to dip for ants. Published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, the [study](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/cameras-capture-adult-chimpanzees-passing-tools-and-know-how-to-their-young-teaching-important-skills-to-the-next-generation-180989538/) argues that such handovers help young chimps climb their own learning curve, by giving them a working tool before they can make one. Apprenticeship, it seems, is older than our species. (Frontiers in Psychology / Smithsonian, Sep–Oct 2026)

**The plunging price of thought** — a new analysis by Luke Emberson and David Roodman of Epoch AI estimates that the cost of reaching a fixed level of AI performance on a range of benchmarks has [fallen by about 47% a quarter](https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought) since 2023, roughly 13-fold a year, a faster decline than Epoch finds for DNA sequencing, computing or batteries. Prices fall fastest just after a capability first appears and then slow, as early sellers' premiums are competed away. Epoch notes caveats, including models tuned to the benchmarks. It measures the fall against time rather than cumulative output, but as the 2013 study in today's opening found, when production grows exponentially the two kinds of curve are almost impossible to tell apart. For strategists the lesson is that a lead at the AI frontier buys pricing power for quarters, not years. (Epoch AI, Sep 22, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

More than two thousand years before Wright looked at his factory data, Aristotle made the same observation about people. "For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them," he wrote in the second book of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "e.g. men become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre." His subject was character rather than cost: we become just, he argued, by doing just acts, and brave by doing brave ones. It is a sentence worth holding against today's evidence. The aircraft workers, the car plant and the surgeons all show that skill is built through repetition inside a particular place and team. The patent lawyers show what is at stake as machines take over more of the doing. Tools that save us effort are welcome; most progress consists of them. But some effort is not waste. It is how the builder becomes a builder, and an organisation that removes all of it may find, a few years on, that it has very good output and very few people who know how to produce it.
