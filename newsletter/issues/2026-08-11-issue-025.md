# The Daily Compass #025 — The balance of power

*Tuesday, August 11, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Yesterday Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word case that the way to make superintelligence safe is to give it to everyone — that concentration, not diffusion, is the real danger. It landed in the same month the US government's answer to the same question is a framework so centralized it's classified. So today is about the balance of power: the promise that distributing intelligence keeps us safe, the organizational reality that intelligence was never really held by individuals in the first place, and an economist's demand that everyone shouting about the risk produce a number you can check. Behind all three stands a 1958 warning that no balance is ever automatic.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The future is for everyone

**What happened.** Zuckerberg published ["The Future Is for Everyone"](https://www.meta.com/thefutureisforeveryone/), a manifesto built on three claims: individual empowerment is the source of prosperity, invention (not automation) is superintelligence's real purpose, and balance of power is the foundation of safety. The load-bearing argument is that no single AI system can be aligned with everyone's competing values at once — so concentrating superintelligence in a few labs, a government, or the AI itself is inherently risky. The fix, he says, is to put it in billions of individual hands, the way PCs and the internet were eventually distributed. The reception was sharp: The Register retitled the vision ["the future is for billionaires — the rest of us will get open weight AI models, maybe"](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/10/the-future-is-for-billionaires-the-rest-of-us-will-get-open-weight-ai-models-maybe/5285690), and Zuckerberg himself said he was [surprised the discourse from AI companies is "so filled with doom."](https://variety.com/2026/biz/news/meta-ai-manifesto-mark-zuckerberg-1236831435/)

**Why it matters.** Strip the corporate voice and this is a genuine philosophical position, and a timely one: a frontier CEO staking the anti-concentration flag in the exact fortnight the government's own frontier framework became a secret nobody outside the executive branch can read (issues #018, #022). Two rival safety philosophies, stated at once — safety by *distribution* versus safety by *classification*. The hard question is whether diffusion is a safety architecture or a business model wearing one: Meta's open-weights strategy commoditizes the model layer that its rivals sell, so "put it in everyone's hands" is also "make my competitors' crown jewels free." Both can be true. But the deeper move is that Zuckerberg has reframed the whole fight from *how powerful* to *who holds it* — the question Landemore raised about participation (#019) and Mangena about whose values ship by default (#021), now asked by the person with the most compute. Distribution is a real answer. It is not obviously a safe one.

*Sources: Meta (Aug 10); The Register, Variety (Aug 10). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. Intelligence was never yours alone

**What happened.** Knowledge at Wharton ran a useful counterweight — ["Who Gets Replaced by AI and Why?"](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/who-gets-replaced-by-ai-and-why/) — in which management scholar Henning Piezunka reframes the replacement question away from individual tasks and toward *collective* intelligence: how an organization accesses, develops, and applies knowledge held across people, not in any one head. Research he cites from Pinar Yildirim adds a sharp org-psychology finding: drop AI into the wrong part of a team's workflow and you can damage employee motivation even while raw output rises.

**Why it matters.** This is the reality-check brief 1's "personal superintelligence" needs. Zuckerberg's picture is one superintelligence per person, empowerment distributed atom by atom. But intelligence inside a working organization was never individual — it lives in the relationships, the handoffs, the shared judgment of a group, and that is exactly what a mis-placed tool degrades. The unit that gets more or less capable is the *system*, not the seat. It rhymes with the task-space finding from #018 (teams beat individuals only on the right kind of task) and with Lancefield's warning last issue about convergence toward the generic: give everyone the same powerful assistant and you may flatten the very diversity that made the collective smart. The distribution that matters isn't how many people hold a model. It's where, in a web of people, the machine lands — and whether it strengthens the connective tissue or dissolves it.

*Source: Piezunka, Knowledge at Wharton (Aug 5). ~12 min.*

### ◆ 3. Act like it is science

**What happened.** Tyler Cowen, responding to the doom-laden reactions to the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident, made a bracing epistemic demand in ["Act like it is science"](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/act-like-it-is-science.html): if you're genuinely worried about the future cybersecurity cost of AI break-ins, then produce two things — a *methodology* for estimating that cost, and a *falsifiable numerical estimate* that reality can later check. Not vibes, not vividness. A number, and a way to be proven wrong.

**Why it matters.** This is the discipline the entire balance-of-power argument is missing. Zuckerberg is "surprised the discourse is so filled with doom"; the doomers are certain he is reckless; and — Cowen's point — neither side is offering a checkable prediction, so the fight can run forever without anyone being right or wrong. It's last issue's Berlin turned toward forecasting: judgment as the discrimination of what actually matters, made accountable by a falsifiable claim. It's also #020's verification argument wearing new clothes — cooperation and trust need something concrete to check against, and so does fear. The move generalizes far past AI: any high-stakes debate gets better the instant someone is forced to write down a number and a date. Calibrated fear beats loud fear, not because the calm are wiser, but because only the calibrated can be corrected.

*Source: Cowen, Marginal Revolution (Aug 10). ~5 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — the secret White House frontier-AI framework produced no new leak or congressional movement past August 10; it stands complete, voluntary on paper, and unreadable, its August 1 deadline lapsed without a public deliverable. Zvi's long-promised deep-read of the FRONTIER Act remains unwritten. A quiet cycle — reported as one.

**Governing the machine that improves itself** — Jack Clark's Import AI 468 rounds up 23 ideas (from the think tank IFP) for governing the automation of AI R&D — the recursive-self-improvement problem — and argues that *trust and transparency* are what decide whether competing labs "race forever," drawing an explicit analogy to nuclear arms control. The governance question brief 1 raises, taken to its logical end: distributing intelligence is one thing; distributing the intelligence that *designs* intelligence is another. ([Import AI](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-468-23-rsi-ideas-posttrainbench), Aug 10)

**Both referees are unfit** — Nathan Lambert's read on the in-development-model hacks: our two governing power structures — hypercompetitive scaling labs, and a federal government that only reacts after measurable harm and then overreacts — both have incentive systems badly suited to a fast technological transition. Not the incident, the institutions. ([Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/lessons-from-the-hacks), Aug 9; paid)

**Sequencing over simultaneity** — A. Wess Mitchell's grand-strategy argument travels well beyond its case: a power facing two rivals at once should concentrate against the more disruptive near-term threat first rather than hedge weakly against both. Prioritization as the actual discipline of scarce resources — the oldest strategic virtue, and the hardest to hold under political pressure to cover everything. ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/sequencing-over-simultaneity-how-to-avert-a-two-front-war/), Apr 28)

**Agency as attunement** — Henrik Karlsson reframes the most over-macho word in personal strategy: agency isn't hard-charging aggression but a gentle attunement — feeling for the grain of reality and the path of least resistance, then leaning in. He reports that six weeks of thinking about it full-time visibly changed how he acted. The quiet complement to last issue's brief on the judgment leaders must not delegate. ([Escaping Flatland](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/agency), May 2026)

**The billion-dollar résumé** — Matt Levine's recurring, wonderful heresy, resurfacing via the "Situational Awareness" fund saga: it can be *good for your career* to lose a billion dollars, because it is proof that someone trusted you with real capital and that you took a real swing. Reputation is built by the size of the bets you were handed, not only by whether they paid. ([Money Stuff](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/banking-law/matt-levines-money-stuff-the-situation-is-fine), Aug 10)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Delicate Balance of Terror** — Albert Wohlstetter, RAND, 1958. The foundational argument that deterrence is *not* automatic: a stable balance requires constant, expensive engineering — survivable second-strike forces, ceaseless vigilance — and the real danger is assuming the balance maintains itself. Why now: Zuckerberg's third principle is "balance of power as the foundation of safety," stated as if balance were self-sustaining. Wohlstetter's whole life's warning was that it never is — a balance looks stable only because someone is paying, continuously, to keep it so. ([RAND, P-1472](https://www.rand.org/pubs/papers/P1472.html), 1958)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the ethics of the shortcut** — the inaugural Aeon Philosophy Prize went to ["When Is Stereotyping a Handy Tool, and When Is It a Sin?"](https://aeon.co/essays/when-is-stereotyping-a-handy-tool-and-when-is-it-a-sin) — on the tension between the cognitive necessity of generalizing and the moral duty to treat each person as an individual. Read in this issue's light it's uncanny: a machine is a generalization engine, a device that does nothing *but* infer the particular from the pattern. The essay is, accidentally, the ethics of the one move AI cannot stop making. ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/when-is-stereotyping-a-handy-tool-and-when-is-it-a-sin), Aug 10)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Under all of today's argument about who should hold machine intelligence sits a stranger question, and 3 Quarks Daily went straight at it yesterday: Morgan Meis asking [whether life is fundamentally computational](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/is-life-fundamentally-computational.html). It's the floor beneath the whole debate. If mind really is computation all the way down, then the fight over distributing superintelligence is, quietly, a fight over how to share out a piece of ourselves — and Wohlstetter's warning cuts deeper than deterrence, because the balance we'd be trying to keep would be a balance *inside* the human project, not just between its powers. If mind isn't reducible to computation, then Zuckerberg is distributing something powerful but partial, and the part that decides what any of it is *for* stays stubbornly, expensively human. Either way the 1958 lesson holds: no balance maintains itself. Someone always has to keep paying to hold it — and the first bill, as ever, is the willingness to say precisely what you fear and let the world check whether you were right.
