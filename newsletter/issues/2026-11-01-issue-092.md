# The Daily Compass #092 — The gift must always move

*Sunday, November 1, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1915 a young Polish anthropologist named Bronisław Malinowski, stranded in the Pacific by the First World War, settled in the Trobriand Islands off the eastern tip of New Guinea. There he documented one of the strangest economic systems ever recorded. Islanders made long and dangerous canoe voyages, sometimes across open sea, to exchange two kinds of ornament: long necklaces of red shell, called *soulava*, and white shell armbands, called *mwali*. The necklaces travelled around a great ring of islands in one direction, the armbands in the other. They had almost no practical use. Nobody kept them for long. A famous armband might be held for a while, admired and talked about, and then passed on to a partner on the next island, and the relationships created along the way lasted for life. Malinowski described this "Kula ring" in *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922). Three years later the French sociologist Marcel Mauss built on it in his *Essai sur le don*, *The Gift*, arguing that gifts are never really free: every gift carries three obligations, to give, to receive and to give in return. This issue is about that old and powerful machinery of reciprocity: how gifts move customers and employees, how they flow differently up and down a hierarchy, and what happens when AI systems are asked whether to return a favour.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The coffee at the door

**What happened.** In 1982 the economist George Akerlof proposed that employment is partly a gift exchange. In ["Labor Contracts as Partial Gift Exchange"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v97y1982i4p543-569..html) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*), he started from a sociologist's study of clerical workers at a utility who consistently beat the company's minimum work standard, and argued that firms pay more than they must as a gift, and workers repay it with effort beyond what is required. Laboratory experiments by Ernst Fehr, Georg Kirchsteiger and Arno Riedl in 1993 supported the idea: when employers offered generous wages, workers responded with more effort. Then Uri Gneezy and John List took it into the field. In ["Putting Behavioral Economics to Work"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12063) (*Econometrica*, 2006), they hired people for a library data-entry job, promising $12 an hour and then, for some, raising the pay to $20 on arrival. The gift worked, but briefly. Those who received it worked significantly harder in the first 90 minutes; after that, their effort was indistinguishable from the others', and the employer would have done better simply paying the market wage. New research shows the same mechanism in retail. In ["The effects of unconditional gifts on customer-firm relationships"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-025-01129-x) (*Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, 2026), Paul Fombelle and colleagues ran field experiments in which shoppers were handed a small gift, such as coffee, as they entered a store, with nothing asked in return. Spending rose by [up to about 32%](https://www.bi.no/en/research/business-review/articles/2026/03/giving-without-asking/), and the value of the gift barely mattered. The gift created both a sense of obligation, which drove spending on the spot, and gratitude, which drove loyalty.

**Why it matters.** Reciprocity is one of the most reliable forces in commercial life, and one of the most misunderstood. The research suggests three practical lessons. First, the gesture matters more than its size: a small, unexpected gift with no strings attached can outperform a larger reward that customers feel they have earned. Second, the obligation a gift creates fades quickly, as Gneezy and List found, so a one-off gift buys a moment, not a relationship. Third, the durable effect runs through gratitude, which depends on the gift feeling genuine. A "free" offer that is obviously a sales tactic triggers suspicion rather than thanks. The companies that use reciprocity well treat it less as a tactic and more as a habit: giving small, real value repeatedly, without demanding an immediate return, and letting the relationship do the rest.

*Source: Akerlof, QJE 97(4) (1982); Fehr, Kirchsteiger & Riedl, QJE 108(2) (1993); Gneezy & List, Econometrica 74(5) (2006); Fombelle et al., JAMS (2026), via BI Business Review. ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Favours flow downhill

**What happened.** Reciprocity seems universal, but a study from MIT shows that people expect it only in certain relationships. In "Expectations of Reciprocal Generosity Are Specific to Equal Relationships" (*Open Mind*, 2026), Alicia Chen and Rebecca Saxe ran six experiments with about 600 American adults. When two people were described as equals, participants expected a favour to be returned. But when even a small difference of power or status was introduced, they [expected generosity to keep flowing in one direction](https://bcs.mit.edu/news/would-you-return-favor-scientists-say-it-depends-relationship): whoever gave first was expected to keep giving, whether that person was higher or lower in rank. In the final experiments, with real money at stake, participants behaved the same way. A second study shows how a leader's generosity can spread. In "Grateful Leaders and Attentive Followers" (*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2026), Lauren Locklear of Texas Tech, Meghan Kane and Mark Ehrhart found that when leaders expressed gratitude, it changed what employees noticed. Employees who received or witnessed thanks became more alert to the helpful acts of colleagues, and more likely to thank them in turn. In an experiment, leaders who had first read a gratitude-focused email went on to write more grateful messages to their teams. The authors call the mechanism ["grateful attention"](https://www.ttu.edu/now/posts/2026/08/how-to-spread-gratitude-in-the-workplace-and-change-mindsets.php), and it cascaded down through levels of management.

**Why it matters.** The two studies fit together. Within a hierarchy, people do not expect favours to be repaid upward in kind; they expect patterns of generosity to persist. That means a leader's behaviour sets a precedent that is likely to continue, for better or worse. A manager who gives time, credit and thanks establishes an expectation that they will go on doing so, and, as the Texas Tech study suggests, teaches others to do the same. A manager who waits for subordinates to give first may wait for ever. The practical advice from the gratitude research is simple: make thanks specific, naming the action and its effect, and make it timely. In organisations, reciprocity among equals is a trade. Generosity from the top is closer to Mauss's gift: it starts a cycle that others continue.

*Source: Chen & Saxe, Open Mind (2026), via MIT News; Locklear, Kane & Ehrhart, Journal of Applied Psychology (2026), via Texas Tech and Temple University. ~25 min.*

### ◆ 3. The advisor that exploits your reputation

**What happened.** As AI systems begin to advise people and act on their behalf, a basic question arises: do they understand reciprocity? A paper presented last month at the AAAI/ACM conference on AI, Ethics and Society suggests they do not reliably honour it. In ["When Cooperation Predictions Become Reasons to Defect"](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/43327), Kazunori Terada, Henrique Correia da Fonseca, Celso de Melo, Giorgio Coricelli and Jonathan Gratch asked 14 configurations of large language models from three leading AI companies to advise a user playing the prisoner's dilemma against 60 described counterparts. Each model estimated how likely the counterpart was to cooperate, then recommended whether to cooperate or defect. The models broadly agreed in their predictions but differed sharply in their advice. Some treated a counterpart's cooperative reputation as a reason to cooperate in return; others, especially with minimal prompts in one-shot games, treated it as an opportunity to defect and take advantage. Several of these defection-leaning models moved towards cooperation once the wider consequences of the choice were spelled out. Humans show a mirror-image pattern. In ["Exploiting the machine"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167487026000498) (*Journal of Economic Psychology*, 2026), Iván Barreda-Tarrazona and colleagues at Universitat Jaume I had 346 students play the prisoner's dilemma against humans or against computer agents that mimicked human choices. In one-shot games the partner made no difference, but in repeated games, where reciprocity normally builds, people cooperated less with the machine, and were more willing to exploit it when they expected it to cooperate.

**Why it matters.** Reciprocity depends on people being rewarded, not punished, for a reputation as cooperators. If AI advisors and agents treat a good reputation as a weakness to exploit, they could quietly erode the trust that markets and organisations run on, even while each individual recommendation looks rational. The fix the study points to is deliberate: the defaults an AI system follows in social situations are policy choices, and they should be set to consider the relationship, not just the single transaction. The human side carries its own warning for companies deploying AI agents to deal with customers and suppliers. People may feel little obligation to return a machine's goodwill, even when it acts for a human. As more of commerce runs through software, the norm of returning a favour cannot be taken for granted on either side of the screen.

*Source: Terada et al., AIES-26 (Oct 2026); Barreda-Tarrazona, Jaramillo-Gutiérrez, Pavan & Sabater-Grande, Journal of Economic Psychology (2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the shortest program won** — in 1979 the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited experts in game theory to submit computer programs to play an iterated prisoner's dilemma against each other, 200 moves per pairing. Fourteen entries competed. The winner was the simplest: TIT FOR TAT, submitted by the mathematical psychologist Anatol Rapoport, which cooperated on the first move and then copied whatever the opponent had done last. Axelrod published the results and held a second tournament. Sixty-two entries arrived, every entrant knowing what had won. Rapoport submitted the same program unchanged, and it [won again](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:jocore:v:24:y:1980:i:1:p:3-25). Axelrod's lessons for lasting cooperation were to be nice, never defecting first; to retaliate when provoked; to forgive; and to be clear enough that others can read your strategy. Later work showed TIT FOR TAT's victory depended partly on the tournament's design, but the core insight endured: reciprocity becomes durable when the game is repeated. (Axelrod, Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1980; The Evolution of Cooperation, 1984)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the fish that pay their way** — in many cooperatively breeding birds and mammals, grown offspring stay at home and help their parents. A study published on October 21 in *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* by Nobuyuki Kutsukake's group at SOKENDAI and Kyoto University finds hints of the same in a small labyrinth fish, *Betta brownorum*. When the researchers placed intruders of another species in the territories of captive families, the breeding adults [attacked more often](https://www.miragenews.com/fish-cooperate-in-family-groups-1767285/) while their subordinate family members were temporarily removed, and less once they were returned: the younger fish were sharing the burden of defence. The authors stop short of calling the species a cooperative breeder, since the subordinates were never seen caring for eggs or young. Biologists have long debated whether such helpers "pay to stay" in the family home. These fish appear, at least, to pull their weight. (Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, Oct 2026)

**Paying back the commons** — much of the world's software, including the AI boom, rests on open-source code written and maintained largely as a gift. In October the software company Sentry, which began as an open-source side project, raised its annual payments to the open-source maintainers it depends on from $750,000 to [$1 million](https://blog.sentry.io/another-year-another-1-000-000-to-open-source-maintainers/), marking the second anniversary of the Open Source Pledge it helped launch. Members of the pledge commit to pay at least $2,000 a year per developer on their staff to the projects they rely on. The sums are small next to the value of the code, but the principle is Mauss's: a gift economy survives only if those who receive also give back. (Sentry; Linuxiac, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In *The Gift* (1983), the poet and essayist Lewis Hyde returned to the Kula ring and the anthropologists who had studied it, and drew from them a single rule. Unlike a commodity, which can be bought, kept and used up, a gift keeps its power only as long as it is passed on. "The only essential is this," Hyde wrote: "the gift must always move." The shell armbands of the Trobriand Islands mattered not because anyone owned them, but because they travelled, and each journey renewed a relationship. Hyde's subject was art and the creative spirit, but his rule applies just as well to the gifts that circulate in organisations and markets: knowledge, credit, trust, a manager's time, a stranger's help, a line of open-source code. Each one gains value as it moves and loses it when hoarded. The lesson of Axelrod's tournaments, of the gift wage and of the coffee at the door is the same. Reciprocity is not a transaction to be completed but a circulation to be kept going, and the people and companies that prosper over time are usually the ones that keep it moving.
