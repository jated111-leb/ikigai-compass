# The Daily Compass #053 — The sorting machine

*Friday, September 18, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

For about seventy years, the rich world ran on a single quiet promise: that brains and effort would carry you upward, that the child who was clever and worked hard could rise past the circumstances of birth, and that the resulting hierarchy — the doctors and lawyers and professors and engineers at the top — was therefore *fair*, earned, legitimate. We called this arrangement meritocracy, and it did two jobs at once. It sorted people, and it justified the sort. It told the winners they deserved their winning and the losers they had only themselves to blame. This week, three very different stories converged on the same disquieting point: the machine that did the sorting is being dismantled, and the thing dismantling it is artificial intelligence. Not by taking jobs, exactly — that story is old — but by attacking the deeper thing underneath, the belief that human cognitive merit is scarce, valuable, and the rightful basis of who gets ahead. This issue is about what happens to a civilization built on rewarding intelligence when intelligence stops being the rarest thing in the room. And it opens with the man who saw it coming, and meant it as a warning.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The end of the upward escalator

**What happened.** In a heavyweight essay for *Noema*, the inequality economist Branko Milanović and the writer Nils Gilman argue that AI is coming not merely for jobs but for ["The End of Upward Mobility"](https://www.noemamag.com/the-end-of-upward-mobility/) — for the meritocratic order itself. Their frame is historical: James Burnham's 1941 *The Managerial Revolution* predicted that capitalism would give way to "managerialism," a system run by the professional-managerial class who knew how to make complex organizations work. That class — credentialed, cognitive, salaried — has been the spine of the social order and the destination of the upward escalator ever since. Milanović and Gilman's unsettling thesis is that AI dissolves the managerial class's reason for existing: if the machine can do the analysis, the coordination, the expert judgment that justified the professional's salary and status, then the whole edifice of cognitive sorting — the SATs, the degrees, the career ladders — loses its function, and with it the promise that being smart is a reliable path up.

**Why it matters.** This reframes the AI-and-work anxiety into something bigger and more destabilizing than unemployment numbers. A society can survive a lot of joblessness with transfers and make-work; what it cannot easily survive is the collapse of its *legitimating story* — the shared belief about who deserves what and why. For seventy years, inequality was made tolerable by the meritocratic bargain: the gaps were vast, but they were supposedly earned, and the ladder was supposedly open. Remove the belief that cognitive merit is scarce and rewardable, and you don't just have displaced workers; you have a professional class that can no longer explain to itself why it sits where it sits, and an underclass no longer told that the door is open if they're clever enough. Milanović, who has spent a career measuring inequality, is pointing at something the inequality statistics miss: the machine coming apart is not the economy but the *justification*. What replaces managerialism, they cannot yet say. That they are asking the question, in Burnham's terms, is the alarm.

*Source: Milanović & Gilman, Noema (Sep 17, 2026). ~16 min.*

### ◆ 2. When the expert becomes the noise

**What happened.** Descend from the abstract to the operating room. In a pointed *STAT* essay, the bioethicist Ezekiel Emanuel and Abe Baker-Butler argue that ["autonomous AI will beat AI-assisted physicians at some medical tasks by 2030"](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/09/ai-medicine-assisted-physicians-research-autonomy/) — and the emphasis is the sting. Not that AI will beat unaided doctors (old news), but that fully autonomous AI will soon beat *doctors working with AI* at five core cognitive tasks: taking a history, forming a differential diagnosis, ordering the right tests on budget, choosing treatments, and managing chronic disease. The mechanism is brutal to professional self-regard: the human in the loop, they argue, introduces *noise* — inconsistency, fatigue, bias — that degrades the machine's performance rather than improving it. The AMA's CEO John Whyte [fired back](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/09/ai-medicine-doctors-replacement-debate-ama-ceo/) that medicine is more than those tasks, that the evidence is mostly simulated, and that we lack the licensure and liability structures to let a machine practice alone — a point Emanuel conceded.

**Why it matters.** The medical profession is the meritocracy's cathedral — the longest training, the highest status, the surest proof that expertise is real and earned. So the claim that the credentialed expert might be *subtracting* value from an automated process, rather than adding it, is a hammer blow aimed squarely at brief 1's foundation. Note the shape of Whyte's defense, because it is the shape every profession will now reach for: not "the machine can't do the tasks" — increasingly it can — but "there is more to what we do than the tasks," the art, the presence, the human relationship. That may well be true. But it is a retreat from the meritocratic high ground (we are measurably the best at the work) to a softer and less defensible line (we are humanly necessary in ways you can't measure) — and once a profession is arguing that its value lies in the parts that resist measurement, it has already conceded the parts that don't. Medicine is simply the first prestige profession to have the argument out loud. It will not be the last.

*Source: Emanuel & Baker-Butler, and Whyte, STAT (Sep 9, 2026). ~13 min.*

### ◆ 3. The mathematicians revolt

**What happened.** And now the summit. Mathematics is the purest meritocracy humans have ever built — no credentials, no politics, just whether your proof is right — and its highest practitioners have just sounded an alarm. Terence Tao, perhaps the most celebrated living mathematician, and a group of the field's leading figures have raised a collective warning about AI's swift, disruptive entry into mathematical research; Tao's own post, ["A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics,"](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/11/a-severe-misalignment-of-ai-in-mathematics/) and his ICM lecture "Mathematics in the age of AI," frame the moment as a genuine crisis — not of capability but of *values and practices*: what mathematics is *for*, and what happens to it, when machines start generating and racing to claim results faster than the human community can absorb or even check them. The worry is not that the machines are wrong. It is that they are fast, and indifferent to the culture of slow, communal, meaning-laden understanding that mathematicians thought was the point.

**Why it matters.** If the meritocracy has a holy of holies, it is this: the conviction that raw, demonstrable intellectual merit is real, that some minds genuinely are more powerful, and that this is the one hierarchy that is truly earned. When the people at the very top of *that* hierarchy — the ones with the least reason to be defensive, who have spent their lives as the living proof that human genius matters — start warning that something essential is being hollowed out, it is worth more than a hundred think-pieces from the anxious middle. Their revolt completes the arc of this issue. Brief 1 said the meritocratic *bargain* is breaking; brief 2 said a prestige *profession* is being outperformed; and here the *ideal itself* — merit as the deepest and most legitimate basis of human worth — is being questioned by its own champions. The mathematicians are not afraid of losing their jobs. They are afraid of something subtler and sadder: that the thing they gave their lives to, the slow human accumulation of understanding, is being turned into a race their machines will always win, and that winning it was never the point.

*Source: Tao, terrytao.wordpress.com (Sep 11, 2026); ICM 2026 lecture. ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Rise of the Meritocracy** — Michael Young, 1958. The most important thing to know about the word "meritocracy" is that it was coined as an insult. The British sociologist Michael Young invented it in a 1958 satire — a mock-history written from 2034 — describing a dystopian Britain that has replaced the old class hierarchy with a new one sorted purely by intelligence and effort, in which a cognitive elite pulls away from a "populace" it has taught to believe it deserves its own subordination. Young's nightmare was a society *more* unjust than the one it replaced, precisely because its inequalities felt earned and so admitted no appeal. In 2001, watching Tony Blair use the word as a compliment, Young wrote a bitter *Guardian* essay protesting that his warning had been swallowed whole and inverted into a boast. Why now: every essay in this issue is a chapter of the book Young wrote seventy years early. He saw that a society which sorts by merit and then *worships* the sort builds a trap — and the AI era is now springing it from an angle he never imagined, by making the merit itself cheap. Read the dystopia everyone mistook for a manual. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_of_the_Meritocracy), 1958)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the collapse that wasn't** — a bracing antidote to all this civilizational foreboding, from [Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/the-great-myth-of-empire-collapse): the "fall of Rome as catastrophe" story may have the story backwards. Bioarchaeological evidence — skeletons, heights, health — suggests ordinary people living *under* the Roman Empire were often physically worse off (shorter, sicker) than those who came before it or lived beyond its borders, and that the "collapse" looked, from the bottom, less like the lights going out on civilization than like the lifting of an extractive weight. A useful splinter to carry into any issue about a social order ending: the people mourning the fall are usually the ones who were doing well under it, and the "dark age" is often just history written by the newly inconvenienced. Endings are the most misread events there are. (2026)

**The corporate mirror** — the same question this issue asks about people, companies are asking about themselves: when the old moats erode, where does advantage come from? In *Harvard Business Review*, the economist Frank Nagle offers a tidy frame — ["Collaborate on the Core, Compete on the Edges"](https://hbr.org/2026/09/collaborate-on-the-core-compete-on-the-edges) — drawn from open-source and standards fights: rivals should join forces to *build* a shared foundational layer, then compete fiercely to *own* the differentiated edges on top. It's a reminder that "where is value captured" is being re-asked at every level at once — in the career, in the profession, and in the firm — and that the winning answers increasingly involve giving away the thing everyone can now do, and hoarding only the thing they can't. (Sep)

**The robots that never came** — a perfectly timed piece of hype-deflation from Brian Potter's [*Construction Physics*](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/where-has-construction-automation): 170 years of attempts to automate construction, from an 1853 plastering machine onward, and why building stays stubbornly human — direct labor is still roughly half the cost of a US house versus 6–8% of a car. His cut: automation wins in the *factory* and loses on the *site*, where every job is bespoke and the world won't hold still. A necessary counterweight to this issue's momentum, and to the medical and mathematical alarms above: the machine's conquest of human work is real but wildly uneven, and the physical, particular, on-site world has humbled a century and a half of people certain they were about to automate it. (Sep 11)

**Doom as a strategy** — the antitrust writer Matt Stoller offers a cynic's reading of AI's existential-risk talk in ["The AI Doomsday Distraction"](https://www.levernews.com/the-ai-doomsday-distraction/): notice, he says, that the people most loudly warning that AI could end the world are the same people asking to write its rules. His deflationary fix is unglamorous and concrete — ordinary liability and criminal law, which would make companies pay for the harms their products actually cause, would discipline the industry far more reliably than any grand self-regulatory "pause." Whatever you make of it, it's a bracing reframe: some share of the apocalypse talk is not a prediction but a bid for regulatory capture, dressed as conscience. (Sep 14)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

*This issue's argument is, at bottom, the takeoff question wearing a sociologist's coat — how fast, how total, and who is left holding value on the far side. The standing pair remains the best way in:* the [Dwarkesh–Greenblatt](https://www.dwarkesh.com/podcast) case for a fast, sweeping takeoff against [CwT–Acemoglu](https://conversationswithtyler.com/episodes/daron-acemoglu-2/), in which the Nobel economist argues that institutions — not raw capability — decide who actually captures the gains, and whether the meritocracy's collapse ends in a broader distribution of worth or a narrower one. Acemoglu is, in effect, the empirical voice under this whole issue: he has spent a career showing that technology does not have a destiny, that the same machine can widen or narrow a society depending on the choices around it. The comfort, such as it is, is that the sorting machine breaking is not the same as the future being written.

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

It is worth being honest about what is and isn't tragic here. The meritocracy was never the fair thing it claimed to be — Michael Young knew that in 1958, and the decades since have proved him right: it laundered inherited advantage into the language of desert, told the comfortable they had earned their comfort, and taught the left-behind to experience their exclusion as a personal verdict. So the dismantling of that machine is not, in itself, a loss to mourn; a story that mostly flattered the winners is not one worth defending on its own terms. But something real is threatened underneath the ideology, and it is the part worth guarding. Not the sorting — the *substance* the sorting was supposed to track: the doctor's decades of pattern-learning, the mathematician's slow communion with a problem, the deep, particular, hard-won human competence that the meritocracy commodified but did not invent. That competence was always more than a credential and more than a wage; it was a form of meaning, a way a life could be spent well. The danger of the AI era is not that it exposes the meritocracy as a fraud — it was always partly that. The danger is that in demolishing the false hierarchy of *worth*, we also cheapen the true dignity of *mastery* — that we let "the machine can do it faster" become "so it was never worth a human doing at all." The mathematicians are right to sound the alarm, but not quite for the reason they think. The thing to save is not their status. It is the idea that understanding something deeply is a good in itself — worth a life, even a life the machine could out-compute in an afternoon. Merit, as a way of ranking people, deserves to fall. Mastery, as a way of being one, does not.
