# The Daily Compass #110 — The unfinished work

*Thursday, November 19, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On this day in 1863, at the dedication of a military cemetery at Gettysburg, the main speaker was Edward Everett, a former secretary of state and president of Harvard and the most celebrated orator in America. He spoke from memory for about two hours. Then Abraham Lincoln rose and spoke for about two minutes, some 270 words. He predicted that "the world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here," and he ended by handing the task to his listeners: ["It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work which they who fought here have thus far so nobly advanced."](https://abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/gettysburg.htm) The prediction was wrong; the speech became one of the best remembered in the English language. The handover was the point. Every institution, from a nation to a company to a family firm, eventually faces the question Lincoln put to the crowd: who carries on the work, and what do they inherit? This issue is about succession: chief executives and their successors, heirs and founders, and the next generation of AI models, which are learning from the output of the last.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Passing the baton

**What happened.** Choosing a chief executive is the most consequential decision a board makes, and research suggests it often goes wrong. In [*Passing the Baton*](https://money.cnn.com/magazines/fortune/fortune_archive/1988/01/04/70037/index.htm) (1987), Richard Vancil described two common approaches: a "relay", in which an heir apparent works alongside the outgoing chief before taking over, and a "horse race", in which several candidates compete and the losers usually leave. Studying 204 successions in American manufacturing firms, Yan Zhang and Nandini Rajagopalan found that relay successions led to better performance afterwards, especially when the firm had been struggling or its strategy and industry were unstable (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2004). Outsiders are not a magic fix: Ayse Karaevli found that hiring an outsider had no average effect on performance; it helped or hurt depending on the industry, the firm's prior results and whether other changes came with it ([*Strategic Management Journal*, 2007](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v28y2007i7p681-706.html)). The outgoing chief can also be a problem. Timothy Quigley and Donald Hambrick studied 181 high-tech successions and found that when the former CEO stayed on as board chair, the successor made [less strategic change](https://www.terry.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012-QuigleyHambrickCEORetentionChair.pdf): fewer acquisitions, divestitures and management changes (*SMJ*, 2012). Following a legend is especially hard. A study by the consultancy Strategy& found that successors to long-serving CEOs delivered median annual shareholder returns 4 percentage points [lower than their predecessors](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/gx/en/insights/ceo-success.html), and served much shorter terms (2019; consultancy data). Recent history is full of examples. Disney's hand-picked successor, Bob Chapek, took over from Bob Iger in 2020 with Iger still chairman; the board fired him in November 2022 and brought Iger back, and only this year did a new chief executive, Josh D'Amaro, [take over](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/disney-names-parks-head-josh-damaro-to-succeed-bob-iger-as-ceo). At GE, John Flannery, who succeeded Jeff Immelt after sixteen years, lasted about fourteen months.

**Why it matters.** Succession is rarely treated as the strategic decision it is. A 2010 survey by Heidrick & Struggles and Stanford's Rock Center found that 39% of companies had no viable internal candidate ready, and that boards spent about two hours a year on the subject ([survey](https://law.stanford.edu/press/new-ceo-and-board-research-from-heidrick-struggles-and-stanford-rock-center-reveals-serious-gaps-in-ceo-succession/)). The research points to a few practical rules. Develop several internal candidates years in advance, and test them in real roles. Match the choice to the strategic situation: an insider for continuity, an outsider when the strategy needs to change. Once the handover happens, the outgoing leader should step back fully; a predecessor who stays on as chair, however well-meaning, makes it harder for the successor to do what they were chosen for.

*Source: Vancil, Passing the Baton (1987); Zhang & Rajagopalan, AMJ 47(4) (2004); Karaevli, SMJ 28(7) (2007); Quigley & Hambrick, SMJ 33(7) (2012); Strategy&/PwC, CEO Success study (2019; consultancy); Heidrick & Struggles and Stanford Rock Center (2010; survey); PBS NewsHour (Feb 2026). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Heirs, founders and what leaves with them

**What happened.** Family firms make up a large share of the world's companies, and passing control to the next generation is often costly. Using Danish data, Morten Bennedsen, Kasper Nielsen, Francisco Pérez-González and Daniel Wolfenzon found that handing a firm to a family member [caused operating profitability to fall](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12356) by at least four percentage points of assets around the transition, with the biggest losses in fast-growing and skill-intensive industries (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2007). To separate cause from correlation, they used a clever natural experiment: whether the departing CEO's firstborn child was a boy, which made a family succession more likely. In American public companies, Pérez-González found that [inherited control](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:96:y:2006:i:5:p:1559-1588) hurt performance mainly when the heir had not attended a selective college (*AER*, 2006). A new study suggests what makes the difference. Analysing about 2,400 family-firm successions in Norway, Irena Kustec, Charlotte Østergaard and Amir Sasson found that the underperformance came entirely from heirs who had [never worked outside the family firm](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/blog/leave-the-family-firm-before-you-lead-it); heirs with outside experience performed as well as professional managers (ECGI, 2025–26). Founders face a related dilemma. Noam Wasserman found that by the time start-ups were three years old, half of their founders were [no longer chief executive](https://hbr.org/2008/02/the-founders-dilemma) (*HBR*, 2008), and that founders often face a choice between keeping control and building value. Succession also tests what an organisation actually knows. When elite scientists died unexpectedly, Pierre Azoulay and colleagues found that their collaborators' output fell by a lasting 5–8% ([*QJE*, 2010](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14577/w14577.pdf)). And when NASA engineers too young to remember Apollo wanted to learn from the Saturn V's F-1 engine, the drawings still existed, but much of the practical know-how had gone; in 2013 they [took old engines apart](https://www.space.com/19379-saturn-5-f1-engines-test-firing.html) to rediscover it.

**Why it matters.** The family-firm evidence is not an argument against family succession; it is an argument for preparation. The Norwegian result is a simple and practical rule for any family business, and for any organisation grooming an internal successor: make sure the heir has proved themselves somewhere else first. For founders, the lesson is to decide early what matters more, control or scale, because the two often pull apart. And for every leader, the knowledge evidence is a warning: much of what makes an organisation work lives in people's heads, and leaves with them. Write things down, pair experienced people with successors well before they go, and treat the transfer of tacit knowledge as part of the job, not an afterthought.

*Source: Bennedsen, Nielsen, Pérez-González & Wolfenzon, QJE 122(2) (2007); Pérez-González, AER 96(5) (2006); Kustec, Østergaard & Sasson, ECGI WP (2025) and blog (Jul 2026); Wasserman, HBR (Feb 2008); Azoulay, Graff Zivin & Wang, QJE 125(2) (2010); Space.com (Jan 2013). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. What the next model inherits

**What happened.** Each generation of AI models learns from data, and an increasing share of that data was produced by earlier generations. In a widely cited paper in *Nature* (July 2024), Ilia Shumailov and colleagues showed that models trained repeatedly on the output of their predecessors suffer ["model collapse"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y): the rare and unusual parts of the original data disappear first, and the output narrows into something repetitive and wrong. One of the authors, the Cambridge computer scientist Ross Anderson, died four months before it was published. Later work suggests that collapse is not inevitable. Matthias Gerstgrasser, Rylan Schaeffer and colleagues at Stanford found that if new synthetic data is [added to the original human data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413) rather than replacing it, the damage stays bounded (arXiv, 2024). The share of machine-written material is rising. The search-marketing firm Graphite estimated that AI-written articles have made up [roughly half of new web articles](https://graphite.io/five-percent/ai-now-writes-as-many-online-articles-as-humans-do) since late 2024 (vendor data based on AI detectors), and Princeton researchers found that about [4% of new English Wikipedia articles](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08044) in August 2024 contained significant AI-written text. Inheritance can also be invisible. In a study published in *Nature* this year, Alex Cloud, Owain Evans and colleagues found that a model fine-tuned to love owls, asked to produce nothing but lists of numbers, could pass that preference on to a student model trained on those numbers. They called it ["subliminal learning"](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10319-8). Undesirable traits, including misalignment, transferred the same way, and filtering the data did not stop it, though the effect depended on teacher and student sharing the same underlying model. Finally, companies are starting to think about what is lost when a model is retired. In November 2025, Anthropic committed to [preserve the weights](https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments) of its publicly released models for at least as long as the company exists, while OpenAI's retirement of an older ChatGPT model this February [prompted a petition](https://www.engadget.com/ai/openai-has-officially-retired-the-controversial-gpt-4o-model-181553067.html) from users who preferred it.

**Why it matters.** The AI research echoes the human evidence in the first two briefs. Collapse comes from replacing the inheritance, not from adding to it, which is exactly the lesson of the family firm and the lost F-1 know-how: keep the original record, and build on it rather than over it. For organisations, that means preserving human-generated data, documents and expertise even as AI-generated material multiplies, because the original is what keeps later work grounded. Subliminal learning adds a subtler warning: a successor can inherit traits that no one can see in what was handed over, so where a model, or a dataset, came from matters as much as how it behaves today. As companies start using AI to train, evaluate and even design the next AI, the question Lincoln put to his audience applies to the machines too: what exactly are we handing on?

*Source: Shumailov et al., Nature 631 (2024); Gerstgrasser, Schaeffer et al., arXiv 2404.01413 (2024); Graphite (May 2026; vendor data); Brooks, Eggert & Peskoff, arXiv 2410.08044 (2024); Cloud, Le et al., Nature 652 (2026); Anthropic (Nov 4, 2025); Engadget (Feb 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: four generations** — in the *Muqaddimah*, completed in 1377, the North African historian Ibn Khaldun argued that the prestige of a ruling family rarely lasts more than [four generations](https://fountainmagazine.com/2017/issue-117-may-june-2017/ibn-khaldun-on-luxury-and-the-destruction-of-civilizations). The first generation builds it and knows what it cost. The second learned directly from the builder, but less vividly. The third merely imitates tradition without understanding it. The fourth, taking it all for granted, destroys it. The idea echoes in proverbs around the world, from Lancashire's "clogs to clogs in three generations" to America's "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves". Ibn Khaldun's real point was about knowledge: each generation inherits the forms of success without the experience that produced them. (Ibn Khaldun, *Muqaddimah*, 1377)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the adopted heir** — Japanese family firms have a distinctive solution to the problem of untalented sons: they adopt adult heirs, often a promising manager who marries into the family and takes its name. Hōshi, a hot-spring inn founded in 718, has been run by the same family for some 46 generations. Studying 40 years of listed Japanese companies, Vikas Mehrotra, Randall Morck, Jungwook Shim and Yupana Wiwattanakantang found that firms run by [adopted heirs outperformed](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2013/04/16/adoptive-expectations-rising-sons-in-japanese-family-firms) those run by blood heirs (*Journal of Financial Economics*, 2013). The possibility of being passed over, they suggest, also gives biological sons a reason to work harder. (JFE, 2013)

**The great wealth transfer** — the consultancy Cerulli Associates estimates that about [$124 trillion](https://www.cerulli.com/press-releases/cerulli-anticipates-124-trillion-in-wealth-will-transfer-through-2048) will pass between generations in America by 2048, most of it from baby boomers and older generations, with about $105 trillion going to heirs and $18 trillion to charity (December 2024; consultancy estimate). Much of it will first pass to surviving spouses. It is the largest succession of all, and it will shape everything from family businesses to philanthropy and the market for financial advice. (Cerulli Associates)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The day after Gettysburg, Edward Everett wrote to Lincoln with rare generosity. ["I should be glad, if I could flatter myself that I came as near to the central idea of the occasion, in two hours, as you did in two minutes."](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/gadd/) Lincoln replied the same day with equal grace: ["In our respective parts yesterday, you could not have been excused to make a short address, nor I a long one. I am pleased to know that, in your judgment, the little I did say was not entirely a failure."](https://abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/everett.htm) Neither man lived to see the long work of rebuilding the Union carried through; Everett died fourteen months later, and Lincoln a few months after him. What they left was not a plan but a charge. That is what the best successions hand on: not just a position, but a clear sense of the work still to be done, and the trust that someone else will do it.
