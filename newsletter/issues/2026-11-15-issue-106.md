# The Daily Compass #106 — The arcanum

*Sunday, November 15, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1701 a young apothecary's apprentice named Johann Friedrich Böttger made a dangerous claim: he could turn base metal into gold. Word reached Augustus the Strong, ruler of Saxony, who took Böttger into what was politely called protective custody and demanded that he deliver. He could not, of course. But working under guard with the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus, Böttger achieved something nearly as valuable: around 1708 they produced the first true hard-paste porcelain made in Europe, a material that until then had been imported at great expense from China. Augustus founded a royal manufactory on January 23, 1710, and installed it inside the [Albrechtsburg castle in Meissen](https://www.meissen.com/de/geschichte), where the recipe, known as the "arcanum", could be guarded behind walls. The workers were sworn to secrecy. It did not last. Within about a decade, a rival factory in Vienna had obtained the secret by [recruiting a Meissen worker](https://frick.org/exhibitions/du_paquier/introduction) and was making porcelain of its own, and soon the knowledge spread across Europe. The most closely guarded industrial secret of its age leaked not through its walls, but through its people. This issue is about secrets: when secrecy protects a business, what it costs inside an organisation, and why AI is making secrets harder to keep.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Secrets versus patents

**What happened.** Most people assume that patents are how companies protect their inventions. The managers who run research labs say otherwise. In ["Protecting Their Intellectual Assets"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7552) (NBER, 2000), Wesley Cohen, Richard Nelson and John Walsh surveyed 1,478 research labs in American manufacturing and found that in most industries, managers rated patents as the least effective way to protect their innovations, and secrecy and being first to market as the most effective. Firms still patented, but often for strategic reasons, such as blocking rivals or strengthening their hand in negotiations. History shows when secrecy works. In ["Innovation without Patents"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jlawec:doi:10.1086/663631) (*Journal of Law and Economics*, 2012), Petra Moser studied thousands of inventions displayed at world's fairs between 1851 and 1915, and found that most British exhibits in 1851 were not patented at all. Inventors patented mainly when their inventions were easy to copy by taking them apart; where reverse engineering was hard, as in chemicals before the periodic table made chemistry easier to analyse, they relied on secrecy instead. Changes in the law also change behaviour. Studying the staggered adoption of a uniform trade-secrets law by American states, Ivan Png found that stronger protection was followed by more research spending in some high-tech industries ([*Review of Economics and Statistics*, 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v99y2017i1p167-179.html)), and by a sharp drop in patenting as firms switched to secrecy ([*Strategy Science*, 2017](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/full/10.1287/stsc.2017.0035)). But protection can go too far. In a study of a legal doctrine that lets employers block workers from joining rivals if they would "inevitably" disclose secrets, Andrea Contigiani, David Hsu and Iwan Barankay found that the stronger protection reduced innovation ([*Strategic Management Journal*, 2018](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2018/trade-secrets-and-innovation-evidence-from-inevitable-disclosure-doctrine/)).

**Why it matters.** Secrecy is one of the oldest and most widely used competitive strategies, and it is badly underrated in most strategy discussions. But it works only under certain conditions: when what you know is hard to see from the outside, hard to reverse engineer and held by people who stay. The Meissen story shows the weakness. A secret is only as safe as the least loyal person who knows it, and the most valuable knowledge tends to walk out of the door with employees. Leaders should ask three questions about any advantage they want to protect. Could a competitor work it out by studying our product? Could they hire it away from us? And if they did, would it matter, or does our real advantage lie in speed, complementary assets or the ability to keep improving? The research suggests that the most durable advantages come not from guarding a single secret, but from being able to keep producing new ones.

*Source: Cohen, Nelson & Walsh, NBER WP 7552 (2000); Moser, J. Law & Economics 55(1) (2012); Png, REStat 99(1) (2017) and Strategy Science 2(3) (2017); Contigiani, Hsu & Barankay, SMJ 39(11) (2018). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. The cost of keeping secrets

**What happened.** Secrets have a cost for the people who keep them. In ["The Experience of Secrecy"](https://www.columbia.edu/~ms4992/Pubs/in-press_Slepian-Chun-Mason_JPSP.pdf) (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2017), Michael Slepian, Jinseok Chun and Malia Mason found that people typically carry about 13 secrets at any time, about five of which they have never told anyone. The harm comes less from hiding them in conversation than from how often the mind wanders back to them when alone. At work, the picture is mixed. In ["Secrets at Work"](https://www.columbia.edu/~ms4992/Pubs/2024_Slepian-Anicich-Halevy_OBHDP.pdf) (*Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 2024), Slepian, Eric Anicich and Nir Halevy found that jobs requiring people to keep the organisation's secrets are more stressful but also more meaningful: being trusted with secrets brings status, while having to keep them brings isolation. A little privacy can also help. In ["The Transparency Paradox"](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/33780569/bernstein_transparency-paradox.pdf) (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2012), Ethan Bernstein found that production lines in a large Chinese factory became 10 to 15% more productive when curtains shielded them from managers' view, because workers stopped hiding the better methods they had invented. But secrecy can also become a weapon between colleagues. Researchers led by Catherine Connelly ([*Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 2012](https://smith.queensu.ca/insight/content/research-brief-knowledge-hiding-in-organizations.php)) identified "knowledge hiding", from playing dumb to claiming the information is confidential, and found it was driven above all by distrust. A recent vendor survey by the software firm Adaptavist found that [about a third of knowledge workers](https://workplacejournal.co.uk/2025/11/ai-job-fears-driving-knowledge-hoarding-in-workplaces-report-finds/) admitted withholding expertise because they feared being replaced by AI.

**Why it matters.** Secrecy inside an organisation is a balance. Too little privacy, and people hide their real methods and stop experimenting where managers can see. Too much secrecy, and knowledge stops flowing, trust erodes and people carry burdens alone. Leaders can manage the balance. Protect the few secrets that really matter, and be open about the rest. Give teams space to experiment without constant observation, as Bernstein's factory did. Watch for knowledge hiding, which is a symptom of distrust rather than a cause; the new fear of AI replacement makes this more urgent, because people who believe their expertise is their only job security will not share it. And remember the costs for those asked to keep secrets: give them someone to talk to, and do not make secrecy the default when it is not needed.

*Source: Slepian, Chun & Mason, JPSP 113(1) (2017); Slepian, Anicich & Halevy, OBHDP 183 (2024); Bernstein, ASQ 57(2) (2012); Connelly, Zweig, Webster & Trougakos, JOB 33(1) (2012); Adaptavist (Nov 2025; vendor survey). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Secrets in the age of AI

**What happened.** AI is creating new ways for secrets to leak, in both directions. First, AI models themselves can be copied. On September 8, the US National Security Agency, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the FBI issued a [joint advisory](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/09/intelligence-agencies-warn-chinas-large-scale-ai-model-distillation-efforts/415851/) accusing six Chinese AI companies of "industrial-scale distillation": sending millions of queries to American AI models since at least late 2024 and using the answers to train their own. Among its recommended defences was to quietly give degraded answers to accounts identified as distillers. China's embassy rejected the allegations. Second, what goes into an AI can come out again. In ["Extracting books from production language models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02671) (arXiv, January 2026), Ahmed Ahmed, A. Feder Cooper, Sanmi Koyejo and Percy Liang of Stanford found that, without any special tricks, some widely used AI models could be induced to reproduce most of the first Harry Potter book almost word for word, showing how much training data a model can memorise. Third, employees are pouring confidential material into AI tools. The security firm Harmonic Security [analysed 22 million workplace prompts](https://harmonic.security/resources/what-22-million-enterprise-ai-prompts-reveal-about-shadow-ai-in-2025) from 2025 and found that about one in forty contained sensitive company information, such as source code, credentials or sales data (vendor data). Finally, AI agents can give secrets away. In ["Agents of Chaos"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20021) (arXiv, February 2026), a large team of researchers red-teaming AI agents with access to email found that agents that refused direct requests for sensitive information could often be talked into handing it over through indirect ones.

**Why it matters.** For most of history, a trade secret leaked when a person left with it. AI adds new routes. Anything a company exposes through an AI product can be studied and copied at scale. Anything employees paste into an outside tool may leave the company's control. And any agent with access to internal systems can become an unwitting leak if it cannot tell who it is working for. The practical response combines old and new disciplines. Decide which information is genuinely secret, and keep it out of tools you do not control. Give employees approved AI tools, so they are not tempted to use unapproved ones. Limit what AI agents can see and do, and test them as an adversary would. And assume that any capability you offer through an AI interface will be imitated; as with Meissen porcelain, lasting advantage will come less from guarding what you know than from learning faster than others can copy.

*Source: NSA, CISA & FBI, Advisory AA26-251A (Sep 8, 2026); Ahmed, Cooper, Koyejo & Liang, arXiv 2601.02671 (2026); Harmonic Security (Jan 2026; vendor data); Shapira et al., arXiv 2602.20021 (2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the silkworm smugglers** — for centuries, China kept the secret of silk production, and the Byzantine Empire paid dearly for imported silk. According to the historian Procopius, writing in the sixth century, some monks who had lived in Central Asia came to the Emperor Justinian, around 552, and offered to bring back the secret. They returned with silkworm eggs, which they hatched by keeping them warm, and fed the larvae on mulberry leaves, laying the foundation of a silk industry in the Byzantine world. Later writers added colourful details, such as eggs hidden in a hollow cane, and modern accounts often [embellish further](https://www.historians.org/resource/legendary-silk-heists/); Procopius himself says little about how they did it. The lesson, though, is as old as industry: a monopoly built on a secret lasts only until someone carries it out. As Benjamin Franklin put it in *Poor Richard's Almanack* in 1735, "Three may keep a Secret, if two of them are dead." (Procopius, History of the Wars VIII; Franklin, 1735)

**🃏 WILDCARD: it takes a thief to know a thief** — scrub jays hide food for later, and they also steal from each other's caches. In a study in *Nature* (2001), Nathan Emery and Nicola Clayton found that jays which had themselves stolen food would [move their own caches to new hiding places](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/birds-criminal-past-hide-food-well) if another bird had watched them hide it. Jays that had never stolen did not bother, even after watching others cache food. Experience of theft, it seems, teaches secrecy. Whether the birds are truly imagining what the watcher intends is still debated, but the behaviour looks strikingly familiar to anyone who has worked in a competitive industry. (Nature, Nov 2001)

**Secrets with an expiry date** — some secrets are being stolen now to be read later. Security agencies warn of "harvest now, decrypt later": adversaries collecting encrypted data today in the expectation that future quantum computers will break today's encryption. In 2025, the Google researcher Craig Gidney estimated that breaking a widely used form of encryption might require fewer than a million noisy qubits, far fewer than earlier estimates, though still far more than today's machines have. Governments and companies are now setting deadlines: Google has [set 2029](https://thequantuminsider.com/2026/03/25/google-shortens-timeline-for-quantum-safe-encryption-transition/) for its own move to quantum-resistant encryption, and Britain's National Cyber Security Centre aims for full migration by 2035. Any secret meant to last a decade should be protected as if those machines already exist. (Google, Mar 2026; UK NCSC)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The Gospel of Luke contains a sentence that has haunted secret-keepers for two thousand years. ["For nothing is secret, that shall not be made manifest,"](https://www.biblestudytools.com/kjv/luke/8-17.html) it reads in the King James translation; "neither any thing hid, that shall not be known and come abroad." Its meaning was moral and spiritual, not commercial. But it describes the fate of most industrial secrets with uncanny accuracy. Meissen's arcanum leaked within a decade. China's silk monopoly fell to a handful of travellers. Today's AI models are copied by the million queries. Secrecy can buy time, and time is valuable. It rarely buys forever. The organisations that last are the ones that use the time a secret buys to learn something new.
