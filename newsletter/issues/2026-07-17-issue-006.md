## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Who gets to decide what people become?

**What happened.** Noema published Jacob Dreyer's ["How AI Is Rewriting Human Nature"](https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-is-rewriting-human-nature/) — a Shanghai-based essayist holding together two facts nobody else connects: the tech hubs closest to the AI frontier (San Francisco, Seoul, Shanghai, Singapore) have the world's *lowest* birthrates, even as AI-driven biotech makes radical longevity plausible. He contrasts American transhumanism — escape the body, upload the soul — with what he calls China's "post-socialist industrial theology": redesign the collective body. Field notes run from peptide raves in San Francisco to brain-computer-interface labs at Huashan Hospital.

**The excerpt:**

> "Humans are going to change — of that there is no doubt. What remains unsettled is who gets to decide what people become."

And the line that lingers: "the giddy, euphoric discourse of AI in a fairly run-down place had the vibe of a civilization trying to outrun decay."

**Why it matters.** Most AI-and-humanity essays are parochial — one country's anxieties projected onto the species. Dreyer's is genuinely comparative, and the comparison is the insight: the US and China aren't just racing to build the same technology, they're running *different experiments on what a human is for*. The demographic frame gives it teeth — civilizations quietly declining to reproduce themselves while investing everything in machine descendants is either a coincidence or the most important fact of the century. Strategy readers will notice this is also a market map: whoever's answer to "what people become" wins adoption wins everything downstream.

*Source: Jacob Dreyer, Noema (Jul 16). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. Keynes's grandchildren, and the case for refusing

**What happened.** The New Atlantis's summer issue arrived as a themed cluster on human limits in the AI age: the issue-lead ["Offloading Ourselves: Staying Human in the Age of AI"](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/offloading-ourselves-staying-human-in-the-age-of-ai) asks what should never be delegated to machines; Brad East's "The Case for the Bartleby Option" argues for principled refusal — *I would prefer not to* — as a live stance rather than Luddism; and a third essay contends that whether or not you keep your job, the meaning of work will never be the same.

**The excerpt** — the ur-text they're all arguing with, Keynes's 1930 ["Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm):

> "Thus for the first time since his creation man will be faced with his real, his permanent problem — how to use his freedom from pressing economic cares, how to occupy the leisure, which science and compound interest will have won for him, to live wisely and agreeably and well."

**Why it matters.** Keynes coined "technological unemployment" in the teeth of the Depression and called it a *temporary maladjustment* on the way to abundance — then immediately worried humanity would suffer a collective "nervous breakdown" upon arrival, since we were "expressly evolved by nature" for solving the economic problem, not for enjoying its solution. Ninety-six years later, every essay in the New Atlantis cluster, every post-labor economics thread, and Dreyer's birthrate paradox above are footnotes to that worry. The strategic version of the question is personal and immediate: the Bartleby Option only means something if you decide *in advance* what you won't offload — judgment, formation, the work that makes you you — before the default decides for you.

*Sources: Keynes (1930); The New Atlantis No. 85 (Summer 2026 — cluster surveyed via issue contents; essays sit behind a subscriber gate). ~30 min with the Keynes.*

### ◆ 3. What kind of thinker is this thing? Licklider's interim, Davies's librarian

**What happened.** Dan Davies published the sharpest short epistemology-of-AI argument of the month, ["it's in the literature"](https://backofmind.substack.com/p/its-in-the-literature): the model for LLM mathematical "discoveries" is Thomas Royen — the retired statistician whose proof of the Gaussian Correlation Inequality was, in a sense, always implicit in a literature split across communities that never read each other. An engine that has read *everything* can industrialize that gap-filling.

**The excerpt:**

> "If you've got an extrapolation engine, and you've interacted with lots of mathematical literature, then you are very well placed to industrialise the process of filling in those gaps."

**Why it matters.** Davies stakes out the precise middle ground between "it's just autocomplete" and "AI is doing science now": genuinely valuable, genuinely impressive — and probably not the mechanism of era-defining breakthroughs, which come from *new frames*, not filled gaps. Set him beside J.C.R. Licklider's 1960 ["Man-Computer Symbiosis"](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/medg/people/psz/Licklider.html), which conceded machines would eventually out-think us and asked only how long the "interim" of true partnership would last: "The 15 may be 10 or 500, but those years should be intellectually the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind." The 2026 agents debate is exactly a debate about whether Licklider's interim is ending. And for what the partnership *feels* like meanwhile, the week's best line came through Zvi's roundup, from Qiaochu Yuan: talking to a frontier model is "like having Google Earth but for the entire human corpus… imagine if Borges had gotten to do this."

*Sources: Davies (Jul 1); Licklider (1960); Zvi's AI #177 (Jul 16). ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The Gemini non-launch** — today was the rumored launch day; instead, the eve brought reports of another delay over coding performance, and Alphabet fell on the news. Nothing official exists: no model card, no API listing, no blog post — every circulating spec traces to unnamed sources. A clean case study in planning around leaks. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/16/alphabet-stock-gemini-3-5-pro-ai.html), Jul 16)

**The governance sleeper in Zvi's roundup** — xAI silently rewrote its Frontier AI Framework on June 30, deleting quantitative deployment criteria *and* whistleblower-protection language (full diff at The Midas Project); meanwhile Ben Bernanke joined Anthropic's Long Term Benefit Trust. Voluntary safety regimes are edited in silence; personnel is policy. (Zvi Mowshowitz, [AI #177](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-177-part-1-tip-of-the-iceberg), Jul 16)

**Sensemaking is the skill that survives** — Cedric Chin's synthesis of his expertise series: "every winning strategy boils down to a few small insights that frame the problem advantageously," and frame-construction — not analysis — "is the bit that will remain important as AI invades our workplaces." Comes with a runnable training protocol. (Cedric Chin, [Commoncog](https://commoncog.com/sensemaking-heart-of-expertise/), Jun 12)

**Strategy needs a center, not a plan** — Rita McGrath's new HBR cover-adjacent piece: in a dematerialized economy, traditional frameworks fail; what works is a single organizing principle — a mission, a customer, an ecosystem — that governs every resource decision. The "end of competitive advantage" thinker, writing for the AI dissolution. (Rita McGrath, [HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/the-power-of-strategic-centering), Jul-Aug issue)

**Skunk Works, 1943 → Anthropic, 2026** — Lenny's interview with Claude Code's head of product describes "launch rooms," research previews, and "just do things" as the operating system of AI-native speed; Kelly Johnson's [14 Rules](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/en-us/who-we-are/business-areas/aeronautics/skunkworks/skunk-works-origin-story.html) — teams restricted "in an almost vicious manner," near-total delegation, XP-80 in 143 days — is the same doctrine, 83 years older. ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-anthropics-product-team-moves), Apr 2026)

**Your model has a worldview** — The Economist ran 25 frontier models through the World Values Survey; they cluster secular/self-expression but diverge more by post-training than by lab. Tunguz's punchline: procurement scores price, latency, benchmarks — never values — "the moment a model is used for business decisions in a specific market, its worldview is a live input." (Tomasz Tunguz, [tomtunguz.com](https://www.tomtunguz.com/godless-hippies-ai-models-values/), Jul 6)

**A century of the information state** — Nicholas Carr reviews *The Information State* for The New Atlantis: from Wilson's propaganda committee through PRISM to this year's platform-pressure fights, "every failure of automated data processing becomes an excuse to collect even more data" — and the system's true master is "the huckster, not the technocrat." (Nicholas Carr, [The New Atlantis](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/operation-gaslight), Summer 2026 issue)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Managing Oneself** — Drucker, 1999: knowledge workers must become their own CEOs — feedback analysis to find your actual strengths, "place ourselves where we can make the greatest contribution… during a 50-year working life." Written for when companies stopped charting careers; re-read for when AI reprices them. The feedback-analysis method is precisely how you find which strengths are complements to AI rather than substitutes. (Peter Drucker, [HBR](https://hbr.org/2005/01/managing-oneself))

**🃏 WILDCARD: Sontag, 1975, resurfaced this week** — "I would still argue that a work of art, qua work of art, cannot advocate anything. But since no work of art is in fact only a work of art, it's often more complicated than that." Fifty years old, and it lands squarely on 2026's argument about whether art — including machine-made media — must carry the right politics to be good. (Susan Sontag, Salmagundi, [via 3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/07/an-interview-with-susan-sontag-1975.html))
