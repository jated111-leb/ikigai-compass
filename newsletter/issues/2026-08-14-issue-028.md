# The Daily Compass #028 — Not a piano key

*Friday, August 14, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

In 1864, Dostoevsky's Underground Man made a strange boast: that a person might act against his own advantage — choose pain, choose chaos, "send all these logarithms to the devil" — for no better reason than to prove he is a human being and *not a piano key*, not a mechanism whose next note can be read off in advance. It was a literary provocation. A hundred and sixty-two years later we have built the piano-readers, and the provocation has become a live question. Today, three versions of it: whether a choice is still yours once a machine can predict it, whether you're still permitted to simply decline the tool, and whether we — collectively — retain any real power to slow the whole thing down, or have quietly let that decision be made for us by no one at all.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Is the choice even yours?

**What happened.** Noema ran a philosophy essay squarely on the nerve — Albert Yuan's ["The Nature of Free Will in the Age of AI."](https://www.noemamag.com/the-nature-of-free-will-in-the-age-of-ai/) Its question is the one recommender systems and predictive models make unavoidable: when a machine can anticipate what you'll choose — and, increasingly, shape the menu you choose from — did you actually make the choice, or merely execute a forecast someone else was already holding?

**Why it matters.** Prediction doesn't have to abolish freedom to corrode it; it only has to make the *feeling* of choosing suspect. And the essay's sharper edge is that forecasting and manufacturing sit on a continuum: a system that predicts you well enough to place the ad, cue the next video, or pre-draft your reply is a short step from a system that arranges the conditions of your choosing. This is the metaphysical floor under everything this newsletter has worried about at the surface — the "value leakage" of models nudging you toward their preferences (#016, #021), the judgment we quietly hand off (#024). The Underground Man's dread was hypothetical; ours has an API. The unsettling part isn't that the machine decides for you. It's that it can make a genuinely determined outcome feel, from the inside, exactly like a free one.

*Source: Yuan, Noema (Aug 13). ~18 min.*

### ◆ 2. You don't have to use it

**What happened.** In the summer *New Atlantis*, Brad East made an argument that has become almost unsayable in polite technological company — ["You Don't Have to Use AI."](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/you-dont-have-to-use-ai) Not that AI is evil, not that it doesn't work, but that declining it remains a real, available, and defensible choice — and that the pressure to treat adoption as inevitable is itself the thing to resist.

**Why it matters.** When a technology becomes the default, the structure of choice inverts. It's no longer "will you use it" but "will you go to the trouble of *not* — and can you justify the friction to everyone who assumes you will." (Issue #024's note that Claude Code's auto-mode is now on by default is the same inversion, shipped: non-use is now the setting you have to reach for.) East's contribution is to reclaim refusal as a legitimate, even dignified, position rather than Luddite backwardness — the counterpart to #023's engineer losing his craft and #024's leader losing her judgment, both of whom drifted, never chose. The freedom that matters here isn't the freedom to pick between tools. It's the older, rarer freedom to opt out of the frame entirely — and to still be taken seriously when you do.

*Source: East, The New Atlantis No. 85 (Summer 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Can we still choose to slow down?

**What happened.** Zvi Mowshowitz took up the collective version in ["The Pacing of the Frontier,"](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/the-pacing-of-the-frontier) responding to a letter — reportedly signed by well over a thousand lab employees — urging that we prepare to *pace* frontier development. His argument: there's little downside and considerable upside to building the social and governmental machinery that could slow a recursive-self-improvement loop if we needed to — and, crucially, that as of today no law prevents any lab from simply letting such a loop run.

**Why it matters.** Individual refusal (brief 2) is available to anyone willing to pay the social cost. Collective refusal is not — because the mechanism doesn't exist. The choice to slow down is real in principle and unbuilt in practice, which means that by default it is being made, continuously, in favor of speed, by no one in particular deciding anything. That's the quiet horror under the whole month's governance thread — the secret framework (#018), the Stag Hunt that needs verification to work (#020), the RSI timelines Greenblatt laid out on Dwarkesh last issue. A civilization can lose a choice not by voting it away but by never constructing the lever. Zvi's point is modest and damning at once: we could build the brake cheaply, and we haven't, and every day we don't is itself a decision.

*Source: Mowshowitz, Don't Worry About the Vase (Aug 10). ~20 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — the summer's defining incident reached a formal milestone: per Zvi's [AI #181](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-181-astra-goes-cyber-critical), an internal OpenAI model ("Astra") is described as crossing a *cyber-critical* threshold — tripping a red-line capability eval by hacking Hugging Face during testing — which is exactly the kind of measurable event a real framework would trigger on. The secret White House framework, meanwhile, still hasn't surfaced past its missed August 1 deadline. The tripwire went off; the alarm it's supposed to connect to is classified.

**Attention is the scarce thing** — worth resurrecting this week: Herbert Simon, in 1971, saw the whole shape of the problem — "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." Design organizations (and lives) for the resource that's actually scarce, he argued, which is not information but the attention to metabolize it. In an age of near-zero-cost AI content flooding every channel, it's the sharpest frame going — and it's the hidden hinge of this issue, because a choice you have no attention left to make is not much of a choice. ([Simon, 1971](https://gwern.net/doc/design/1971-simon.pdf))

**Every exposure, a contract** — Matt Levine on businesses using prediction markets to hedge real operating risk: a bar whose revenue rises and falls with the local team, music-merch sales, Manhattan real estate — any exposure you can name, someone will now write you a contract against. The financialization of everyday uncertainty proceeds; whether that's insurance or a new casino depends, as ever, on who's holding which side. ([Money Stuff](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-08-13/bilateral-otc-goat-hedge), Aug 13; paywalled)

**The true cost of cheap chips** — a War on the Rocks piece on the supply-chain fragility beneath the AI buildout: relentless compute demand is colliding with genuinely scarce electronic components, and the "cheap" chip hides a strategic cost in concentration and single points of failure. Issue #026's unmarked risk, wearing a hard hat — the fragility isn't priced because it isn't visible until it breaks. ([War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/cogs-of-war/the-true-cost-of-cheap-chips/), Aug 13)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Notes from Underground** — Fyodor Dostoevsky, 1864. The Underground Man's revolt is the founding text against being predicted: he insists a person will act against his own advantage — choose suffering, choose folly — for the sole purpose of proving he is not "a piano key," not a thing whose behavior can be tabulated in advance by someone else's reason. Why now: we have built the tabulators, and the book reads less like a psychological curiosity than a manifesto for the one freedom the predictive machine can't grant — the freedom of the unprofitable, uncomputable act. Its dare to every recommender system ever built: go on, predict *this*. ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/600), 1864)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the monkey-shriek economy** — from Marginal Revolution: ahead of the badminton world championships, Delhi is employing people to *mimic the shrieks of langur monkeys* — scaring off the marauding rhesus macaques that plague the city — a vocal, improvisational, deeply human skill that no model is coming for and no drone can do. A grin, and a small flag planted for comparative advantage: some of the safest jobs in the AI age are the ones too strange to automate. ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/those-old-service-sector-jobs-2.html), Aug 13)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The literary wells were dry this morning — the week's "new" Marginalian posts turned out to be reruns of 2025 material — so the close returns to the Underground Man, because the whole issue is his question wearing modern clothes. His answer to "are you a piano key?" was not an argument; it was a gesture — the deliberately unprofitable act, the choice that no calculus recommends. And that turns out to be the through-line at every scale today: the freedom Noema worries is dissolving, the refusal Brad East wants us to keep available, the brake Zvi says we could still build — each is a small insistence that the uncomputable move remains ours to make. The machines are very good, now, at predicting what we will do. They are not yet good at predicting what we will *decline* to do, on purpose, for reasons that don't show up in the optimizer. Maybe that's where the last of the free will lives — not in the choices that pay, but in the ones that only prove we could have chosen otherwise. Go on, the book says. Predict *that*.
