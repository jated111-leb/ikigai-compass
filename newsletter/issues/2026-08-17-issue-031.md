# The Daily Compass #031 — What is not seen

*Monday, August 17, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

In 1850, Frédéric Bastiat drew the line that still separates good thinking from bad: the seen and the unseen. A boy breaks a shop window; the townsfolk console themselves that at least the glazier gets work — and forget the suit the shopkeeper now can't buy, the unseen alternative that simply never happens. The bad economist, Bastiat said, stops at the visible effect; the good one accounts for the invisible one too. This issue is about the unseen — the decisive things that never show up because they didn't occur. A useful household product that doesn't exist in America because of a choice made a century ago about how to frame a house; a disagreement inside a team that everyone politely performs their way past; and the actual texture of what AI is doing, buried under the hype that's so much easier to see. The good strategist, the good manager, the good reader all share one habit: they count the dog that didn't bark.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The unseen market

**What happened.** Tyler Cowen posed a small mystery — ["Things you cannot buy in America?"](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/things-you-cannot-buy-in-america.html) — built around the exterior roller shutter, the *Rollladen* that is standard across Germany and much of Europe: a device that delivers total blackout and real insulation at the touch of a crank. In the US it is effectively unavailable — not because Americans wouldn't want a dark, well-insulated bedroom, but because the shutters have to be integrated during construction and are incompatible with the wood-frame-and-drywall way American houses are built. The market never formed. Not banned, not rejected — just quietly foreclosed.

**Why it matters.** This is Bastiat's unseen at the scale of an entire product category, and it's a sharper idea than it looks. The option that never forms is the hardest thing in the world to value, because you never feel its absence — you just live in a slightly worse version of things and call it normal. It's also a warning label for every "the market has spoken" claim, including last issue's about which AI models customers buy: sometimes the market never got to speak, because the rails were laid a century ago and the alternative was dead on arrival. Path dependence doesn't only pick winners among the things that exist; it silently deletes things before they can compete. And the deletions don't show up anywhere — not in a sales chart, not in a preference survey, not in a single customer's complaint — which is exactly why they run the world unexamined.

*Source: Cowen, Marginal Revolution (Aug 16). ~5 min.*

### ◆ 2. The unseen disagreement

**What happened.** In HBR, Julia Dhar and BCG colleagues named a failure mode most executives will recognize the moment they hear it — ["The False Alignment Trap."](https://hbr.org/2026/07/the-false-alignment-trap) Their finding: change efforts usually fail not on execution but earlier and more quietly, because senior leaders *believe* they agree on the why, the what, and the how of a change when they actually don't. The false agreement is bred by vague discussion, conflict-avoidance, and the pressure to move fast — and it produces paralysis, wasted motion, or a watered-down compromise nobody chose. The fix is counterintuitive: deliberately surface disagreement *before* execution, not after. (The piece drew a public counter-shot from Steve Denning in Forbes — a useful reminder that even the diagnosis is contested.)

**Why it matters.** The objection that never gets voiced is the unseen that sinks the plan. Everyone nods, the meeting ends on a warm consensus, and the real divergence surfaces only months later in the wreckage — by which point it looks like an execution problem, though it was a comprehension problem all along. It's the organizational cousin of two threads this newsletter keeps pulling: issue #029's "leadership drift," where private conviction dissolves into collective comfort, and issue #027's revealed preference, where what a team *actually* believes shows up in what it does, not what it said in the room. Open conflict is manageable precisely because it's visible. False alignment is dangerous because it wears the face of its opposite — the calm surface is the symptom, not the health.

*Source: Dhar et al., HBR (Jul/Aug 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. The unseen reality

**What happened.** Cedric Chin, at Commoncog, offered the antidote to a year of AI vertigo — ["How to Make Sense of AI."](https://commoncog.com/how-to-make-sense-of-ai/) His frame is *sensemaking*: the deliberate effort to understand what is actually happening, built by reasoning from concrete practitioner field reports — an engineer shipping working software with no human-written code, a specific workflow that broke, a specific one that didn't — rather than reacting to the ambient roar of takes. Not "is AI over- or under-hyped," but: what, precisely, is it doing, for whom, where, and how do we know?

**Why it matters.** The hype is the *seen*: loud, cheap, everywhere, optimized to be shared. The real texture of what AI is and isn't doing inside actual work is the *unseen* — quieter, slower, and available only to someone willing to go look. Chin's method is Bastiat's discipline pointed at the present tense, and it is, not incidentally, the whole premise of this newsletter: selection is the commentary, and the job is to see the second-order substance under the first-order spectacle. The strategic error of the moment isn't being too bullish or too bearish on AI; it's letting the volume of the visible stand in for knowledge of the real. What you can see about this technology is mostly what someone paid to make visible. What decides your next five years is almost all in the part nobody's amplifying.

*Source: Chin, Commoncog (2026). ~18 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — Azeem Azhar's Sunday Exponential View finally returned (#597) with the economics of collapsing inference costs: a new model reportedly undercutting top rivals by more than 60%, even as Ramp data shows the top 1% of businesses spending on the order of $7,400 per employee on AI. The *seen* is that intelligence is getting nearly free; the *unseen* is where the value migrates when it does — and who ends up paying the concentrated bill. (Meanwhile: no fresh movement on the secret framework, and Zvi's next weekly hadn't landed at press.) ([Exponential View](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/ev-597), Aug 16)

**Deviance as diagnosis** — a genuinely useful reframe from HBR: drawing on 250-plus studies, Oxford's Michael Gill argues not all rule-breaking is self-serving — people also break rules under environmental pressure, or to *help* the organization, and some violations expose a policy that was wrong to begin with. The move he recommends is to treat a broken rule as information about your incentives, not just misbehavior to punish — curiosity before judgment. The unseen signal hiding inside the visible infraction. ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/07/how-the-best-leaders-respond-to-rule-breaking), Jul/Aug 2026)

**The model that overthinks** — Simon Willison's hands-on note on the new open-weight Qwen 3.8 27B: strong, but it "defaults to wildly overthinking things," burning long chains of thought on tasks that need none. A tidy little parable of the seen and unseen — the visible output is a correct answer; the unseen cost is the compute spent thinking in circles to reach it. More reasoning is not the same as more sense. ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/), Aug 16)

**⏳ TIMELESS: What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen** — Frédéric Bastiat, 1850. The founding statement of opportunity cost and second-order effects, built on the parable of the broken window: the glazier's visible gain blinds us to the tailor's invisible loss, and "the bad economist confines himself to the visible effect; the good economist takes into account both the effect that can be seen and those effects that must be foreseen." Why now: every argument for an AI-capex build-out, a tariff, or an industrial-policy subsidy is, underneath, a fight about the unseen — the foregone use of the capital, the alternative the visible project quietly displaces. 176 years on, we still mistake the glazier's invoice for growth. ([bastiat.org](http://bastiat.org/en/twisatwins.html), 1850)

**🃏 WILDCARD: say the fare out loud** — Cowen again, with a perfect little specimen of hidden second-order effects: a transit agency now requires bus drivers to *verbally state* the $2.25 fare — and one under-discussed reason ordinary workers resist quoting prices aloud is that doing so invites harassment, even assault. A social-friction theory of sticky prices that no menu-cost model contains: sometimes prices are rigid not because changing the sign is costly, but because *saying the number* is. ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/another-rationale-for-sticky-prices.html), Aug 16)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The literary wells were thin this morning — reruns and unconfirmed dates where the fresh essays usually are — so the close stays with Bastiat, because his broken window is 176 years old and we still can't see past the glass. Here is the quiet thing his parable gets at, and this whole issue circles: the unseen is rarely hidden. It's not a conspiracy or a secret. It's the roller shutter that was never on the shelf, the objection no one said out loud, the real work the money isn't doing, the panda-shaped fact you walk past every morning without once asking why it's there. All of it sits in plain sight, unremarked, because it has always been that way or never was — and the eye slides right over what it has learned to expect. The discipline Bastiat asked for isn't intelligence; it's a smaller and harder thing: the willingness to stop in front of the familiar and ask what *isn't* there. Most of what shapes a life or a strategy is invisible not because it's concealed, but because we've stopped looking. The good reader's one trick is to keep looking — especially at the window that isn't broken, and the dog that didn't bark.
