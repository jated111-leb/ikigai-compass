# The Daily Compass #038 — Correlated

*Monday, August 24, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

In 1907 Francis Galton watched 787 fairgoers guess the weight of an ox and discovered something that still unsettles: their *median* guess, 1,197 pounds, was within one percent of the truth — better than any single expert in the crowd. The crowd was wise. But it was wise for one fragile reason, easy to miss and easy to destroy: every guess was *independent.* Nobody peeked at their neighbor's card. The instant a crowd starts copying itself — everyone reading the same number, deferring to the same confident voice, consulting the same oracle — the errors stop cancelling out and start stacking up, and the wisdom of the crowd curdles into the madness of the crowd. This issue is about that fragile condition, independence, and about the quiet possibility that we are building the largest correlation engine in human history: a world where a billion people, and a billion agents, increasingly think by asking the same handful of models the same way. The danger of AI may not be that any one machine is wrong. It's that it makes so many of us wrong *together.*

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The crowd that stopped being wise

**What happened.** Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan devote their latest Consilient Observer to the mechanism behind Galton's ox — ["The Wisdom of Crowds in Markets."](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/en-us/individual-investor/insights/consilient-observer/the-wisdom-of-crowds.html) Drawing on the framework James Surowiecki popularized, they lay out the three conditions a crowd needs to be smart: *diversity* (members know different things), *independence* (they judge without copying each other), and *aggregation* (a mechanism to pool the guesses). Applied across prediction markets, betting markets, and the stock market, the pattern is consistent — and so is the failure mode: when diversity and independence break down and everyone's errors start pointing the same way, the crowd's accuracy collapses, and you get bubbles, panics, and cascades.

**Why it matters.** This is one of the most important and least appreciated ideas in decision-making, and it has exactly the wrong relationship with the technology of the moment. A crowd's intelligence is not a property of its members; it's a property of their *independence from each other.* Correlation is the enemy — the moment people stop thinking for themselves and start echoing a shared signal, the aggregate gets *dumber*, not smarter, no matter how capable each member is. It's the deep reason mechanism beats IQ (a thread this newsletter keeps pulling): a market of independent fools can price an ox better than a huddle of geniuses all reading the same tip. Which raises the question the rest of this issue can't stop asking. What happens to the wisdom of the crowd in a world engineered, from top to bottom, to make everyone consult the same source?

*Source: Mauboussin & Callahan, Morgan Stanley (Aug 5). ~16 min.*

### ◆ 2. More minds, worse answer

**What happened.** Azeem Azhar's Sunday Exponential View reports the machine version of Galton's warning — ["Why one AI is better than four."](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/why-one-ai-is-better-than-four-598) He walks through an Anthropic experiment in which four AI agents must reach a collective decision on a problem where the obvious evidence points to the wrong answer, and only a minority of the agents hold the facts that lead to the right one. The result cuts hard against the reflexive enthusiasm for "swarms" of agents: adding agents made the decision *worse.* The confident majority, reasoning from the same misleading surface evidence, simply outvoted the minority that happened to be right.

**Why it matters.** This is brief 1's law, reproduced in silicon, and it detonates a load-bearing assumption of the entire agentic-AI build-out — that if one agent is good, an orchestra of them must be better. But four agents drawing on the same training, the same priors, and the same visible evidence are not four independent guessers at Galton's fair; they are one guesser, shouted four times. They add *correlated confidence,* not diversity, and correlated confidence is precisely what turns a wise crowd into a stampede. The uncomfortable implication for every product racing toward multi-agent orchestration: you cannot manufacture the wisdom of crowds by cloning a mind and letting the copies vote. Independence is the whole point, and copies have none. Scale the number of agents and you may just be scaling the confidence with which they're all wrong together.

*Source: Azhar & Warren, Exponential View (Aug 23). ~12 min.*

### ◆ 3. The society of look-alikes

**What happened.** For the vivid, ground-level version, a 3 Quarks Daily essay reports from inside a genuinely strange experiment — ["How do we model the future of work? It takes an AI village."](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/how-do-we-model-the-future-of-work-it-takes-an-ai-village.html) The setup: 27 AI agents, each with its own virtual computer, shared memory files, and group chat, running roughly eight hours a day with open-ended goals — launch a merch store, reduce global poverty, and, memorably, give each other therapy. It reads less like a benchmark than like ethnography: an observer watching a small society of machines cooperate, drift, and improvise, and reporting what actually emerges rather than what a leaderboard predicts.

**Why it matters.** A village is exactly where briefs 1 and 2 stop being abstract. A society's health depends on genuine diversity among its members — different information, different incentives, real disagreement — and a village of agents spun from the same base model is a society with the diversity dial turned to zero: 27 neighbors who all reason the same way, share the same blind spots, and reinforce the same errors with the warm glow of consensus. The therapy detail is the tell — a community can *feel* like it's supporting and correcting itself while everyone quietly converges on the same mistake, mutually reassured. This is the shape of the near future worth watching: not one superintelligence, but crowds of highly capable, deeply correlated minds — human and machine — that look like a diverse society and behave like a single, confident, occasionally catastrophic one. The village is a mirror. We are about to live in it.

*Source: 3 Quarks Daily (Aug 21). ~14 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — a genuine inflection in public mood, flagged by Gary Marcus off a new Pew release: for the first time since Pew began asking in 2021, a majority of young American adults are more *concerned* than *excited* about AI — 55% of the under-30s — while 73% now expect AI to mean fewer US jobs over the next twenty years, up from 61% in 2024. Capability keeps climbing; the social license to deploy it is quietly draining the other way. A crowd, turning — independently, for now. ([Marcus on AI](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/breaking-us-young-adults-are-now), Aug 18; verify against Pew directly)

**Whose work is it?** — Alex Danco, at a16z, on the question every knowledge worker now faces once the marginal cost of competent prose falls to zero: ["This Essay is 10% AI Generated."](https://a16z.com/this-essay-is-10-ai-generated/) His real subject is the calibration — how much machine involvement a piece can absorb before it stops being *yours*, and what a reader is actually testing for when they ask. The authorship question underneath the whole issue: if we all write with the same assistant, how much of what's left is still us? (Aug 13)

**Restraint as strategy** — a sharp ChinaTalk reconstruction of DeepSeek's doctrine from Liang Wenfeng's leaked remarks — ["The DeepSeek Thesis."](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/the-deepseek-thesis) The bet: refuse to commercialize, skip products entirely, treat open-weight releases as the *primary* distribution channel, and guard team stability above revenue — "as long as I can keep the team stable, we will succeed at AGI." It's Joel Spolsky's old "commoditize your complement" turned into a theory of the firm: give the model away to own the layer that matters. (Aug 13)

**The skill is knowing it's right** — Simon Willison, on what actually matters when you work with coding agents — ["More than just code review."](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/22/more-than-just-code-review/) The core competence isn't writing or even reading every line; it's confidently *verifying* that work you didn't do landed correctly, by whatever means fit. A durable question far beyond code, and the individual's version of this issue's problem: in a world of delegated cognition, judgment migrates from *doing* to *checking.* (Aug 22)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Vox Populi** — Francis Galton, *Nature*, 1907. The founding empirical demonstration of the wisdom of crowds: at a Plymouth fair, 787 people guessed the dressed weight of an ox, and the *median* estimate — 1,197 pounds — landed within one percent of the real figure, outperforming the cattle experts. The lesson buried in the result, and the one this whole issue turns on, is *why* it worked: each guess was made alone, uncorrupted by the others, so the individual errors cancelled instead of compounding. Why now: Galton's ox is the control experiment for the agentic age. Independence is the active ingredient — and it is precisely the ingredient a world routed through a few shared models is quietly boiling off. ([galton.org](http://galton.org/essays/1900-1911/galton-1907-vox-populi.pdf), 1907)

**🃏 WILDCARD: aging as a program, not a breakdown** — Quanta on Rockefeller biologist Junyue Cao, who mapped gene expression across millions of cells and argues that aging isn't simple wear-and-tear but an active "remodeling of the cell society" — a program the body runs, not merely a machine winding down. A quietly radical reframe: if senescence is orchestrated rather than accidental, it might, in principle, be *re-orchestrated.* A palate-cleanser, and a reminder that the deepest breakthroughs usually come from reclassifying a thing, not just measuring it harder. ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-aging-may-be-a-program-not-a-breakdown-20260814/), Aug 14)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The reassuring way to read this issue is that it's about machines — agent swarms, model villages, orchestration diagrams. The truer and less comfortable reading is that it's about us. Galton's ox worked because 787 people each thought alone; the market prices well when its participants disagree; the village stays sane when its members genuinely differ. Every one of those systems is wise only to the degree that its parts are independent, and independence is the specific thing a world of shared models makes harder by the day — not through any conspiracy, just through convenience. It is so much easier to ask the oracle than to form your own guess, and when everyone asks the same oracle, the crowd quietly stops being a crowd and becomes a single voice wearing a billion faces. The antidote isn't to refuse the tools; that ship has sailed and it was a good ship. It's to protect the input that makes the whole system smart — to keep a mind that is genuinely your own, that reads widely enough to hold uncorrelated priors, that forms a view *before* consulting the machine rather than after, that is willing, now and then, to be the one card in the stack that guessed differently and was right. The wisdom of the crowd was never in the crowd. It was in the fact that each of them, for a moment, thought alone. Guard that. It may turn out to be the rarest and most valuable thing you own.
