# The Daily Compass #060 — Spheres

*Sunday, September 27, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

For a generation, the working assumption was that the world was *converging*. One global order, widening outward from a liberal core; markets dissolving borders; democracy as the default endpoint history was bending toward; and technology as a frictionless solvent thinning out distance, matter, and constraint until only information remained. Almost every institution we built and every strategy we ran assumed that arrow. This issue is about the death of that assumption — not as a lament, but as a map. Because something is replacing it, and the replacement is visible now on three fronts at once: in the *ideology* rising to organize American power, in the *material base* that actually underwrites power, and in the *structure* diplomats are reaching for as the universal order fails. The through-line is a single word — *spheres* — a world reorganizing into bounded, particular, materially-grounded zones rather than one convergent whole. And because none of this is unprecedented, we close with the 1957 book about the last time statesmen had to build an order out of the wreckage of a universal dream.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The idea: a world of civilizations, not creeds

**What happened.** For a decade the American New Right has been better at demolition than construction — clear about its contempt for liberal internationalism, vague about the alternative. In Foreign Affairs, the scholar Leonard Schuette argues the alternative has now arrived and has a name: ["The New Right's Grand Strategy After Trump"](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/new-rights-grand-strategy-after-trump) calls it *civilizationism*. The idea is a repudiation of the universalist premise that has organized US foreign policy since 1945 — the notion that American values are everyone's values and the world should converge on them. Civilizationism instead defines the West by *particulars*: territory, ancestry, and Christianity rather than a universal creed. It carves the globe into bounded civilizational spheres and assigns each a hegemon — the United States dominant in its hemisphere, content to cede Eastern Europe to Russia and East Asia to China, uninterested in the universal entanglements (NATO, the transatlantic alliance, the whole liberal-order scaffolding) that a creed-based mission required. Schuette's point is not that this is desirable but that it is *coherent* — a genuine grand strategy, poised to outlast Trump and to become the organizing worldview of a post-liberal Washington.

**Why it matters.** Ideas about world order usually look soft until they harden into policy, and Schuette's service is to name the hardening early. What makes civilizationism consequential is not its moral content — which is bracing and, to many, alarming — but its structural implication: it would formalize what has been creeping for a decade, the retreat from a single universal order into a world of spheres of influence, each with its own rules, its own hegemon, its own definition of who belongs. For anyone whose mental model of "the West" still runs on the postwar creed — an alliance of shared values, open to any nation that adopts them — this is the news that the ground has moved. The organizing question of American strategy is shifting from *how do we extend the order* to *which sphere is ours, and what do we concede in the others*. Whatever you think of the answer, the question itself is a rupture.

*Source: Schuette, Foreign Affairs (Aug 25, 2026). ~16 min.*

### ◆ 2. The base: power is physical again

**What happened.** If brief 1 is about the ideology of a spheres world, this is about what those spheres will actually be made of — and the answer is not software. In a much-discussed Not Boring essay, ["The Electric Slide,"](https://www.notboring.co/p/the-electric-slide) Packy McCormick and Sam D'Amico argue that America is playing the wrong game: pouring its capital and genius into *intelligence* — models, chips, the abstract layer — while China has quietly built the *electric tech stack*, the vast physical substrate of mining, refining, motors, batteries, and grid on which intelligence (and everything else) physically runs. The cost of that stack has fallen something like 99% over recent decades, and China now controls most of it, despite many of the underlying inventions being American or Japanese. Their frame borrows Clayton Christensen's "law of conservation of attractive profits": when one layer of a system commoditizes, the profits and the power migrate to the adjacent layer that's still scarce. If intelligence is becoming cheap and abundant, then the scarce, decisive layer is the physical one — the atoms and the amps — and that is precisely the layer America has been treating as someone else's problem.

**Why it matters.** This is the hinge of the whole issue, because it drags "grand strategy" out of the realm of ideas and into the realm of factories. A civilizational sphere is only as sovereign as its ability to *make things* and *power them*; you cannot run a hemisphere, or an AI industry, on financial engineering and clever models if the transformers, the refined gallium, and the grid capacity all come from a rival's sphere. The deep reversal here is against the frictionless-tech story of the last twenty years — the belief that the important economy had dematerialized into bits. It hasn't; it has just been *hiding* its materiality in someone else's supply chain. As the universal order fragments into spheres, the binding constraint on power reverts to the oldest one there is: who can actually build and electrify. The abstract century is ending, and the century of the physical is quietly resuming.

*Source: McCormick & D'Amico, Not Boring (2026). ~a lot; it is 40,000 words. Skim the section headers.*

### ◆ 3. The structure: a concert of the powers that be

**What happened.** So if the universal order is fracturing into materially-grounded spheres, what governs the whole? The political scientist Charles Kupchan's answer, in Foreign Policy's ["The World's Only Real Hope of Maintaining Order"](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/09/08/charles-kupchan-global-order-international-organizations-concert-system/) (adapted from his forthcoming book *Bringing Order to Anarchy*), is drawn straight from the 19th century: a *concert of powers*. He observes that regionalization is already happening organically — the US reasserting preeminence in the Western Hemisphere, China and Russia anchoring a bloc from Eastern Europe to the Pacific, regional powers like Brazil, Indonesia, and Saudi Arabia carving out autonomy. The paralyzed UN cannot govern this. What might is a small, high-level steering group of the major powers that sits *above* the regional spheres, cuts across ideological lines, and manages the frictions between them — explicitly modeled on the Concert of Europe that kept the great-power peace for a century after Napoleon.

**Why it matters.** Notice what happens when you set brief 3 beside brief 1: the sober centrist scholar (Kupchan) and the ascendant New Right ideology (Schuette's civilizationism) are describing *the same map* — a world of regional spheres with the US dominant in its hemisphere and rivals dominant in theirs. One arrives there as a worldview to be desired; the other as a structure to be managed. That convergence is the real signal of the issue: the spheres world is no longer a fringe prediction or a partisan preference but the shared premise of analysts who agree on almost nothing else. Kupchan's contribution is to insist that a concert is not surrender to power politics but a way to *govern* it — a mechanism for the great powers to keep their competition from becoming catastrophic. The uncomfortable part, which the next section makes explicit, is what a concert historically costs: order among the powers has often meant a free hand *within* each sphere, and the people inside a sphere rarely get a vote on its concert.

*Source: Kupchan, Foreign Policy (Sep 8, 2026); book* Bringing Order to Anarchy *(Yale, Oct 2026). ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: how an order gets built** — the deep history under brief 3 is a young Henry Kissinger's 1957 book (his doctoral work), [*A World Restored: Metternich, Castlereagh and the Problems of Peace, 1812–22*.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_World_Restored) It studies exactly the moment Kupchan wants to revive: how, after the French Revolution and Napoleon shattered Europe, a handful of statesmen — Austria's Metternich and Britain's Castlereagh above all — assembled the Concert of Europe that kept the great-power peace for nearly a century. Kissinger's enduring insight is that a durable order rests on two legs: *balance* (no power strong enough to dominate) and *legitimacy* (the major powers agreeing on the basic rules, so they seek advantage within the system rather than overthrowing it). Orders are not discovered or inherited; they are *constructed*, deliberately, usually out of wreckage. But keep the moral ledger open, because it's the issue's shadow: the legitimacy the Concert prized often meant propping up reactionary regimes and suppressing the nations and peoples inside each sphere. An order stable among the powers can be a cage for everyone else. That is the promise and the price of the spheres world, stated 169 years ago. ([A World Restored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_World_Restored), 1957)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the superpower race runs on a 19th-century craft** — here is where "power is physical" gets gloriously specific. The entire electric-and-AI buildout — every data center, every electrified grid — depends on *high-voltage transformers*, and the world is desperately short of them: demand is up roughly 300% since 2020, and a new U.S. executive order ([EO 14420](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/02/u-s-bulk-power-equipment-ban-set-to-worsen-critical-transformer-shortage/)) restricting foreign-made grid hardware is set to make the squeeze worse. The delicious part, widely reported in the trade press, is *why* they're scarce: the binding constraint isn't capital or even the specialized electrical steel — it's the tiny, aging workforce that still *hand-winds* the copper coils, an arcane manual craft with lead times now quoted in years, not months. Sit with the image. The most futuristic project in human history — machine intelligence, the electrified planet — bottlenecks on whether enough people know how to wind a coil of copper wire by hand. The abstract future is gated by the most physical of skills. Sovereignty, it turns out, runs through the winding room. (pv magazine / trade press, 2026)

**The survival guide to a world without rules** — the practical companion to the whole issue, from the historian Hal Brands in Foreign Policy's Fall 2026 issue, ["How to Survive in a World Without Rules."](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/09/09/hal-brands-rules-based-order-great-power-politics-geopolitics/) As great powers revive dreams of empire and the old norms shred, Brands's counsel is unsentimental and worth generalizing past nations to firms and individuals: *don't be nostalgic* for the order that's leaving; *get ready to pay higher risk premiums* on everything, because a ruleless world is a more dangerous and expensive one; and *invest in your own defense and your own manufacturing* — your capacity to protect and to make. It is the personal-strategy translation of brief 2: in a spheres world, self-reliance in the physical things stops being paranoia and becomes prudence. (Foreign Policy, Sep 9, 2026)

**Even the great story must answer to arithmetic** — a note that the material turn is showing up in finance, too. The NYU valuation scholar Aswath Damodaran calls this AI's ["Bar Mitzvah moment,"](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/ais-bar-mitzvah-moment-from-hope) the passage from childhood (grand narratives, limitless TAM) to adulthood (revenues, margins, unit economics, return on capital). His arithmetic is stark: six hyperscalers have committed something like $1.73 trillion in AI capital spending against an AI product-and-services market on the order of $250 billion, and he judges the ~$2 trillion marks on OpenAI and Anthropic *rich* — plausible only on heroic future revenue. It rhymes with the issue: the abstract, frictionless story — infinite intelligence, infinite scaling — is colliding with hard material limits, whether those limits are copper coils or cash flows. (Musings on Markets, Aug 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Set the pieces together and the shape is unmistakable. The ideology (Schuette) says the future is bounded civilizational spheres, not a universal order. The material base (McCormick and D'Amico) says power in those spheres will be measured in refined metals, motors, and megawatts, not lines of code. The structure (Kupchan) says the whole will be governed, if at all, by a concert of the great powers presiding over their regions — and Kissinger, from 1957, says that is exactly how orders have always been built, out of balance and legitimacy, deliberately, from wreckage, at a cost borne mostly by whoever lives inside a sphere without a seat at its table. Even the wildcard and the arithmetic point the same way: the abstract, frictionless, converging world we spent thirty years assuming is yielding to one that is physical, particular, and plural — a world you can measure in copper coils and capital expenditure and lines on a map. This is not necessarily a darker world, though it may be a harder one, and it is emphatically not the one most of our institutions and instincts were built for. The task the issue leaves you with is neither nostalgia for the order that is leaving nor romance for the one arriving, but the harder discipline of *seeing the new shape clearly* — because you cannot navigate, hedge, build, or resist a world you are still pretending is the last one. The map is being redrawn into spheres. Better to read it with your eyes open than to keep steering by a compass that points to a pole that has moved.
