# The Daily Compass #114 — Capable of ruling

*Monday, November 23, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1969 a Canadian education professor, Laurence J. Peter, and the writer Raymond Hull published a short, mischievous book. Hull wrote the text from Peter's research, and they meant it as satire. Readers took it as a diagnosis, and its central sentence has outlived almost every serious management book of its decade: ["In a hierarchy every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_principle) The logic is simple. People who do a job well get promoted. They keep getting promoted until they reach a job they do badly, and there they stay. For half a century the Peter principle was a joke that everyone recognised and nobody could measure. Now economists have measured it, and they have also begun to measure the other side of the ledger: what a good manager is actually worth. That matters now, because companies are removing management layers faster than at any time in recent memory, and some of the evidence suggests AI is speeding that up. This issue is about who we promote, what they're worth, and what happens when there are fewer of them.

## ── THE BRIEFS ──────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The best salesperson's reward

**What happened.** The first rigorous test of the Peter principle came from sales, where performance is unusually easy to count. In ["Promotions and the Peter Principle"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24343) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2019), Alan Benson, Danielle Li and Kelly Shue studied sales workers at 131 US firms. Firms did promote their best sellers: when a salesperson's sales doubled, their chance of promotion rose by 14.3% relative to the base rate. But the best sellers made worse managers. When a new manager's sales before promotion doubled, the sales of each person who then reported to them fell by 7.5%. The authors conclude that "the costs of promoting workers with lower managerial potential are high". They also found that firms rely less on sales figures when the management job carries more responsibility, a sign that some firms know the trade-off. Edward Lazear had offered a gentler explanation in ["The Peter Principle: A Theory of Decline"](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/peter-principle-theory-decline) (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2004). Some of a person's apparent brilliance before promotion is luck, so performance tends to fall back after it. This is regression to the mean, and firms can soften it by raising the bar for promotion but never remove it. The idea has a comic footnote. In 2010 three physicists at the University of Catania, Alessandro Pluchino, Andrea Rapisarda and Cesare Garofalo, won the Ig Nobel Prize for management for a [computer simulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.0455) suggesting that, if the Peter principle holds, organisations would do better promoting people at random. It is a stylised model, not field evidence, but it makes the point vividly. What does predict good management? In experiments by David Deming of Harvard and colleagues, good managers had roughly twice the impact on team performance that good workers had, and age, experience, personality and gender did not predict who they would be. Deming's advice, [in the Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2024/08/you-want-to-be-boss-you-probably-wont-be-good-at-it): "If you want to know who's going to be a good manager, make them manage."

**Why it matters.** Most organisations use promotion to do two different jobs at once: rewarding past performance and choosing future managers. The Benson, Li and Shue evidence shows how costly it is to mix them up. Three practical lessons follow. First, separate reward from role. Pay and status for outstanding individual work should not require taking on a team. This is the case for "dual ladders" that let engineers and specialists advance without managing, but they need to be built with care. A classic 1986 study by Thomas Allen and Ralph Katz found that some firms used their technical ladder as ["a repository for failing managers"](https://core.ac.uk/download/4379977.pdf), which quickly cheapens it. Second, test management before you promote into it: give candidates a project team, a new hire to mentor or a temporary lead role, and watch what happens to the people around them. Third, don't let volunteering decide it. Deming's warning is against relying on personality or on who raises a hand to say they want the job.

*Source: Benson, Li & Shue, QJE 134(4) (2019); Lazear, JPE 112(S1) (2004); Pluchino, Rapisarda & Garofalo, Physica A (2010); Deming et al., via Harvard Gazette (Aug 2024); Allen & Katz, R&D Management 16(2) (1986). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. What a boss is worth

**What happened.** If bad managers are costly, good ones should be valuable, and the evidence says they are. In ["The Value of Bosses"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18317) (*Journal of Labor Economics*, 2015), Edward Lazear, Kathryn Shaw and Christopher Stanton used five years of data from a large technology-based services firm, covering about 24,000 workers and 1,900 bosses. Replacing a boss in the bottom tenth for quality with one in the top tenth raised a team's total output by more than adding a tenth worker to a nine-person team would. Much of a good manager's value lies in keeping people. Mitchell Hoffman and Steven Tadelis studied a large high-tech firm in ["People Management Skills, Employee Attrition, and Manager Rewards"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24360) (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2021). They measured people-management skill from employees' own surveys of their managers. Moving a manager from the 10th to the 90th percentile of skill predicted a 60% fall in staff turnover, especially among the people the firm most wanted to keep. The authors argue the effect is causal, using new hires and workers who switch managers. But good managers also have their own incentives, and these can work against the firm. Ingrid Haegele studied a large German manufacturer, in [work now published in the *American Economic Review*](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/talent-hoarding-organizations-germany), and found that three-quarters of managers said "talent hoarding" happens. Hoarding means discouraging your best people from applying for jobs elsewhere in the company. It was more common where performance pay, team size and the visibility of talent gave managers more reason to do it. When rotations temporarily removed that incentive, more workers applied for promotions. Part of the difficulty is the job itself. In her study of new managers ("Becoming the Boss", *Harvard Business Review*, January 2007), Linda Hill of Harvard Business School found that new managers expect the role to bring authority. What it actually brings is interdependence: more entanglements, not more power.

**Why it matters.** Taken together, this research suggests that managers are one of the highest-leverage investments a firm can make, and one of the least measured. The value of a good manager shows up less in their own output than in their team's output, their team's retention, and the careers of the people who leave them for bigger jobs. So reward managers for what they export, not only for what they keep. If the managers whose people get promoted elsewhere are the ones who are rated and paid best, hoarding loses its appeal. And train new managers early. Hill's finding implies that the hardest part of the transition is giving up the idea that the job is about control.

*Source: Lazear, Shaw & Stanton, JOLE 33(4) (2015); Hoffman & Tadelis, JPE 129(1) (2021); Haegele, AER (AEA Research Highlights); Hill, HBR (Jan 2007). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. The great flattening

**What happened.** Just as research is showing how much managers matter, many companies are cutting them. In August 2025 Brian Welle, Google's vice president of people analytics, told staff that the company had [35% fewer managers of small teams](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/google-eliminated-35-percent-managers-overseeing-small-teams-exec-says-rcna227486) than a year earlier, referring to managers with fewer than three reports. In September this year Uber said it would [cut about 3,300 jobs](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/2/uber-lays-off-3300-employees-in-largest-cuts-since-the-pandemic), roughly 10% of staff, mostly in middle management. The plan reduces the number of managers by 20% and halves the number of teams with only one or two direct reports. Chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi blamed "more layers, more coordination, more fragmented ownership", and notably did not blame AI. Academic research suggests AI may be part of the story all the same. Using the online résumés of 18.1 million employees at US public companies, Michael Ewens and Xavier Giroud found in ["Corporate Hierarchy"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34162) (NBER working paper, 2025) that firms add layers after going public but flatten their hierarchies after adopting AI. A separate study by Anastassia Fedyk and colleagues, which drew on hundreds of millions of CVs, found that firms investing in AI became ["less top-heavy"](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/fewer-managers-more-tech-talent-how-early-ai-investments-rewired-company-org-charts/), with fewer middle managers and senior staff; the authors stress that this is correlation, not proof of cause. And as AI agents join the organisation, a new problem appears. In a [working paper](https://emmawiles.github.io/storage/ai_employee.pdf) by Emma Wiles of Boston University with researchers at BCG, about 23% of 1,261 HR and finance managers surveyed said their companies already list AI agents on their org charts. In an experiment, managers at those companies caught fewer planted errors in identical documents when the work was labelled as coming from an "AI employee" rather than an AI tool. The authors report a drop of about 16%.

**Why it matters.** Fewer layers can mean faster decisions and clearer ownership, and many firms really did carry too many managers of tiny teams. But the evidence in this issue points to three risks. First, wider spans make each manager more important, so a Peter-principle promotion does more damage than it used to. Second, a flatter company has fewer first rungs on which to learn management, which matters all the more if, as Deming's work suggests, the only good test of a manager is managing. Third, accountability can quietly leak. When work is done by AI agents that sit on the org chart like colleagues, someone still has to own their mistakes, and the Wiles experiment suggests managers feel less responsible for errors from an "AI employee". The firms that flatten well will probably be the ones that cut layers while investing more, not less, in the managers who remain.

*Source: NBC News (Aug 2025); Al Jazeera (Sep 2, 2026); Ewens & Giroud, NBER w34162 (2025); Fedyk et al., Berkeley Haas (Mar 2025); Wiles et al., working paper (Mar 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ──────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Rome's ladder of offices** — the Roman Republic built one of history's first formal promotion ladders, the *cursus honorum*. The [lex Villia Annalis of 180 BC](https://www.britannica.com/topic/lex-Villia-annalis) set minimum ages for the senior magistracies and required a gap of two years between offices, so that no one could rise to the top without being tested on the way. Sulla tightened the rules in 81 BC, partly in reaction to Marius holding the consulship again and again. Rome also bent its own rules when it wanted a proven general to stay in command, an early example of the exception for talent that every promotion system eventually makes. (Britannica; [Livius](https://www.livius.org/articles/concept/cursus-honorum/))

**🃏 WILDCARD: when stars do make good coaches** — not every expert falls to the Peter principle. In a study of about 15,000 NBA games, Amanda Goodall, Lawrence Kahn and Andrew Oswald found that having a former All-Star player as head coach was worth [about six extra places](https://news.cornell.edu/node/274355) in the league's rankings of team winning percentage, after allowing for the quality of the players. Their reading is that deep expertise in the core work can make a better leader, provided the person can also lead. The research is debated, but it is a useful reminder that the problem is not promoting experts. It is promoting them without checking. (*Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, 2011)

**The cost of founder mode** — in his 2024 essay ["Founder Mode"](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html), Paul Graham drew on a talk by Airbnb's Brian Chesky to argue that founders should stay deeply involved rather than manage only through their direct reports. This month Chesky told Guy Raz on *How I Built This* that the hands-on approach eventually cost him [most of his executive team](https://fortune.com/2026/11/04/airbnb-brian-chesky-founder-mode-executive-team/), who left or did not want to work for him. He still defends it. The lesson cuts both ways: skipping layers can be the right call, but it is a management choice with a price, not an escape from management. (Fortune, Nov 4, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────

Nearly nineteen centuries before Peter and Hull, the Roman historian Tacitus wrote the Peter principle's finest epitaph. Describing the emperor Galba, who held power for only a few months before he was murdered in AD 69, Tacitus wrote in his *Histories* that he seemed too great for a private citizen while he was one, and by everyone's agreement was capable of ruling, had he never ruled: [*"omnium consensu capax imperii nisi imperasset."*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capax_imperii_nisi_imperasset) The only way to know whether someone can rule is to let them, and by then the cost of being wrong has already been paid. Good organisations find cheaper ways to find out.
