# The Daily Compass #067 — Secret apprenticeships

*Monday, October 5, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Every expert you admire was once bad at their job, and somebody paid for it. Usually nobody noticed the payment. The junior analyst's clumsy first models, the resident's slow sutures, the new engineer's code that needed three rounds of review: organizations absorbed that cost because the work still had to get done, and because doing the work was how people learned. Expertise was a by-product of production. This issue is about what happens when that hidden arrangement breaks. A tool arrives that does the routine work faster, an office empties, a robot takes over the part of the operation the trainee used to do, and the output holds up, sometimes even improves, while the pipeline that produced the next generation of experts quietly stops. The three pieces below come at this from aviation psychology, labor economics and the operating room. They suggest the most important question about any new tool is not only "what can it do?" but "who will learn to do what it can't?"

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The irony nobody fixed

**What happened.** In 1983 the British psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper in the journal *Automatica* that has become one of the most cited texts in the study of humans and machines. ["Ironies of Automation"](https://blog.acolyer.org/2020/01/08/ironies-of-automation/) was about industrial process control and aircraft cockpits, and its argument is deceptively simple. When designers automate most of a job, they do not remove the human. They leave the human with whatever could not be automated: the rare, strange, high-stakes situations. Meanwhile the routine work that used to keep the operator's skills sharp has gone to the machine, so those skills fade. The operator's everyday job becomes watching the system, a task people are notoriously bad at sustaining. Then, when something finally goes wrong, the person least practiced at intervening is asked to intervene, under pressure, often with little time to understand what the machine has been doing. Her conclusion has an elegant cruelty: the more advanced the automated system, the more crucial the human operator's contribution may become, which means highly automated systems may need *more* investment in training, not less.

**Why it matters.** Bainbridge was writing about chemical plants and autopilots, but her paper reads today as if it were written about knowledge work. Replace "operator" with "the analyst who checks the AI's financial model," "the editor who signs off on generated copy" or "the engineer who reviews code she didn't write," and every irony holds. The skill needed to catch the machine's mistakes is built by doing the work the machine now does. Aviation took the lesson seriously, which is why pilots still hand-fly and train in simulators for failures they may never see. Most offices have not. The practical question for any team adopting automation is the one Bainbridge posed four decades ago: if this works ninety-nine times out of a hundred, who on the team will still know how to handle the hundredth, and how will they have learned?

*Source: Bainbridge, Automatica 19(6) (1983); summary by Adrian Colyer, the morning paper (2020). ~20 min for the original, which is short and readable.*

### ◆ 2. Mentoring has a price, and it shows up in the data

**What happened.** Teaching is usually invisible in productivity statistics. Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington and Amanda Pallais found a way to see it. In ["The Power of Proximity to Coworkers,"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880) an NBER working paper, they studied software engineers at a Fortune 500 company whose main campus had two buildings several blocks apart, so some engineers happened to sit with all their teammates while others had teammates in the other building. Using comments in code review as a measurable trace of mentoring, they found that before the pandemic, engineers who sat in the same building as their whole team received 22% more online feedback than those whose teammates were elsewhere. When COVID closed the offices, that advantage largely disappeared. The feedback came at a cost. Sitting together lowered programming output, especially for senior engineers, who were spending time teaching rather than shipping. The benefits went mainly to younger, less experienced employees, who were the ones building skills.

**Why it matters.** The paper's subtitle states the trade-off plainly: training for tomorrow or productivity today. Proximity made the firm less productive this quarter in order to make its people more capable later. That is exactly the kind of cost a dashboard punishes and a long-run strategy needs. The finding is not an argument that everyone must return to the office. It is an argument that when a change of tools or settings removes the informal channels through which expertise flows, the loss does not show up where anyone is looking. Output may even rise. What falls is the rate at which juniors become seniors, and you only discover it years later, when there is no one ready to promote. For managers of hybrid teams, the lesson is to build the teaching back in deliberately (pairing, structured review, real time for mentoring) and to count it as work rather than as a drag on productivity.

*Source: Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, NBER Working Paper 31880 (2023). ~50 min for the paper; the introduction carries the argument.*

### ◆ 3. The apprenticeship nobody budgeted for

**What happened.** The AI version of this problem arrived in an op-ed last week from Patrick T. Harker of Wharton, the former president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia, in [*Fortune*](https://fortune.com/2026/09/25/ai-junior-jobs-apprenticeship-talent-pipeline/) (republished at [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/opinion/entry-level-jobs-were-actually-secret-apprenticeships-all-along-and-ai-just-cut-the-subsidy/)). His argument is in the title: entry-level jobs were secret apprenticeships all along, and AI just cut the subsidy. Employers paid junior workers to do routine tasks, and the workers used those years to learn the judgment, institutional knowledge and professional habits that senior roles require. The routine output paid for the learning. If AI makes that routine output cheap, employers will be less willing to keep paying for the learning that came bundled with it. Harker is careful about cause: postings in the most AI-exposed occupations began falling in early 2022, months before ChatGPT, amid rising interest rates and the unwinding of pandemic hiring. But his conclusion is that the old bargain will not repair itself. Apprenticeships, structured work-based learning, mentoring and supervised practice will have to be built on purpose, and someone will have to pay for them.

**Why it matters.** Harker's frame connects the first two briefs. Bainbridge showed that automation erodes the skills needed to supervise it. Emanuel and colleagues showed that teaching is a real cost that firms bear, often unknowingly. Put the two together and AI creates a collective-action problem: every firm is better off letting someone else train the juniors and hiring them once they are good. A field study published years before ChatGPT shows what that looks like. When the organizational researcher Matt Beane of UC Santa Barbara studied robotic surgery ([*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0001839217751692)), he found that the robot let a senior surgeon operate alone, which pushed trainees to the margins. Only a minority became competent, and they did it through what Beane calls "shadow learning": specializing early, rehearsing on video, and seeking out work at the edge of their ability with little supervision, often in ways that bent the rules. A new study from Seoul National University, accepted at a conference on AI ethics and society, finds the same pattern taking shape in software, where junior work is being absorbed into "senior plus AI" workflows and juniors lose the productive struggle that used to make them senior. The pipeline does not break loudly. It narrows, and the people who still get through are those who find a way around the system.

*Source: Harker, Fortune (Sep 25, 2026), ~6 min; Beane, ASQ (2019), ~45 min, or his book The Skill Code (2024); Yu, Moon et al., AIES 2026 / [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17067) (Jul 2026).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: we know more than we can tell** — the idea under this whole issue was named by the chemist-turned-philosopher Michael Polanyi in his 1962 Terry Lectures at Yale, published as [*The Tacit Dimension*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge) (1966). His starting point is a single sentence: "we can know more than we can tell." His favorite example is a face. You can recognize someone you know among a thousand, perhaps a million, and yet you usually cannot say how you do it. Much of real expertise is like that: the surgeon's feel for tissue, the editor's sense that a paragraph is wrong before she knows why, the engineer's instinct for where the bug probably lives. Knowledge of this kind cannot be written in a manual. It passes mostly by working alongside someone who has it, which is precisely the channel that the briefs above describe being narrowed. (Polanyi, 1966)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the schoolboys and the 2,000 gods** — Babylon had no religion class. Its trainee scribes learned theology anyway, by copying. A new paper in the journal *Iraq* by Shana Zaia of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, ["Gods in the Classroom,"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/gods-in-the-classroom-religion-and-education-in-1st-millennium-bce-babylonia/EB059EE81804DCBC1848CACBD6298E0B) reconstructs first-millennium BCE schooling from surviving cuneiform exercise tablets from cities such as Babylon and Nippur. Students began with god-lists containing hundreds of names and advanced to versions that paired Sumerian and Akkadian names and added titles, nearly 2,000 divine names in all. They copied hymns to Marduk and, on some evidence, took their schoolwork to the temple as an offering. Craft, belief and identity were transmitted the same way: by the hand, one tablet at a time. ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-clay-tablets-reveal-babylonian-students.html), Sep 2026)

**The prophet of obsolete history** — in 1901 the banker and military theorist Jan Bloch declared that new weapons had transformed war so completely that studying military history had become useless, and he mocked officers who prepared by "musty precedents." Writing in [*War on the Rocks*](https://warontherocks.com/school-of-war-or-school-of-battle-educating-for-an-uncertain-future/), the West Point historians Michael P. Ferguson and Clifford J. Rogers argue that Bloch belongs to a long line of reformers who predicted that a technological revolution would make history-based education obsolete, and that experience has usually proved them wrong. It is a timely warning to anyone tempted to redesign training entirely around today's tools. The new tools change the character of the work. They rarely change its nature. (War on the Rocks, Oct 1, 2026)

**The tutor that taught less** — the week's most sobering AI-and-learning result comes from a large randomized trial at the University of Maryland, ["The Effects of Course-Integrated AI Tutoring,"](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1598) involving 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors across disciplines. Within the same courses, offering students an AI tutor *lowered* final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and cut participation on the course's learning platform by 0.90 standard deviations, with larger losses for first-generation students. Most requests to the tutor asked for explanations or answers rather than feedback on the students' own work. It is one university, one tool and a working paper, and other trials have found gains. But it is a clean warning that a tool that supplies answers can displace the struggle that produces learning. (EdWorkingPapers, Oct 2026)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

**Lenny's Podcast — Molly Graham, "The grief, loneliness, and burnout sweeping through the tech industry right now"** (Sep 27, 2026)
Graham, who led teams at Google, Facebook, Quip and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, is known for the advice to "give away your Legos": hand off the work you've mastered so you can grow into the next role. Here she explains why that advice no longer works in the same way when you are handing work to AI rather than to a person, and what that does to how people learn and grow at work. **Queue it** for the first half; at 94 minutes, the second half is mostly advice for managers. ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-grief-loneliness-and-burnout))

**EconTalk — "The Death of Reading," with James Marriott** (Sep 21, 2026)
The *Times* columnist and author of *The New Dark Ages* argues that as we abandon sustained reading, our societies begin to resemble pre-literate ones, and that this threatens the Enlightenment's gains, including rational public argument. Russ Roberts traces literacy with him from Mesopotamia to the 18th-century "Reading Revolution." It is the cultural version of today's theme: a skill that took centuries to spread can fade when the tools change. **Queue it**, with the caution that it is a declinist argument and Roberts pushes back only gently. ([EconTalk](https://www.econtalk.org/the-death-of-reading-with-james-marriott/))

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1968 the English furniture designer David Pye, who taught at the Royal College of Art, published [*The Nature and Art of Workmanship*](https://blog.lostartpress.com/2018/12/02/deflating-david-pye-50-years-later/), a short book that makes a distinction worth carrying around. In what he called the *workmanship of certainty*, the quality of the result is fixed before production begins: the jig, the mold and the machine settings determine what comes out, as in mass production and, in its pure form, full automation. In the *workmanship of risk*, the result is never guaranteed. It depends on the judgment, dexterity and care the maker brings to the work as it goes. Pye was not simply romanticizing the hand against the machine; plenty of work done with machines, he argued, is still workmanship of risk, because the outcome depends on the person guiding the tool. Read today, his distinction suggests that every technology moves the risk somewhere, and that the quality of a civilization's things depends on whether people still exist who can work where the risk now sits. Today's briefs are about that relocation. AI, like the autopilot and the surgical robot before it, turns a great deal of risky work into certain work. That is a real gain. But the risk does not disappear. It concentrates in the unusual case, the judgment call, the moment when the machine is confidently wrong, and those moments still need someone who has practiced at the edge of their ability, made mistakes and been corrected by someone better. The question every organization now faces, and every person thinking about a career, is not whether to use the new tools. It is where the workmanship of risk will now be learned, and who is willing to pay for the apprenticeship.
