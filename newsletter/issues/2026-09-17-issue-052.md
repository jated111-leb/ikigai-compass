# The Daily Compass #052 — What the strong do

*Thursday, September 17, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Twenty-five centuries ago, on a small Aegean island, envoys from imperial Athens told the people of Melos that there was no point discussing justice, because "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians appealed to fairness, to the gods, to their Spartan kin; the Athenians destroyed them anyway — killed the men, enslaved the women and children — and the whole grim exchange was set down by Thucydides as the coldest paragraph in the founding text of political realism. It is having a moment. The line is quoted now at Davos and in war rooms, by doves warning against empire and hawks justifying it, because the polite fiction of a rules-based order has worn thin enough that the old grammar of raw power is showing through. This issue is about that grammar — the return of exclusion, coercion, and force as the open currency of statecraft — read through the man who understood it best and is quoted worst. Because the striking thing about Thucydides is not that he thought the strong should win. It is that he watched what happened to Athens two years after Melos, and left us the most devastating warning ever written about what power does to the mind that holds it.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Everyone quotes Thucydides. Almost everyone is wrong.

**What happened.** A superb [*Aeon* essay](https://aeon.co/essays/thucydides-account-of-war-is-as-timely-as-it-is-misunderstood) takes on the most misused author in international relations. Thucydides is claimed by the hawks, who read the Melian Dialogue as a bracing endorsement of might-makes-right, and by the doves — the Canadian PM Mark Carney reached for him at Davos as an anti-Trump warning — who read it as a lament for lost norms. The essay's argument is that both camps miss the point, because Thucydides is not taking sides at all: his real subject is *self-deception*, and he distributes it evenly. The Athenians are deceived by their power, which convinces them that necessity excuses everything and that they cannot fall. But the Melians are deceived too — by their justice. They persuade themselves that being in the right will bring rescue from Sparta or the gods, that virtue is a strategy. Thucydides shows both delusions colliding, and both sides paying.

**Why it matters.** This is the hype-deflation the moment needs, because we are drowning in bad Thucydides — in the swagger that says power is its own justification and in the sentimentality that says the arc of history bends toward the rules. The essay's Thucydides is colder and wiser than either: he thinks a "fervid attachment to justice," especially in wartime, carries its own dangerous illusions — that the just will prosper *because* they are just, and that people are as attached to justice as they flatter themselves to be. For a reader anywhere near a conflict zone, this lands hard. It is a warning against both the intoxication of strength and the consolation of righteousness — against believing either that you cannot lose because you are powerful, or that you cannot lose because you are right. The strategic mind Thucydides is trying to train is the one that can hold power and justice in view at once and be fooled by neither. That mind is rare in any era. It is nearly extinct in this one.

*Source: Aeon (Aug 2026). ~16 min.*

### ◆ 2. The age of the closed door

**What happened.** If brief 1 is the psychology of power, here is its 2026 mechanism. In *War on the Rocks*, Patrick Gill-Tiney of King's College London names the organizing logic of the new competition: ["The Age of Strategic Exclusion."](https://warontherocks.com/the-age-of-strategic-exclusion/) The old great-power game, he argues, was largely a contest over *shared* access — who could trade, invest, and ally most widely in an open system. The new one is a contest over *restricted* access: the strategic move is to lock your rival *out*. Territorial conquest is the most extreme form — Russia's war in Ukraine is, at bottom, a bid to exclude the West from a country it wanted inside its own orbit — but exclusion runs through everything now: the United States walling China off from advanced semiconductors, China using Belt and Road to secure privileged positions in ports and grids and supply chains, the weaponizing of security guarantees and economic ties into instruments of denial.

**Why it matters.** Strategic exclusion is the concrete shape that "the strong do what they can" takes when the strong are nuclear-armed and can't simply invade each other. You cannot conquer China, so you strangle its chip supply; you cannot hold all of Ukraine, so you ensure no one else can integrate it; you cannot rule the global South, so you wire its infrastructure to depend on you. It reframes a hundred scattered headlines — export controls, port deals, the scramble for critical minerals — as a single coherent game whose object is not to win access but to deny it. And it is a peculiarly dangerous game, because exclusion is inherently escalatory: every door one power closes, another experiences as an act of aggression, and the tools of denial (sanctions, blockades, chokepoints) blur steadily into the tools of war. The open, positive-sum world was not just more prosperous; it was, precisely because it was about access rather than denial, structurally less prone to the spiral this new logic invites. We have not just lost a trading system. We have swapped the logic of the open hand for the logic of the closed door.

*Source: Gill-Tiney, War on the Rocks (Sep 2026). ~13 min.*

### ◆ 3. A remade Iran, and the neighborhood it leaves

**What happened.** Nowhere is the grammar of power more legible right now than next door. In *Foreign Affairs*, the Johns Hopkins scholars Narges Bajoghli and Vali Nasr argue, in ["Iran's New Grand Strategy,"](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/iran/irans-new-grand-strategy) that the Islamic Republic emerged from the U.S.-Israeli war of early 2026 — the bombing that gutted its industry, the naval blockade that throttled its economy — battered but not broken, and is now recalibrating rather than retreating: a leaner, more bureaucratized, arguably more durable state feeling for a new regional posture after the shattering of its old forward-defense model. The thesis is genuinely contested — critics charge it dresses a strategic defeat up as reinvention — and that argument is itself part of the story: no one, inside Iran or out, yet knows whether what happened in 2026 was the end of the Islamic Republic's project or its painful reset.

**Why it matters.** For this newsletter's reader, this is not geopolitics in the abstract; it is the weather. The reconstitution of Iranian strategy is the single biggest variable shaping the security environment of Lebanon and the wider Levant, and the Bajoghli-Nasr debate is really a debate about which Thucydidean error is being made — whether Tehran is the overreaching Athenian, mistaking survival for strength and setting up the next catastrophe, or whether its adversaries are, having "won" the war, deceiving themselves that a battered adversary is a beaten one. Both readings fit the pattern of brief 1, and both have been wrong before in exactly this region. What the essay makes unavoidable is that the post-2026 Middle East is being rebuilt on the logic of briefs 1 and 2 — power and exclusion, not rules and access — and that the states now redrawing the map are doing it with the same mix of clear-eyed calculation and self-flattering delusion that Thucydides catalogued on Melos. The question for everyone living downwind is which is which, and no one yet knows.

*Source: Bajoghli & Nasr, Foreign Affairs (Jun 2026). ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Melian Dialogue** — Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book V (416 BC). The primary source under this entire issue, and the most quoted, least finished argument in the canon. Everyone remembers the Athenian line — "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" — and the massacre that followed. Almost no one remembers what Thucydides does next: he places the Melian atrocity immediately before the Sicilian Expedition, in which the same Athens, drunk on its own power and certain it cannot fail, sails to Syracuse, overreaches catastrophically, and is annihilated — the beginning of its long defeat. That juxtaposition *is* the argument. Read alone, the dialogue looks like a hymn to power. Read where Thucydides put it, it is a trap closing: the intoxication that let Athens butcher Melos is the same intoxication that destroys Athens. Why now: everyone reaching for the famous line is quoting the setup and skipping the punchline. The strong do what they can — and then, very often, precisely because they could, they do the thing that ruins them. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melian_dialogue), 416 BC)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the equation was a shadow all along** — a gorgeous escape from statecraft into pure wonder: [Quanta reports](https://www.quantamagazine.org/theory-of-fluids-enters-the-21st-century-20260817/) that physicists have spent two decades quietly rebuilding the theory of fluids from its foundations — redefining what it even *means* to be a fluid in terms of deep symmetries, and showing that the 200-year-old Navier-Stokes equations, among the most-used in all of engineering, fall out as a mere *consequence* of those symmetries rather than a starting point. One of the workhorses of modern physics turns out to be the visible shadow of something more fundamental underneath. A reminder, on a week heavy with power, that some of the deepest human progress is made by people simply trying to understand why the water moves the way it does. (Aug 17)

**The machine front** — the instrument of raw power is changing faster than the theory of it. Per [West Point's Modern War Institute](https://mwi.westpoint.edu/battlefield-drones-and-the-accelerating-autonomous-arms-race-in-ukraine/) and other frontline reporting, Ukraine's drone output has gone from a few thousand units in 2022 to more than seven million this year; its robotic systems ran over 22,000 frontline missions in a single recent quarter; and in April, Zelensky announced a Russian position taken by ground robots and drones *alone*, with no Ukrainian troops involved and none lost. Strategists now call 2026 "the year of the unmanned ground vehicle," with one Ukrainian corps moving to replace up to a third of its most-exposed frontline personnel with machines. The Melian calculus — who can impose their will, and at what cost in blood — is being rewritten in real time, as the "cost in blood" half of the equation is quietly automated away. (2026)

**How long does a moat last?** — the corporate mirror of strategic exclusion. Michael Mauboussin's ["The Neglected Value Driver"](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/en-us/individual-investor/insights/consilient-observer/the-neglected-value-driver.html) revives a concept he coined decades ago — the *competitive advantage period*, or how long a company can earn returns above its cost of capital before competition erodes them — and argues it's the single most-neglected term in valuation. Set it against the contrarian cry from operators like [OnlyCFO](https://www.onlycfo.io/p/revenue-durability-is-failing-in) that in the AI era that period is *collapsing* — that when switching costs vanish and inference gets 80% cheaper in two years, the durable moat many software businesses assumed becomes a puddle. Exclusion, it turns out, is as central to corporate strategy as to grand strategy: the whole game is locking in an advantage before someone locks you out. (2026)

**Governing from too far away** — a quietly useful [HBR study](https://hbr.org/2026/05/what-global-companies-lose-when-decision-making-revolves-around-headquarters), drawn from 150+ leaders across the US, Europe, Asia and the Middle East, on what multinationals lose when every real decision routes back through headquarters: the periphery, closest to the actual problem, is reduced to reporting upward and executing downward, and its intelligence arrives too late to shape anything. The fix is to reverse the flow — let the region nearest the problem frame it first, and engineer cheap channels that push frontline knowledge to the center early. A management finding, but read it against briefs 2 and 3 and it rhymes: the center that cannot see clearly from the periphery, and mistakes its distance for objectivity, is making a smaller version of the oldest imperial mistake there is. (May)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The reason Thucydides survives is that he refused the two comforts on offer, and they are the same two comforts on offer now. The first is the comfort of the strong: that power is self-justifying, that necessity dissolves morality, that because you *can*, the question of whether you *should* simply evaporates. The second is the comfort of the weak: that being wronged is a kind of strength, that justice is a strategy, that the arc bends your way because you are right. Thucydides watched both consolations get people killed. The Athenians at Melos were not wrong that they had the power; they were wrong that having it made them safe, and Syracuse was the bill. The Melians were not wrong that they had the justice; they were wrong that having it would save them, and the grave was the bill. What he offers instead is the hardest thing of all: sight without consolation — the discipline of seeing your own power and your own righteousness as *facts about your position*, not guarantees about your future, and of noticing the exact moment when either one begins to feel like destiny. That is the moment, he shows again and again, just before the fall. We are entering an age that has rediscovered the logic of Melos — exclusion, coercion, the closed door, the automated front — and mostly rediscovered it in the triumphal key, as license. The deeper lesson of the text everyone is quoting is the opposite of license. It is that the strong do what they can, and then, blinded by the very power that let them, do what destroys them. The only real advantage, in the end, is the one almost no one holds: the refusal to be deceived — least of all by yourself.
