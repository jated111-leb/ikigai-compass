# The Daily Compass #062 — The unfinished universe

*Tuesday, September 29, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

It is comforting to believe the foundations are settled — that the big questions (what the universe is made of and doing, where matter comes from, what separates the living from the dead) were answered by clever people some decades ago, and only the details remain. This issue is a bracing correction to that comfort, and it means the correction kindly. Because the most exciting thing happening in science right now is not the filling-in of a finished picture but the *reopening* of its foundations: three results from this year, each taking a hammer to a pillar most of us assumed was load-bearing and permanent. One questions whether the universe is accelerating at all. One watches matter wink into existence from pure energy inside a tabletop machine. One proposes a way to recognize *life itself* without knowing any of its chemistry. None is the last word — two are hotly contested, which is part of the point. Together they are an invitation to the oldest and best scientific posture, the one this whole issue is really about: not the false comfort of settled knowledge, but the live, humbling, electric wonder of standing at an edge that is still being drawn.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The 68% of the universe that might not be there

**What happened.** For a quarter-century, the textbook has said the universe's expansion is *accelerating*, pushed apart by a mysterious "dark energy" that makes up roughly 68% of everything — a discovery that won the 2011 Nobel Prize. A new re-analysis published in the *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* by Animesh Sah, Mohamed Rameez, and the Oxford physicist Subir Sarkar (widely resurfaced this month) argues the acceleration may be an *artifact*. Their move is subtle and physical: the Type Ia supernovae used as cosmic "standard candles" turn out to depend on the *age* of the stars that produced them, and once you [correct for that progenitor-age bias](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260907201600.htm) across 1,700+ supernovae, the evidence for acceleration weakens — even, they claim, reversing toward a gently *decelerating* universe. It dovetails with David Wiltshire's "timescape" cosmology, in which the apparent acceleration is a mirage produced by clocks ticking at different rates in dense galaxies versus empty voids — no dark energy required.

**Why it matters.** Be careful here, and that care is the lesson: this is a genuinely *contested* claim, disputed by many cosmologists, and it may not survive — the coming DESI and Euclid surveys will referee it. But sit with what's even *on the table*. The possibility that the single largest constituent of the cosmos — the thing most of physics has spent twenty-five years trying to explain — is a *statistical shadow* cast by an uncorrected bias in our measuring sticks. Whether or not Sarkar's group is right, the fact that the question is live tells you something humbling about the state of our knowledge: the confident pie chart of "68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, 5% everything we understand" rests on foundations still under active dispute. The universe has not agreed to be as settled as the textbook diagram implies. That is not a scandal. It is science doing the only thing that keeps it honest — reopening its own case.

*Source: Sah, Rameez & Sarkar, MNRAS (2026); resurfaced Sep 2026. Contested. ~12 min for the writeups.*

### ◆ 2. Matter, popping into existence, on a tabletop

**What happened.** Here is a sentence that should not be possible to write about a device sitting in a room in North Carolina: physicists watched *new matter come into being from pure energy*, and did it on purpose. A team at the Duke Quantum Center led by Christopher Monroe, [publishing in *Nature Physics*,](https://pratt.duke.edu/news/quantum-simulation-string-breaking/) encoded a simplified model of the strong nuclear force into a chain of 13 trapped ions and simulated "string breaking." Picture two bound particles — like a quark and antiquark — pulled apart. The force between them doesn't weaken with distance the way gravity or magnetism does; it behaves like a stretching string, storing more and more energy, until the string *snaps* and that stored energy condenses into a brand-new particle–antiparticle pair. It is how matter forms in the aftermath of high-energy collisions and, plausibly, in the early universe — a process normally glimpsed only inside the Large Hadron Collider. The team watched it unfold in real time, and even caught an unexpected "edge-driven" mechanism, pairs forming at the ends of the string and spreading inward.

**Why it matters.** Two kinds of wonder are braided here. The first is the physics itself: the vacuum is not empty and matter is not conserved in the intuitive way; given enough energy, *stuff simply appears*, obeying Einstein's E=mc² read in the direction we usually forget — energy becoming mass, being becoming beings. The second is methodological, and quietly historic: a modest, programmable quantum machine reproduced a process that classical supercomputers struggle to simulate, which means quantum simulators are crossing from promise into *instrument* — becoming tabletop laboratories for questions that used to require a 27-kilometer particle accelerator or the first microseconds of creation. When the tools change like this, the frontier doesn't just advance; it *relocates*, into rooms and budgets that were never before able to ask these questions. The origin of matter is now something you can study down the hall.

*Source: Duke Quantum Center, Nature Physics (Sep 23, 2026). ~10 min.*

### ◆ 3. A way to recognize life without knowing its chemistry

**What happened.** The third pillar is the most primal question of all — *what is life?* — and a group at the University of Glasgow led by Leroy Cronin (with Lindsay Rutter and Abhishek Sharma) has proposed an answer with a working payoff, in [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2619198123). Their framework, Assembly Theory, sidesteps the endless definitional debate by asking a different question: not *is this alive?* but *how hard is this object to build?* Every molecule has an "assembly index" — the minimum number of steps to construct it from basic parts. Below a complexity threshold, physics and chemistry can throw molecules together by chance. Above it, the only thing in the known universe that reliably produces complex molecules *in abundance* is a selective, evolving process — life. The new work shows you can infer a molecule's assembly index straight from *mass-spectrometry data*, using machine learning, without first working out its structure. The upshot: a life-detector that doesn't presume life looks anything like Earth's — a way to scan the plumes of Enceladus or the sands of Mars and ask, agnostically, *has abundant high-assembly complexity appeared here?*

**Why it matters.** Assembly Theory is itself contested — critics argue it's a repackaging of older complexity measures — so hold it, too, provisionally. But the *reframe* is the durable prize, and it's beautiful: it turns "what is life?" from a metaphysical argument into a *measurable physical quantity*, a threshold on the difficulty of matter's arrangements. That is exactly the move that turns a philosophical impasse into a science — the way "what is heat?" became temperature and "what is heredity?" became the gene. Whether or not the specific index holds, the ambition points at something real: a definition of the living that could travel to worlds whose chemistry we can't imagine, and recognize the signature of selection in the shape of complexity itself. It reopens the oldest boundary — living versus not — not as a word game but as a number you could, in principle, measure on another planet. The question of life is not closed. We are, remarkably, still learning how to ask it.

*Source: Cronin group (Rutter, Sharma, Cronin), PNAS (Aug 2026). Contested framework. ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: what it is like to be a bat** — for all three briefs' reopening of the *external* foundations, one frontier stays stubbornly shut, and Thomas Nagel named it in 1974. In ["What Is It Like to Be a Bat?",](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Nagel_Bat.pdf) he argued that consciousness has an *irreducibly subjective* character — there is something it is like, from the inside, to be a bat navigating by sonar — and that no amount of objective, third-person physical description can ever capture that inner point of view. You can map every neuron and echolocation click and still not know what the bat's world *feels like* to the bat. It is the permanent counterweight to a science-triumphant issue: even as we reopen dark energy, watch matter form, and quantify life, the one thing the outward-facing method structurally cannot reach is the inward fact of experience itself — including the experience of the scientist doing the reaching. The universe may be unfinished on the outside. But the deepest unfinished question is the one looking back out through your own eyes. ([Nagel, 1974](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Nagel_Bat.pdf))

**🃏 WILDCARD: order, coaxed out of pure chance** — a delicious result for anyone who thinks randomness is the enemy of structure. This month *Quanta* [reported](https://www.quantamagazine.org/mathematicians-harness-randomness-to-crack-a-55-year-old-conjecture-20260928/) that Lisa Sauermann (Bonn) and Huy Tuan Pham (Chicago) closed a 55-year-old problem posed by the great combinatorialist (and juggler) Ronald Graham in 1971 — a question about whether you can always reorder a set of numbers so their running sums stay distinct in "clock" arithmetic. The surprise is *how* they did it: by deliberately injecting **randomness**. Their key tool, "anti-concentration," shows that a randomly chosen arrangement is overwhelmingly likely to have the orderly property you want — so you prove a rigid, deterministic fact by appealing to chance. It's a recurring magic in modern math: to force hidden order to reveal itself, stop trying to construct it and let randomness do the work. A small, beautiful reminder that disorder and structure are not opposites but collaborators. (Quanta, Sep 2026)

**The AI-discovery question gets real** — the one dispatch from the machine world, and it's an epistemology question wearing a lab coat. Prompted by a claim that an AI system made its "first scientific discovery," *MIT Technology Review* [asks the sharp question](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/28/1145230/when-can-we-say-ai-made-a-scientific-discovery/): what actually *counts* as a discovery? In the case at issue, AI agents narrowed hundreds of thousands of candidates and flagged an uncatalogued pattern near a known enzyme — genuinely useful — but did not do the harder, meaning-making work of determining what the enzyme *does*. The field is now arguing, productively, over where pattern-finding ends and discovery begins. The stakes are practical: overclaim now, and we'll struggle to recognize the real thing when it arrives. It rhymes with brief 3 — the difference between generating complexity and *understanding* it is exactly the line at issue. (MIT Tech Review, Sep 2026)

**A breadcrumb trail in another galaxy** — a pure hit of cosmic wonder. Astronomers (Julie Kiel Holm and Sarah Pearson among them), publishing in *Nature*, found the [first "stellar stream" from a globular cluster outside the Milky Way](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10878-w) — a faint ribbon of stars torn from a cluster and stretched by gravity across an ultra-diffuse galaxy some 115 million light-years away. Because such streams bend and fray according to the invisible mass around them, they are exquisite tracers of *dark matter* — and finding one beyond our own galaxy opens a new way to map the unseen scaffolding of the cosmos, soon to multiply as Euclid and the Roman telescope come online. A trail of stars, followed to find the thing that has no light at all. (Nature, 2026)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

The perfect companion to this issue's foundations-reopening spirit, and beautifully hosted by Janna Levin and Steven Strogatz. The physicist Jonathan Halliwell walks through *decoherence* — the still-not-fully-settled process by which the fuzzy, superposed quantum world hardens into the crisp, definite classical one we actually experience — and the question of whether there is a real *boundary* between the two, or just a gradient we haven't fully understood. **Queue it**: forty-seven minutes on one of the deepest open seams in physics, in plain language. ([The Joy of Why, Sep 17](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-does-the-quantum-world-end-and-ours-begin-20260917/))

The palate cleanser, and a small masterpiece of the "small subject, enormous awe" form. The biologist-writer David George Haskell delivers an ode to *grass* — the humble, world-shaping family of plants that fed the rise of grazing animals and then of us, and with which our fate is quietly entangled. **Queue it** when you want wonder without heavy lifting: short, lyrical, and a reminder that the frontier of the marvelous is often underfoot, not overhead. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Three hammers, three pillars, and the same strange gift. If the universe isn't accelerating, if matter blinks into being from pure energy on a lab bench, if life is a measurable threshold of complexity rather than a mystery or a miracle — then the foundations we were told to take for granted are, in fact, *still being poured*. The instinct is to find this unsettling, as if the value of science were the security of its answers. But the scientists in this issue suggest the opposite. The physicist Carlo Rovelli, in a recent Noema essay, argues that the supposed gap between the objective universe and the subject observing it is an illusion — that ["objectivity without a subject is an abstract construct,"](https://www.noemamag.com/subjective-perspective-makes-objective-reality-everywhere-whole/) and that the knowing mind is not a spectator outside reality but part of what makes it whole. And the biologist Sean B. Carroll, writing in Nautilus, makes the case that [awe and wonder are not the *byproducts* of discovery but its *engine*](https://nautil.us/more-than-a-feeling-1242661) — the emotions that send people to the frontier and keep them there. Put those together with Nagel's bat and you have the issue's real argument: the point of science was never to hand you a finished universe to file away. It was to keep you standing, alert and a little astonished, at the moving edge where the biggest questions are still open — the expansion of the cosmos, the birth of matter, the definition of life, and the strange inward fact that *you*, a temporary arrangement of that same matter, are the part of the universe that gets to wonder about it at all. The universe is unfinished. So, thankfully, is our understanding. That is not a gap to be closed. It is the whole invitation.
