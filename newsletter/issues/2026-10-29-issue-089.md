# The Daily Compass #089 — The fable of the keys

*Thursday, October 29, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In June 1883 the railway bridge over the Murray River opened at Albury, joining the colonies of New South Wales and Victoria by rail for the first time. It joined them, but not quite. New South Wales had built its line to the "standard" gauge of 4 feet 8½ inches; Victoria had chosen the broader Irish gauge of 5 feet 3 inches. So at Albury every passenger travelling between Sydney and Melbourne had to get off one train, walk across the platform and board another, while freight was unloaded and reloaded by hand. During the Second World War every soldier and every crate of supplies moving between the two states went through the same ritual. The break was not removed until 1962, when the first standard-gauge freight train ran through to Melbourne, 79 years after the bridge opened; the last capital-to-capital line, Melbourne to Adelaide, was converted only in 1995, at a cost of A$160 million. Nobody had chosen to make Australian railways inefficient. A few early decisions, made separately and for local reasons, hardened into steel and then into habit. This issue is about that process, path dependence and lock-in: how early choices become hard to undo, when the story of a "locked-in" inferior standard is true and when it is a myth, and where the switching costs of the AI era are quietly accumulating.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The fable of the keys

**What happened.** The most famous example of lock-in is the keyboard you are probably reading this on. In ["Clio and the Economics of QWERTY"](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1805621) (*American Economic Review*, 1985), the economic historian Paul David argued that the QWERTY layout, designed in the 1870s for mechanical typewriters, survived not because it was best but because of history: once typists trained on it and employers bought machines for those typists, each reinforced the other, and a superior design such as August Dvorak's 1930s layout could never break in. Four years later W. Brian Arthur gave the idea its formal theory in ["Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v99y1989i394p116-31.html) (*Economic Journal*, 1989): when a technology becomes more attractive the more people adopt it, small chance events early on can tip a whole market onto one path, and not necessarily the best one. Then came the twist. In ["The Fable of the Keys"](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1069950) (*Journal of Law & Economics*, 1990), Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis went back to the evidence for Dvorak's superiority and found it surprisingly thin. The much-cited 1944 US Navy study showing large gains from retraining had been overseen in some capacity by Dvorak himself, who held a patent on the layout, and a more careful 1956 study for the US General Services Administration failed to confirm its results. They concluded that there was virtually no good evidence that QWERTY was inferior at all.

**Why it matters.** Both halves of the story are useful. Arthur's insight is real and powerful: in markets with network effects, standards and complementary investments, early leads compound, and the winner can stay ahead long after its advantages have faded. That is why standards battles and early adoption matter so much in technology. But Liebowitz and Margolis add a necessary discipline. Claims that the world is "stuck" with an inferior option are often made by people selling the alternative, and the cost of switching is real. Before paying to escape a supposed lock-in, it is worth asking how much better the alternative actually is, who produced the evidence, and whether the incumbent has survived because it is good enough rather than because it is entrenched. Path dependence is a reason to choose carefully at the start, when choices are cheap, not a universal excuse for regret later.

*Source: David, AER 75(2) (1985); Arthur, Economic Journal 99 (1989); Liebowitz & Margolis, Journal of Law & Economics 33(1) (1990). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Imprinted at birth

**What happened.** Organisations have their own versions of a railway gauge: the assumptions built in at the start, which shape everything that follows. The Stanford Project on Emerging Companies, led by James Baron and Michael Hannan from the mid-1990s, followed about 170 young Silicon Valley technology firms and asked their founders how they had pictured the employment relationship: whether they hired "stars" and paid for talent, built a "commitment" culture on emotional bonds and fit, ran an "engineering" culture, or something more bureaucratic or autocratic. In ["Organizational Blueprints for Success in High-Tech Start-Ups"](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2002/05/44-3-organizational-blueprints-for-success-in-high-tech-start-ups-lessons-from-the-stanford-project-on-emerging-companies) (*California Management Review*, 2002), they reported that these founding blueprints persisted as companies grew, and that firms which changed their blueprint "paid a heavy price" in higher staff turnover and weaker performance; in related work, a change of blueprint was more destabilising than replacing the founding chief executive. A different kind of inertia appears in Clark Gilbert's study of eight American newspapers confronting the internet (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2005). The sense of threat was strong enough to make them invest in digital ventures, overcoming what he called resource rigidity. But the same threat made them cling harder to their established routines, so the new online units were often run on the old print playbook.

**Why it matters.** For founders, the lesson is that early choices about culture and organisation are not easily revised; they become part of the company's identity, and changing them later is expensive and risky. It is worth deciding deliberately what kind of organisation you are building while it is still small. For established companies facing disruption, Gilbert's finding is a warning that fear is a double-edged tool: it can unlock budgets while locking in habits. The usual remedy is structural separation, giving a new venture its own leadership, processes and measures, so that it is not quietly run on the routines of the business it is meant to replace. In both cases, inertia is not simply resistance to change. It is the accumulated weight of decisions that once made sense.

*Source: Baron & Hannan, California Management Review 44(3) (2002); Gilbert, Academy of Management Journal 48(5) (2005). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Where AI lock-in really lives

**What happened.** Much of the anxiety about AI lock-in has focused on dependence on a single model provider. A survey published on the [Stack Overflow blog](https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/10/21/survey-says-you-ll-swap-the-model-but-everything-you-build-around-it-is-staying-put/) on October 21 suggests the risk has moved. The software company Stacklok surveyed 520 technical leaders at North American companies with more than 500 employees, all running AI models or agents in pilot or production. Only 8% relied on a single model provider, and a quarter used five or more: swapping models has become routine. What is hard to move is everything built around them. Asked which parts of their AI systems were hardest to migrate, 53% named the integrations connecting AI to their internal systems, and roughly as many named multi-step agent workflows. Around four in five said they had already delayed, scaled back or restructured a migration because the switching costs looked too high. Stacklok sells tools for this layer, so its framing deserves some caution, but the pattern is consistent with a deeper argument. In ["The Hardware Lottery"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06489) (2020), the computer scientist Sara Hooker argued that research ideas in AI often succeed because they suit the available hardware and software, not because they are better than the alternatives, and that specialised chips make it "increasingly costly to stray off of the beaten path".

**Why it matters.** Lock-in in AI is forming at two levels. At the level of the industry, today's dominant architectures and chips reinforce one another, as Hooker described, which makes it harder for alternatives to be tested fairly. At the level of the company, the switching costs are building up not in the choice of model but in the integrations, workflows, data formats and agent logic wired around it, much as the cost of a railway lies in the track rather than the locomotive. For leaders, the practical advice follows from Arthur and from Albury. Decide early which layers should be standardised and portable, such as data formats, interfaces and evaluation tests, and treat those choices as strategic rather than technical. It is far cheaper to agree the gauge before the track is laid.

*Source: Stacklok survey, via Stack Overflow blog (Oct 21, 2026); Hooker, "The Hardware Lottery", Communications of the ACM (2021). ~20 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the horse, the rocket and the 36-hour switch** — a popular story claims that the Space Shuttle's solid rocket boosters were sized by the width of two Roman horses, because the boosters travelled by rail, railway gauge came from English tramways, and the tramways followed ancient Roman chariot ruts. It is a myth: the standard gauge came from the colliery wagonways of north-east England that George Stephenson knew, and the boosters were far wider than the track. The true story is better. In the American South, railways had been built to a five-foot gauge, cutting them off from the North. On May 31 and June 1, 1886, in about 36 hours, gangs of workers shifted the rails on roughly 13,000 miles of track to 4 feet 9 inches, close to the northern standard. As the economist Daniel Gross has [shown](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26261), the switch was coordinated by a railway cartel. Lock-in is often less a technical problem than a coordination problem. (Gross, Management Science, 2020)

**🃏 WILDCARD: not all roads led to Rome** — a new network analysis of the Roman Empire's roads, published in September in *Nature Communications* by Adam Pažout, Tom Brughmans and colleagues, finds that the most central route in the [nearly 300,000-kilometre network](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/researchers-have-created-a-digital-map-of-the-roman-empires-190000-mile-network-of-roads-turns-out-not-all-of-them-led-to-rome-180989509/) did not run through Rome at all, and that Constantinople was more central to the road system than the capital. The study also traces the roads' lasting impact. Some modern highways, such as Spain's N-340 along the old Via Augusta, still follow Roman routes, though in no region do Roman roads fully predict today's network, and the exact location of only 2.7% of the roads is known with certainty. Two thousand years on, the empire's choices still shape some of the paths we drive. (Nature Communications / Smithsonian, Sep 2026)

**Switching costs on trial** — on October 16 Britain's Competition and Markets Authority [proposed](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/microsofts-business-software-ecosystem) designating Microsoft as having "strategic market status" in business software, which would allow it to intervene in how the company bundles products such as Teams, Excel and its Copilot AI tools, and in licensing practices that make it costlier to run Microsoft software on rival clouds. The regulator found that Microsoft's strength rests partly on the barriers customers face when switching; Microsoft disputes its view. A final decision is due by February 2027. Separately, from January 12, 2027, the EU Data Act will bar cloud providers from charging customers for switching to a rival. Regulators are starting to treat switching costs as a policy choice. (CMA; EU Data Act)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

"Men make their own history," Karl Marx wrote in *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* in 1852, "but they do not make it as they please; they do not make it under self-selected circumstances, but under circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past. The tradition of all dead generations weighs like a nightmare on the brains of the living." One need not share Marx's politics to recognise the truth in the sentence. Every organisation, market and society inherits the gauges, keyboards, routines and assumptions of those who came before, and works within them whether it notices or not. The lesson of path dependence is double. It counsels humility about how free our choices really are, and it gives special weight to the moments when choices are still open: the founding of a company, the design of a system, the first standards of a new technology. Most of the time we inherit the track. Occasionally we get to lay it, and those are the decisions that deserve our most careful thought.
