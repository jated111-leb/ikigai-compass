# The Daily Compass #064 — Sediment

*Friday, October 2, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Institutions rarely die of a single blow. They silt up. Every failure leaves behind a new rule, every scandal a new review step, every crisis a new committee, and almost nothing is ever removed. Each layer made sense when it was laid down. Together they harden into something nobody designed and nobody can move: the agency that needs five years to issue a regulation, the company whose transformation program becomes one more layer of process, the leadership team so anxious about change that it defends the old order while announcing the new one. This issue is about that sediment, and about the less common moments when institutions manage to dig themselves out. Three recent essays look at the same problem at three scales (the state, the firm, and the minds of the people running them), and a forty-year-old book explains why the silt builds up in the first place.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The state that can't deliver

**What happened.** Jennifer Pahlka, who helped found the U.S. Digital Service and wrote *Recoding America*, and Andrew Greenway, a veteran of the UK's Government Digital Service, argue in [*Foreign Affairs*](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/democracies-need-creative-destruction-pahlka-greenway) that the most serious threat to democratic government is not polarization or disinformation. It is that the state increasingly cannot do the things it promises. Their diagnosis is accumulation. Each past failure produced procedures meant to prevent a repeat, and those procedures were almost never retired. The result, as they tell it, is agencies so encumbered that a major federal rule can take five years or more to put in place. Drawing on Anthony Downs's 1967 classic *Inside Bureaucracy*, they argue that institutions which stop adapting become shells of rules. The remedy they propose is uncomfortable for a reformer's instincts. Rather than endlessly repairing old agencies, governments should be willing to build new ones (Greenway calls them "departments of now") with fresh people, methods and technology, and to shut down programs that no longer work, the way a company retires a product.

**Why it matters.** The argument changes what "fixing government" means. The usual approach is to add something: another oversight office, another compliance requirement, another layer of review, each a reasonable response to a real failure. Pahlka and Greenway's point is that the additions are the problem, because they accumulate faster than anything is taken away, and the cost shows up as a democracy that cannot deliver within an electoral cycle. When voters elect people who promise change and then see nothing happen, they draw conclusions about democracy itself. The authors still credit steady improvement of existing agencies as part of statecraft. But their sharper claim is that renewal sometimes requires subtraction, starting fresh beside the old structure rather than inside it, and that this is a skill democracies have mostly forgotten how to use.

*Source: Pahlka & Greenway, Foreign Affairs, Sep/Oct 2026. ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. Transformation without a finish line

**What happened.** If government's problem is accumulation, corporate "transformation" often has the opposite problem: too much certainty about the destination. In *Harvard Business Review*, Evgeny Kaganer and Christoph Loch of IESE argue that [most transformations fail because they are run as fixed programs](https://hbr.org/2026/09/transformation-should-be-a-learning-journey), with a set end state, a timeline and financial targets, in conditions that change faster than the plan. They propose treating transformation as a learning journey in which strategy, priorities and even the goal itself get revised as the organization finds out what works. The vision still has to be ambitious and concrete. The authors sort the work into four kinds of initiative (pilots, options, improvements to established businesses, and new ventures) so that learning and delivery happen side by side. Their contrast is DBS Bank and GE. When DBS piloted a digital bank in India, it judged the pilot by customers acquired rather than revenue, because the point was to learn. GE, they argue, judged its transformation by whether the plan was on track.

**Why it matters.** The fixed-program model is attractive because it can be governed: milestones, dashboards and accountability all come easily. But it treats a voyage into unknown territory as if it were a construction project with known blueprints. When reality diverges from the plan, the organization either pretends it hasn't or declares failure. Kaganer and Loch's alternative is harder to manage, because it asks leaders to tolerate a moving target. It also fits the first brief: a transformation run as a rigid program tends to become one more layer of process, and that is how the sediment builds.

*Source: Kaganer & Loch, Harvard Business Review, Sep–Oct 2026. ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. The anxiety underneath

**What happened.** The third essay goes beneath structure and strategy to feeling. Declan Fitzsimons and Gianpiero and Jennifer Petriglieri, writing in [*MIT Sloan Management Review*](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/how-leadership-anxiety-derails-transformation/), report on a four-year study of a professional-services firm in the middle of a transformation, built on roughly 760 hours of observation and more than 300 interviews. Anxiety, they found, can start change: a sense of threat rallies people around the need to act. But when leaders do not acknowledge and contain that anxiety, it turns into what the authors call "defensive organizing." The change effort itself gets quietly put to use keeping anxiety at bay. Blame circulates, scapegoats are found, and the status quo survives under the banner of transformation, along with the leaders who were anxious about losing their place in it. The firm's chief executive told the researchers he felt "set up" when a three-year effort his own team had championed failed.

**Why it matters.** This is the explanation the first two briefs leave out. Pahlka and Greenway describe procedures piling up; Kaganer and Loch describe plans held too rigidly. Both raise the question of why intelligent people keep doing things that make their institutions worse. The Petriglieris' answer is that people are managing fear, not just executing plans, and that an organization's structures often serve to protect those inside it from discomfort. Rules accumulate partly because each one reassures someone. Fixed programs persist because certainty is soothing. Transformations become theater because real change threatens the people leading it. Their practical counsel is modest and hard: name the anxiety, share it, and hold it, rather than letting it drive the work underground.

*Source: Fitzsimons, Petriglieri & Petriglieri, MIT Sloan Management Review, Fall 2026. ~15 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: why the silt builds up** — the deepest explanation of institutional sediment is Mancur Olson's [*The Rise and Decline of Nations*](https://yalebooks.co.uk/book/9780300254068/the-rise-and-decline-of-nations/) (1982). Olson observed that small, focused groups organize far more easily than large, diffuse publics. So the longer a society stays stable, the more "distributional coalitions" it accumulates: trade associations, professional guilds, cartels, lobbies. These groups gain more from enlarging their share of the pie than from enlarging the pie. Over time they slow decisions, complicate regulation and resist new technology, producing what came to be called institutional sclerosis. His most provocative evidence was the fast postwar growth of West Germany and Japan, where defeat and occupation had swept away entrenched coalitions, set against Britain's slower growth. The uncomfortable implication is that renewal has often followed shocks that break up established interests. Pahlka and Greenway are, in effect, asking whether democracies can do on purpose what Olson thought usually took a catastrophe. ([A 25-year retrospective on Olson](https://heckeljc.sites.wfu.edu/papers/published/SEJ2007.pdf), Southern Economic Journal, 2007)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the bones that washed downhill** — for decades, archaeologists at Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey, the 11,000-year-old site of carved stone pillars often called the world's oldest temple, puzzled over hundreds of scattered human bone fragments with no graves to explain them. A study in [*PLOS One*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0358979) by Julia Gresky and Lee Clare of the German Archaeological Institute reports the site's first intact burials, dated to roughly 8700–8000 BC. One pit beneath the plaster floor of a rectangular building held at least three people, a woman over 35, a young man and a girl of about 12. The bones in the graves show the same wear as the loose fragments, which suggests the scattered remains washed down from burials higher up the mound in slope deposits. The burials also strengthen the case that people lived at Göbekli Tepe rather than visiting it only for ritual. The site's mystery, it turns out, was literally a matter of sediment. (PLOS One, Sep 22, 2026)

**The firm's boundary moves inward** — the week's best structural idea about AI and institutions comes from the economist Luis Garicano, in a paper presented at the [Brookings Papers on Economic Activity](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-vanishing-advantage-of-specialization/) conference. Specialist firms (law firms, consultancies, agencies) exist because they spread the fixed cost of acquiring expertise across many clients. AI sharply lowers that fixed cost, which helps most the organizations that need a given kind of knowledge only occasionally. Garicano expects firms to bring more expert work in-house with AI's help, leaving outside specialists with the hardest and rarest problems. He identifies six exposed occupations covering about 5.9 million U.S. workers, among them lawyers, accountants, software developers and management analysts. It is an argument about how institutions themselves get redrawn, not a headcount forecast. (BPEA conference draft, Sep 2026)

**The renewer renews itself** — a small irony behind brief 3. The *MIT Sloan Management Review* issue that carried the Petriglieris' essay is the magazine's last in print. MIT announced in May that it would end print publication after 67 years, a decision that drew sharp objections from some board members, and it plans to relaunch the review as a fully digital publication this winter. A journal of organizational change is going through one. (Poets&Quants; MIT Sloan, 2026)

**Statecraft — "How to Build a Policy School," with Will Howell** (Sep 29, 2026)
The audio-and-text companion to brief 1. Santi Ruiz talks with the founding dean of Johns Hopkins' new School of Government and Policy, who argues that the standard policy degree trains people to analyze institutions but not to repair them. His alternative puts project management, systems thinking and technology for process reform at the center. **Queue it** for a concrete answer to the question Pahlka and Greenway leave open: who will build the "departments of now"? ([Statecraft](https://www.statecraft.pub/p/how-to-build-a-policy-school))

**EconTalk — "Public Choice and Politics Without Romance," with Mike Munger** (Sep 28, 2026)
Olson's intellectual neighborhood, made conversational. Munger walks Russ Roberts through public choice theory, which treats politicians and bureaucrats as people with incentives rather than as selfless servants of the public good. Along the way: why voters rationally know so little, and his "Munger test" (before giving government a power, imagine it wielded by the politician you trust least). **Queue it** if Olson's argument intrigued you. It is familiar Munger ground, done well. ([EconTalk](https://www.econtalk.org/public-choice-and-politics-without-romance-with-mike-munger))

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1970, the economist Albert Hirschman published a short book with a long reach, [*Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674276604). His question was how declining organizations (firms, parties, states) get the signal that something is wrong. Members have two responses. They can exit, taking their custom or their talent elsewhere, or they can use voice, staying and pushing for change from inside. Loyalty matters because it slows exit long enough for voice to work. Decline goes uncorrected, Hirschman argued, when the balance is off: when the most demanding people leave quietly instead of complaining, or when complaint is ignored until there is no one left to make it. Read beside this week's essays, his framework suggests what sediment really costs. It is not only slowness. It is that the people best placed to renew an institution stop believing their voice can move anything, and they leave. The citizen gives up on the agency, the talented manager leaves the firm stuck in a transformation that never ends, the critic stops speaking up in a meeting run on defensive organizing. Renewal, on this reading, depends less on any single reform than on keeping voice worth using: removing enough sediment that effort can be seen to matter, and leading in a way that holds anxiety rather than spreading it. Olson thought it usually took a catastrophe to clear the channel. The more hopeful view in these essays is that institutions can sometimes do it themselves, if enough people inside them are willing to subtract.
