# The Daily Compass #105 — Stand and wait

*Saturday, November 14, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

The story goes like this. Some years ago, executives at an airport in Houston were flooded with complaints about how long passengers waited for their bags. So they did the obvious thing: they hired more baggage handlers, and cut the average wait to about eight minutes, well within industry norms. The complaints kept coming. Looking closer, they noticed that passengers walked for about a minute from their gate to baggage claim and then stood at the carousel for about seven. So the airport tried something counterintuitive. It moved arrivals to gates further from the main terminal and sent bags to the most distant carousel. Passengers now walked about six times as far, and spent most of their wait moving rather than standing. According to Alex Stone, who told the story in [*The New York Times*](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-waiting-is-torture.html) in 2012, complaints "dropped to near zero." The airport has never been named and the story is often retold, so it is best treated as a parable. But it captures something that research has confirmed many times: how long we wait matters much less than how the wait feels. This issue is about waiting: the psychology of queues, the hidden queues inside organisations, and what happens when AI changes how long we wait, and how we feel about it.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Designing the wait

**What happened.** In 1985 the management consultant David Maister published a short essay, "The Psychology of Waiting Lines", that has shaped service design ever since. Among his principles: occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time; waits before a service begins feel longer than waits during it; anxiety, uncertainty and unexplained delays all make waits feel longer; unfair waits feel longer than fair ones; and people will wait longer for something they value. Two years later, in ["Perspectives on Queues"](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.35.6.895) (*Operations Research*, 1987), the MIT queueing expert Richard Larson argued that fairness often matters more than duration: people are far more upset by being overtaken than by waiting a little longer, which is why a single, snaking line often feels better than several faster ones. Later research found that showing people the work being done can make a wait feel worthwhile. In ["The Labor Illusion"](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376) (*Management Science*, 2011), Ryan Buell and Michael Norton found that people sometimes preferred a travel website that made them wait while displaying the airlines it was searching to one that delivered the same results instantly. In a field experiment in a university dining hall ([*Management Science*, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2411)), Buell, Tami Kim and Chia-Jung Tsay let cooks and diners see each other through video screens. Diners rated the food 22% higher, and the time to serve them fell by 19%, because cooks who could see their customers worked harder. And a new study by Yuqian Luo, Leonardo Valdés and Sera Linardi ([*Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1287/msom.2022.0352)) found that people judge a queue by what still lies ahead of them, not by how long they have already waited.

**Why it matters.** The arithmetic of a queue is unforgiving: in John Little's famous formula, the number of people waiting equals the rate at which they arrive multiplied by how long each one waits. Reducing waits usually means adding capacity, which is expensive. But how a wait feels can often be redesigned cheaply. Keep people occupied. Tell them how long it will take and why. Make the order visibly fair. Show the work being done on their behalf, which can make both customers and staff value it more. And because people judge by what is ahead, focus on showing progress and remaining steps rather than apologising for time already spent. The lesson applies well beyond airports and restaurants: to customer service, onboarding, approvals, hiring processes and product launches. Every organisation makes people wait. The question is whether it designs the wait or leaves it to chance.

*Source: Maister (1985); Larson, Operations Research 35(6) (1987); Little, Operations Research 9(3) (1961); Buell & Norton, Management Science 57(9) (2011); Buell, Kim & Tsay, Management Science 63(6) (2017); Luo, Valdés & Linardi, MSOM (2025). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. The queues inside the company

**What happened.** Organisations are full of queues that nobody sees: work waiting for a decision, a review, an approval or a reply. In *The Principles of Product Development Flow* (2009), the engineer and consultant Donald Reinertsen argued that these invisible queues are a root cause of slow and expensive product development, because unlike a line of customers, a pile of half-finished work does not complain. A recent consultancy survey suggests executives feel the cost. In January, the advisory firm West Monroe [reported](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/slow-decisions-are-costing-companies-millions-in-lost-revenue-new-west-monroe-research-finds-302670875.html) that 73% of the senior leaders it surveyed believed slow decisions and delayed execution cost their companies up to 5% of annual revenue; managers blamed layers of approval and unclear decision rights. Some companies have attacked these queues directly. At Meta, engineers studying code review found that most developers were satisfied with how long reviews took; the dissatisfaction came from the slowest quarter of reviews, which could wait more than a day. So the company built a tool that [nudged reviewers](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/11/16/culture/meta-code-review-time-improving/) about changes that had gone stale. In a trial, the average time changes spent waiting for review fell by about 7%, with no sign that reviewers were simply rubber-stamping to clear the queue. Waiting can also be a growth strategy. When the trading app Robinhood launched in December 2014, it had [500,000 people on its waitlist](https://techcrunch.com/2014/12/11/robinhood-free-stock-trading/), who could move up the queue by referring friends. Research suggests why this can work: in ["Worth Waiting For"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-010-0222-5) (*Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, 2011), Michael Giebelhausen and colleagues found that a required wait can increase satisfaction when quality is hard to judge in advance, because people infer that something worth queueing for must be good.

**Why it matters.** Most leaders measure how busy their people are, not how long work waits. Yet the waiting is often where the time goes. A few practical lessons follow. Make queues visible: count the decisions, reviews and approvals waiting, and how long they have waited. Focus on the slowest cases, not the average, as Meta did; the tail is where frustration and delay concentrate. Clarify who can decide what, so that work does not wait for permission nobody needs to give. And when you must make customers wait, as with a launch or a scarce product, design that wait deliberately: explain it, show progress, and make it feel fair. A queue that people understand can build anticipation; one they do not understand only builds resentment.

*Source: Reinertsen (2009); West Monroe, "Speed Wins" (Jan 2026; consultancy survey); Shan et al., ESEC/FSE (2022); TechCrunch (Dec 2014); Giebelhausen, Robinson & Cronin, JAMS 39(6) (2011). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Should AI make us wait?

**What happened.** AI promises to eliminate waiting, but the research on how people experience its speed is surprising. In a study presented at the CHI conference in April ([arXiv, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)), Felicia Fang-Yi Tan, Oded Nov and colleagues at NYU had 240 people work on everyday tasks with a chatbot that was set to answer after 2, 9 or 20 seconds. The answers were identical. Yet people [rated the two-second answers](https://engineering.nyu.edu/news/why-faster-ai-isnt-always-better) as less thoughtful than the slower ones, with perceived usefulness peaking at around nine seconds. The researchers warn that people may "place undue trust in slower systems". A companion study by Tan and Nov ([arXiv, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04138)) found that when computer systems made 425 people wait, a countdown of remaining time caused more frustration than a display of elapsed time, while showing nothing at all made waits feel longer. Meanwhile, the effect of AI on real queues can be counterintuitive. In a field experiment in Alibaba's customer service operation ([arXiv, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29888)), Xiao Ni, Yiwei Wang and colleagues gave chat agents an AI assistant that helped diagnose problems. Each conversation got shorter, but agents then took on more chats at once, so customers waited longer between replies and more of them abandoned the conversation. Where AI handles the front of the queue, results can be better. In July, NHS England [reported](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2026/07/nhs-accelerates-artificial-intelligence-rollout-to-cut-waiting-times-and-improve-care-for-millions/) that an AI triage tool in its app had reduced the number of patients queueing on the phone for a GP practice by 29% in a pilot, and plans to roll it out nationally by 2028.

**Why it matters.** The obvious goal for AI is to make everything faster. The research suggests that speed alone is the wrong target. People judge AI, like any service, partly by the effort they believe went into it, which creates a temptation that companies should resist: adding artificial delays or fake "thinking" to seem more thoughtful. A better approach is honest design. Show real progress and real reasoning where they exist. Tell people what is happening and what is left, rather than counting down a timer. And measure the whole system, not just the step AI speeds up. The Alibaba study is a warning for any leader deploying AI in operations: a faster agent who handles more work at once can leave every customer waiting longer. The aim is not the shortest possible wait, but the right wait, honestly explained, for an answer worth having.

*Source: Tan, Messerschmidt, Yin & Nov, CHI 2026 (arXiv 2604.06183); Tan & Nov, CHI 2026 (arXiv 2602.04138); Ni, Wang et al., arXiv 2603.29888 (2026); NHS England (Jul 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Erlang counts the callers** — in 1908 the Copenhagen Telephone Company hired a young Danish mathematician named Agner Krarup Erlang to run its new laboratory. The company had a practical problem: how many lines and operators did it need so that callers would not wait too long, without paying for capacity that sat idle? In a [paper published in 1909](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Erlang/), Erlang showed that telephone calls arrived randomly in a pattern that could be described mathematically, and in later work he derived formulas for how long callers would wait given a certain number of lines. It was the beginning of queueing theory, which now governs everything from call centres to computer networks and hospital emergency departments. In 1946 the international telephone standards body named the unit of telephone traffic the "erlang" in his honour. (Erlang, 1909; MacTutor)

**🃏 WILDCARD: how cicadas count to seventeen** — periodical cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground before emerging in their billions, and nobody knew how they kept time. In a clever experiment ([*Ecology Letters*, 2000](https://www.discovermagazine.com/tree-sucking-clock-watchers-14666)), Richard Karban and colleagues moved 15-year-old cicada nymphs onto the roots of peach trees that had been manipulated to flower twice a year. The cicadas emerged a year early. They were not counting days, it seems, but seasons: the yearly rhythm of the tree whose roots they fed on. Even nature's most patient waiters measure time by what changes around them. (Ecology Letters, 2000)

**The vanishing wait** — on November 5, Walmart [announced](https://www.supermarketnews.com/grocery-technology/walmart-zipline-reach-agreement-to-add-300-drone-delivery-sites) a multiyear deal with Zipline to add 300 drone-delivery sites across the United States, after more than 2 million drone deliveries. Earlier this year, Walmart said its drone deliveries took an average of about 23 minutes from order to doorstep. The packages are small and the regulations are still catching up, but for some purchases, the wait is shrinking from days to minutes, and with it, the chance to anticipate anything at all. (Walmart and Zipline, Nov 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In the early 1650s the poet John Milton, then in his forties and serving as a senior official in England's government, lost his sight completely. In a sonnet usually known by its first line, ["When I consider how my light is spent"](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44750/sonnet-19-when-i-consider-how-my-light-is-spent), he wrestled with what it meant to be unable to do the work he believed he was meant to do. The poem ends with an answer from Patience: "His state / Is Kingly. Thousands at his bidding speed / And post o'er Land and Ocean without rest: / They also serve who only stand and wait." Milton was writing about faith, not queues. But his last line has outlived its context because it names something every organisation forgets. Waiting is not always wasted time. Sometimes it is preparation, attention or the space in which something worth having takes shape. The task is to tell the difference: to cut the waits that serve no one, and to honour the ones that do.
