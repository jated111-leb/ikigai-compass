# The Daily Compass #065 — You are embarked

*Saturday, October 3, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Most of the decisions that matter are made without the information we would like to have. Which job to take, whether to start the company, when to raise rates, whether to shut down the project that has eaten two years: none of these comes with a probability attached. We still talk about them as if they did, borrowing the language of odds and expected value because it feels rigorous. In 1921 the economist Frank Knight drew a line that helps here. On one side is *risk*, where the odds can be known, priced and insured. On the other is *uncertainty*, where they cannot, because the situation has no real precedent. This issue is about life on the second side of that line. It looks at what happened when the world's best forecasters tried to predict a technology that kept rewriting its own base rates, at why a central banker has started arguing for robust rules over clever models, and at how a machine built to reveal the future made it murkier. The common thread is a question we don't ask often enough: when you cannot know, how should you choose?

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Even the superforecasters missed it

**What happened.** Since 2022 the Forecasting Research Institute, the organization Philip Tetlock co-founded to study prediction, has been asking panels of AI experts and "superforecasters" (people with proven records of beating the crowd) how quickly AI would advance. Its new report, ["How Accurate Have AI Progress Forecasts Been So Far?"](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-progress-forecasts-accuracy), now that many of those questions have resolved, is humbling. Both groups dramatically underestimated progress on AI benchmarks, and the pattern held from forecasts made in 2022 through forecasts gathered as recently as this spring. The starkest case is the International Mathematical Olympiad. AI reached gold-medal level in July 2025, five years before the experts' median guess and ten years before the superforecasters'. Beforehand, the superforecasters gave that outcome a 2.3% chance and the experts 8.6%. Across four benchmarks, the superforecasters put an average probability of under 10% on what actually happened. The experts, less disciplined but closer to the field, did better, at about 25%.

**Why it matters.** Superforecasters are good for a reason. They anchor on base rates, adjust slowly, and distrust dramatic stories. That habit beats intuition in most domains, because most of the world changes slowly. It failed here because AI progress was a Knightian situation dressed up as a risky one: the process producing the outcomes was itself accelerating, so the historical base rates the forecasters leaned on were measuring the wrong world. This does not mean forecasting is useless. Averaging many forecasts still beat individuals, and the institute's own work suggests AI forecasting systems are now roughly matching the best humans on general questions. It means something more specific. Careful, well-calibrated judgment can fail all in one direction when the ground is moving, and the people best trained to resist hype can be the slowest to see a real discontinuity. When a question is about a process that is changing its own rules, the honest move is to widen your error bars, not to trust your base rate.

*Source: Forecasting Research Institute (Sep 22, 2026). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. Rules that survive being wrong

**What happened.** If precise prediction fails under real uncertainty, what should someone responsible for big decisions do instead? Huw Pill, the Bank of England's chief economist, offered an answer this year in a speech titled simply ["Robustness."](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2026/march/huw-pill-speech-at-national-bank-of-the-republic-of-north-macedonia-and-suerf-conference) Looking back on five years on the Monetary Policy Committee through a pandemic, wars, energy shocks and market turmoil, he argued that central banks should stop optimizing policy against a single best-guess model of the economy. Instead they should look for policies that deliver acceptable results across a wide range of possible worlds, and use scenario analysis to find them. The idea has deep roots. In *Radical Uncertainty* (2020), the economist John Kay and the former Bank of England governor Mervyn King argued that the first question in a genuinely uncertain situation is not "what are the probabilities?" but "what is going on here?" The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer has spent decades showing that simple rules of thumb, which deliberately ignore information, often [outperform complex models](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-120709-145346) when the future is unstable, because they have fewer parameters to get wrong.

**Why it matters.** This is a quiet but important reversal of how expertise usually presents itself. The prestige of a model comes from its precision, and its weakness is that it is precisely wrong when the world drifts away from its assumptions. A robust rule gives up the best possible outcome in the world you expect in exchange for an acceptable outcome in worlds you did not. That trade looks timid in calm periods and wise after every shock. It applies well beyond central banking. A company planning for a single forecast of demand, a person planning a career around one prediction about AI, a government budgeting on one growth path are all optimizing for a world that may not arrive. The question Pill, Kay and King, and Gigerenzer push you to ask is different: not "what will happen?" but "what can I do that will still look sensible if I am wrong about what happens?"

*Source: Pill, Bank of England (Mar 24, 2026); Kay & King, Radical Uncertainty (2020); Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, Annual Review of Psychology (2011). ~25 min for the speech.*

### ◆ 3. The market that made the future murkier

**What happened.** One answer to uncertainty has been to let markets price it. Prediction markets, where people bet on future events, were supposed to gather scattered private knowledge into a single, honest public number, a modern application of Friedrich Hayek's point that prices can carry information no planner has. In a long essay for *Harper's*, ["The Chaos Machine,"](https://harpers.org/archive/2026/09/the-chaos-machine-finn-brunton-crypto-anarchism/) the historian of technology Finn Brunton reports on what that dream has produced now that markets like Polymarket have grown huge. He recounts a $32,000 bet on the U.S. ousting of Nicolás Maduro, placed hours before the announcement, that paid around $400,000. He describes winnings on the timing of a head of state's death, and accounts that hide insider knowledge behind multiple anonymous crypto wallets. His conclusion is that the dream of an honest consensus "now works as a kind of acid," dissolving the capacity for coordination and turning the markets into "a machine for generating chaos."

**Why it matters.** Brunton's essay is a sharp warning about a fashionable idea. Prediction markets can be informative, and economists like Robin Hanson have made a strong case for them. But a market only aggregates knowledge honestly if the people trading are mostly guessing. Once insiders can trade on what they already know, and once the size of the bets gives people a reason to influence outcomes rather than merely forecast them, the price stops reflecting the world and starts reflecting who has privileged access to it. For the rest of us, the lesson connects to the other briefs. A number that looks like a probability is not automatically one. Before acting on a forecast, whether it comes from a market, a model or an expert, ask what process produced it and who had reasons to bend it.

*Source: Brunton, Harper's (September 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: risk is not uncertainty** — the distinction behind this whole issue comes from Frank Knight's [*Risk, Uncertainty and Profit*](https://www.econlib.org/library/Knight/knRUP.html) (1921). Knight argued that a "measurable uncertainty," the kind you can calculate from repeated cases, as an insurer does with house fires, is so different from an unmeasurable one that it is not really uncertainty at all. True uncertainty arises with situations that are new, where there is no meaningful history of similar cases to count. His further claim was the bold one: entrepreneurial profit is precisely the reward for acting where calculation is impossible. If the odds could be computed, competition would price the opportunity away. What is left to be paid for is judgment. A century later it remains the clearest reason to distrust anyone who puts a confident probability on a genuinely unprecedented event, including, as brief 1 shows, the best forecasters in the world. (Knight, 1921)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the stump that refused** — three years ago this week, on September 28, 2023, two men cut down the Sycamore Gap tree, the much-loved lone sycamore in a dip of Hadrian's Wall in Northumberland, in an act of vandalism that drew grief across Britain. They were later convicted. Now the National Trust has released photographs showing [a thick ring of shoots](https://www.itv.com/news/tyne-tees/2026-09-28/sycamore-gap-stump-shows-ring-of-shoots-three-years-on-from-felling), two to three feet tall, around the stump. Staff loosened the compacted soil around the roots and kept people away, and the tree did the rest. If the shoots are left alone, they may grow into new trees around the old one. Nobody could have put a confident probability on that outcome in 2023. The stump simply kept going. (ITV / National Trust, Sep 28, 2026)

**Decide in advance when to quit** — the most practical tool in this territory comes from the former professional poker player Annie Duke, whose book [*Quit*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/674603/quit-by-annie-duke/) (2022) argues that people persist far too long with failing jobs, projects and investments, because sunk costs and identity make walking away feel like losing. Her remedy is "kill criteria": before you start, write down a specific state and a date that will trigger an exit (if we have not reached this many customers by March, we stop). Deciding while you are still clear-headed protects you from the reasoning you will be tempted by later. It is Pill's robustness argument applied to your own commitments. (Duke, 2022)

**The machine that won't quit** — the one AI item this week, from someone who forecasts for a living. Nate Silver, writing at [Silver Bulletin](https://www.natesilver.net/p/were-not-ready-for-superpersistent), argues that the most underrated property of AI systems is not intelligence but *persistence*: a system that is slow but never tires, never gives up and cannot be deterred. He illustrates it with the internet's "immortal snail," and grounds it in his own work. AI tools now do much of the coding behind his election and sports models, he reports, yet the judgment about which model to build, and when its output is nonsense, is still his. It is a useful division of labor to notice: in a world of tireless machines, the scarce human contribution is deciding what is worth pursuing. (Silver Bulletin, Sep 15, 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In the 1650s, Blaise Pascal sketched the most famous decision under uncertainty in Western philosophy. Reason, he argued, cannot settle whether God exists, so you cannot calculate your way to the answer. But you also cannot decline to choose. [*Il faut parier*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-h/18269-h.htm): you must wager, it is not optional, *vous êtes embarqué*, you are already embarked. Whatever one makes of his conclusion, his framing has outlasted his theology because it describes ordinary life so well. Not deciding is itself a decision, usually in favor of whatever was already happening. The forecasters in brief 1 were embarked on a technology they could not model. The central banker in brief 2 is embarked on an economy that will not hold still for his equations. Everyone who bets on a prediction market is embarked, too, often against people who know more. The temptation in each case is to wait for certainty that will not arrive, or to borrow false precision from a model, a market or an expert. Knight's lesson, and Pascal's, is that judgment is what remains when calculation runs out, and that it cannot be delegated away. The practical form is modest: notice when you are facing uncertainty rather than risk, choose options that survive being wrong, decide in advance when you will quit, and then commit. The boat has left the shore. The question is only how well you steer it.
