# The Daily Compass #076 — Who keeps it running

*Friday, October 16, 2026 · ~14 minute read*

Every twenty years, at the Ise Grand Shrine in Japan, the most sacred buildings of the Shinto faith are taken down and rebuilt from fresh cypress on an adjacent plot, exactly as before. The ritual, called the Shikinen Sengu, is said to go back some thirteen centuries; the 62nd rebuilding was completed in 2013, and preparations for the 63rd, due in 2033, are already under way. Nobody fully agrees on why the cycle is twenty years. One appealing answer is that twenty years is roughly the span over which the carpenters' skills can be handed from one generation to the next. The shrine is new and ancient at the same time, because the thing being preserved is not the wood but the knowledge of how to make it. It is a fitting image for this issue, which is about the least glamorous work in any economy: maintenance. Why do societies celebrate invention and neglect upkeep, why do organisations fall into a trap that rewards cutting corners, and what is AI-written software doing to the maintenance bill?

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Hail the maintainers

**What happened.** In 2016 the historians of technology Andrew Russell and Lee Vinsel published an essay in *Aeon* with a deliberately provocative title, ["Hail the Maintainers"](https://aeon.co/essays/innovation-is-overvalued-maintenance-often-matters-more). Their argument was that modern societies have become obsessed with innovation, in business schools, in government strategy and in the language of Silicon Valley, while the work that actually keeps life going, maintaining roads, pipes, machines, buildings and software, is underfunded and undervalued. Most human labour, they pointed out, is spent not on creating new things but on keeping existing ones running. The essay grew into a movement, a network of scholars and practitioners called The Maintainers, and in 2020 into a book, *The Innovation Delusion*, which argued that the cult of the new has led governments and companies to defer repairs, neglect the workers who do them and celebrate disruption as an end in itself.

**Why it matters.** Russell and Vinsel's point is not that innovation is bad, but that it is only the first and shortest part of a technology's life. A bridge, a power plant or a software platform is built once and maintained for decades, and most of its value, and most of its cost, comes in those decades. Organisations that budget for the launch but not for the upkeep are borrowing against the future. The practical questions they suggest are simple and uncomfortable. Who in your organisation is responsible for keeping things running, and are they paid and promoted like the people who build new things? What is the maintenance budget as a share of the build budget, and is anyone tracking it? For anyone in a fast-growing company or a country rebuilding its infrastructure, the lesson is that the ribbon-cutting is the cheap part.

*Source: Russell & Vinsel, Aeon (2016); Vinsel & Russell, The Innovation Delusion (2020). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. The capability trap

**What happened.** Why do organisations under-invest in maintenance even when everyone knows they should? Nelson Repenning and John Sterman of MIT gave one of the best answers in a paper with a memorable title: ["Nobody Ever Gets Credit for Fixing Problems That Never Happened"](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2001/08/43-4-nobody-ever-gets-credit-for-fixing-problems-that-never-happened-creating-and-sustaining-process-improvement) (*California Management Review*, 2001). Studying why process-improvement programmes so often fail, they found the problem was rarely the technique. It was the way managers responded to pressure. When output falls short, there are two ways to close the gap: work harder, or work smarter by investing in the underlying capability, through maintenance, training and better processes. Working harder pays off immediately. Investing in capability pays off only later, and in the meantime it takes time away from production. So under pressure, people cut corners on upkeep. Capability then slowly erodes, problems multiply, pressure rises, and even more time goes into firefighting. Repenning and Sterman called this self-reinforcing cycle the capability trap.

**Why it matters.** The trap is hard to see from inside because the delay hides the cause. A manager who skips maintenance this quarter looks productive; the breakdowns arrive months later and are blamed on bad luck or bad workers. And successful prevention is invisible by definition, which is the point of the paper's title. The authors' remedy is mostly a change of mental model: recognise that today's firefighting is often the result of yesterday's deferred maintenance, protect time for improvement even when things are busy, and judge teams partly by the problems that did not happen. Anyone who has worked in a growing company will recognise the pattern, and so will anyone who has lived through a power grid that was run hard and repaired late.

*Source: Repenning & Sterman, California Management Review 43(4) (2001). ~45 min (paper).*

### ◆ 3. AI writes the code, someone else maintains it

**What happened.** AI coding assistants are now writing a large share of new software, and researchers have started to ask what happens to that code after it is written. The most ambitious study so far, ["Debt Behind the AI Boom"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28592) by Yue Liu, David Lo and colleagues (arXiv, 2026), traced roughly 300,000 verified AI-authored commits across more than 6,000 GitHub repositories and five widely used coding assistants, checking each for problems with standard code-analysis tools. Every assistant introduced at least one issue in more than 15% of its commits. The vast majority of issues, close to 90%, were "code smells", design and readability problems that make software harder to change rather than bugs that break it immediately. And about 22.7% of the issues tracked were still present in the latest version of the repository, meaning nobody had cleaned them up. A separate study presented at ICML this year, ["Do AI Agents Write Less Maintainable Code Than Human Developers?"](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/67974), found that code written by AI agents led to more failures when later tasks had to build on it than code written by humans.

**Why it matters.** None of this means that AI-written code is worse than human code in every case, and the studies measure quality with automated tools that miss a great deal. But they point to a familiar pattern from the first two briefs. AI dramatically lowers the cost of producing new code, the innovation side of the ledger, while the cost of maintaining that code stays with people and may grow. A team that measures productivity by features shipped will look more productive with AI; a team that also tracks how much time goes to understanding, fixing and changing old code may tell a different story. The lesson for any organisation adopting these tools is to budget for the maintenance at the same time as celebrating the speed, and to give someone the job of cleaning up.

*Source: Liu, Widyasari, Zhao, Irsan, Chen & Lo, arXiv 2603.28592 (2026); ICML 2026. ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the shrine that is always new** — the [Shikinen Sengu](https://en.ise-kanko.jp/shikinen-sengu-63/) at Ise is not one ceremony but a cycle of rituals lasting about nine years, which rebuilds not only the main halls of the inner and outer shrines but also the sacred treasures and costumes kept inside them. Preparations for the 63rd rebuilding, due in 2033, formally began with a ceremony in the mountains where the timber is sourced, and the public timber-hauling festivals begin in earnest this year. It may be the world's longest-running maintenance programme, and a reminder that some institutions survive precisely because they plan for decay rather than pretending it will not happen. (Ise tourism sources)

**🃏 WILDCARD: a wreck from the age of the first cities** — off Kekova island on Turkey's southern coast, underwater archaeologists from Akdeniz University have been studying a wreck carrying Early Bronze Age storage jars, similar in shape to types made in the Levant. A [preliminary report](https://avesis.akdeniz.edu.tr/yayin/a64debbb-dc64-471b-9d6f-740ca395b19b/preliminary-report-on-the-early-bronze-age-shipwreck-in-the-kekova-region-of-antalya) appeared this year in the *Journal of Maritime Archaeology*, and news reports this week describe the wreck as nearly 5,000 years old and possibly the oldest shipwreck yet found. That superlative should wait for the full excavation and dating, but even the cautious version is remarkable: a cargo ship moving goods across the eastern Mediterranean before the great pyramids were built. (Journal of Maritime Archaeology; ABC News, Oct 2026)

**Maintenance is invisible until it isn't** — the artist Mierle Laderman Ukeles made the point half a century before the software industry did. Her 1969 ["Manifesto for Maintenance Art"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mierle_Laderman_Ukeles), written as a proposal for an exhibition called "CARE", divided human activity into two systems: development, which gets the glory, and maintenance, which keeps development alive. In 1979–80 she spent nearly a year visiting every district of New York's Department of Sanitation to shake the hand of each of its roughly 8,500 workers who would accept, thanking them for keeping the city alive. (Wikipedia; Lilith)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Ukeles opened her manifesto with a question that still stings: after the revolution, who is going to pick up the garbage on Monday morning? It is a question every reformer, founder and planner should keep pinned above the desk. The excitement of any project lives in the opening, the launch, the new building, the first version of the product. But the life of the thing, and most of its meaning for the people who use it, comes afterwards, in the long middle that nobody photographs: the water that runs, the lights that stay on, the code that still works on Monday. The carpenters at Ise understood this. They did not try to build a shrine that would never decay; they built a way of renewing it, and then they trusted the next generation to keep doing it. Maintenance is often described as the opposite of progress. It may be closer to the truth to say that it is what progress turns into if it is lucky enough to last.
