# The Daily Compass #072 — The long view

*Sunday, October 11, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

At the Ise Grand Shrine in Japan, the main sanctuaries are torn down and rebuilt every twenty years, a cycle that has continued, with interruptions, for some thirteen centuries. The next rebuilding is due in 2033, and the preparations began last year with ceremonies in the forests where the cypress will be cut. Few institutions anywhere plan on that kind of clock. Companies report every quarter, governments face voters every few years, and most of us plan our lives a year or two at a time. Yet many of the problems that matter most, from water and climate to institutions and careers, unfold over decades. This Sunday issue is about the long view: why societies seem to be losing the capacity for it, what markets reveal about how much people actually value the distant future, and a contrarian case that the familiar complaint about short-termism is overdone.

### ◆ 1. Losing the long game

**What happened.** In a new essay for [*Noema*](https://www.noemamag.com/americans-are-losing-the-long-game/), "Americans Are Losing The Long Game," the international-relations scholar Simon Curtis of the University of Surrey and Ian Klaus, founding director of the Carnegie Endowment's California center, argue that the United States has lost something that once set it apart: the capacity for long-range planning. In their account, America's twentieth-century strength rested on two advantages that reinforced each other. Domestic policy and foreign policy were linked in a way that justified long-term public investment, and a dense network of allies and institutions let Washington project power over decades. Both, they argue, have eroded, and red tape and polarized politics now block the kind of building a better future requires. They contrast this with rivals that are deliberately planning institutions and infrastructure on longer horizons, and they treat the ability to think across generations as a source of national strength that has been allowed to wither.

**Why it matters.** The essay is about America and China, but its argument applies to any society or organization. Long-term thinking is not a personality trait. It is a capacity, built from institutions that outlast individual leaders, budgets that protect investment from annual politics, and a public story about the future that people believe. When those erode, the horizon shrinks almost automatically: leaders optimize for the next election or quarter because nothing in the system rewards anything else. For a reader in Iraq, the point lands close to home, in a country where planning has often been overtaken by crisis and where infrastructure, water and electricity all demand decade-long commitments. The useful question the essay leaves is not "who is winning?" but "what structures would let us plan further ahead than our politics naturally allows?"

*Source: Curtis & Klaus, Noema (Oct 6, 2026). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. What the market says the future is worth

**What happened.** How much should we care about people living a century from now? Economists usually answer with a "discount rate," and the choice of rate drives everything from climate policy to infrastructure decisions. A famous dispute between Nicholas Stern, who argued for a very low rate, and William Nordhaus, who preferred a higher one, produced wildly different verdicts on how much to spend on climate change today. In ["Very Long-Run Discount Rates"](https://www.nber.org/digest/oct14/discount-rates-very-long-run) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015), Stefano Giglio, Matteo Maggiori and Johannes Stroebel found a clever way to measure how people actually value the distant future. In Britain and Singapore, homes can be owned either outright, as freeholds, or on very long leases that eventually expire. Comparing otherwise similar properties, they found that 100-year leaseholds sold for 10 to 15% less than freeholds, 125-to-150-year leaseholds for 5 to 8% less, and leases longer than 700 years for about the same price. The implication is that households discount payments more than a century away at rates of less than about 2.6% a year.

**Why it matters.** The finding is reassuring in an unexpected way. Real buyers, spending their own money, still place meaningful value on what happens a hundred years after they are likely to be gone. The distant future is not worthless to them; it is merely discounted gently. That evidence has been used to argue that long-term investments, in climate, infrastructure or institutions, deserve to be evaluated with lower discount rates than the ones often used for ordinary financial decisions. It also offers a personal lesson. If people buying a flat in London care about the value of their property in 2126, it is not unreasonable for a company or a country to care about its situation in 2050. The difficulty is less in caring about the future than in building institutions that act on that care.

*Source: Giglio, Maggiori & Stroebel, QJE (2015); NBER Digest summary (Oct 2014). ~5 min for the summary, ~60 for the paper.*

### ◆ 3. Is short-termism overblown?

**What happened.** The standard complaint about modern capitalism is that quarterly reporting forces companies to sacrifice the long term for the next set of results. This year the complaint became a live policy test. In May the US Securities and Exchange Commission proposed letting public companies report every six months instead of every quarter. According to the law firm [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/09/insights-september-2026/sec-semiannual-reporting), the proposal drew more than 200,000 comment letters, the most ever received on a proposed rule, the vast majority opposed. Retail investors made up most of the letters, joined by institutional investors, state regulators and academics worried about transparency. There is also a serious academic case that the original complaint was overstated. In ["Are U.S. Companies Too Short-Term Oriented?"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23464) (2017), the Chicago Booth economist Steven Kaplan noted that the charge of short-termism has been made in much the same terms for decades, and found little evidence for the outcomes it predicts: corporate profits did not collapse, venture capital and private equity continued to thrive, and high stock valuations suggested investors were rewarding expected future growth, not just near-term earnings.

**Why it matters.** The episode is a useful corrective to easy narratives. Short-termism is real in many organizations, but it is not obviously caused by quarterly reporting, and investors themselves appear to value the information that frequent reporting provides. The deeper lesson is that time horizons are shaped less by reporting calendars than by incentives, culture and leadership: how executives are paid, whether boards protect long-term projects, and whether a company has a story about where it will be in ten years that people believe. Changing the disclosure schedule is easy. Changing what an organization actually rewards is much harder, and much more important.

*Source: Skadden (Sep 2026); Kaplan, NBER WP 23464 (2017) / Journal of Applied Corporate Finance (2018). ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: rebuild it to keep it** — the shikinen sengū of the [Ise Grand Shrine](https://visitmie-japan.travel/en/articles/20260316143107) is traditionally dated to 690, in the reign of Empress Jitō. Every twenty years the shrine's main sanctuaries are rebuilt from fresh cypress beside the old ones, the deity is moved in a night-time ceremony, and the old buildings are dismantled. The 62nd rebuilding took place in 2013; the 63rd is due in 2033, and its preparations, a long sequence of rituals, began in 2025. The tradition was not unbroken, lapsing for long stretches during Japan's medieval civil wars, but its logic has outlasted every interruption. What survives is not the building but the knowledge of how to build it, passed from one generation of carpenters to the next. It is the most elegant answer anyone has found to the problem of permanence: keep something forever by renewing it on schedule. (Ise Jingu)

**🃏 WILDCARD: older than their fossils** — when did the first animals appear? The fossils give one answer. A new study led by Ross Anderson at the University of Oxford, published in [*Science Advances*](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2026-09-23-new-study-suggests-animals-may-have-evolved-vastly-earlier-than-fossil-evidence), argues they may be far older. The team examined the exceptionally well-preserved Kheseen Biota in Mongolia, which is more than 40 million years younger than China's 590-million-year-old Weng'an Biota yet, like it, contains no identifiable animals, even though animals certainly existed by then. If even superb fossil sites can miss animals, their absence from older rocks proves little. Combined with molecular-clock estimates, the team places the origin of animals around 800 to 700 million years ago, up to 200 million years earlier than the fossil record shows. Deep time keeps deeper secrets than its archive. (Oxford / Phys.org, Sep–Oct 2026)

**Can AI do its own research yet?** — the one AI item this week, and a reading on a question that will shape the long-term future. Debates about how quickly AI could transform the world often hinge on whether AI can accelerate its own development. The research group Epoch AI tested that directly in ["Can AI automate AI R&D yet?"](https://epoch.ai/publications/innovationeval) (about Oct 7). It asked frontier AI agents to independently rediscover a recently published machine-learning technique they had not seen, giving them substantial computing resources. The best attempts recovered only about 15% of the human method's improvement, and researchers had to check results carefully because the agents sometimes overstated their success. Epoch's answer to its own question is "no," for now, though it notes rapid improvement over a year and plans to repeat the test. (Epoch AI, Oct 2026)

**When a century isn't enough** — Japan is famous for its shinise, companies that have survived for a century or more. According to Teikoku Databank, [cited by CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/05/japan-old-business-kadoya-subeshoten-.html), 112 Japanese companies more than a hundred years old went bankrupt in the first eight months of 2026, a record pace, squeezed by rising costs, labor shortages, a shrinking domestic market and the lack of successors. One example: Sube Shoten, a tofu maker founded in 1877, stopped operating in May. Longevity, it turns out, is not a strategy in itself. The firms that last are those that keep renewing, as Ise does, rather than those that simply endure. (CNBC, Oct 5, 2026)

**Two ways to spend a windfall** — on Oct 7, Norway's government proposed [drawing about 608 billion crowns](https://oilprice.com/Latest-Energy-News/World-News/Norway-Plans-to-Tap-637-Billion-From-Its-Oil-Fund-in-2027.html) (roughly $64 billion) from its sovereign wealth fund for the 2027 budget, about 2.7% of the fund's value, within a rule designed to let the fund last indefinitely. The fund is the textbook case of turning a finite oil endowment into a permanent one for future generations. It is a useful mirror for other oil-dependent states, Iraq among them, whose budgets still rise and fall with each year's oil price. (Reuters via OilPrice, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1957 the anthropologist and essayist Loren Eiseley published *The Immense Journey*, a collection of essays about evolution and deep time written with a poet's attention. One of them, ["The Flow of the River,"](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2021/09/10/magic-water/) opens with a sentence that has outlived its book: "If there is magic on this planet, it is contained in water." In the essay, Eiseley lets himself float down the shallow Platte River in Nebraska and feels, as the current carries him, the whole history of life moving through him, as if he were briefly part of the slow processes that carved the continent. It is a way of experiencing deep time not as a number but as a sensation. Today's briefs are about the institutional version of that experience: whether societies can plan beyond their elections, how much people value a future they will not see, and whether companies can look past the next quarter. Eiseley's essay suggests that the long view begins somewhere more personal, in the willingness to feel yourself as one moment in a much longer current. That is also why water is a fitting image for a reader in the land between two rivers, where the Tigris and the Euphrates have carried civilizations for longer than any calendar records, and where the question of what will be left for the next generation is no abstraction. The future is not a destination we reach. It is a river we are already in.
