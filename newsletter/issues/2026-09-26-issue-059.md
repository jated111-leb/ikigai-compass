# The Daily Compass #059 — The present tense

*Saturday, September 26, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

We are almost never where we are. We are ahead of ourselves, in the next deadline, the next milestone, the version of our life that begins once this stretch is finally over — or we are behind ourselves, turning over what we should have said, mourning what we lost. The one place we struggle to actually stand is the present tense, which is also the only place life is ever lived. This issue is a small argument for coming back to it, and it approaches the art of being here through three doors that turn out to open onto the same room: how we hold *time* (and why a life organized entirely around finishing things quietly defeats itself), how we hold *loss* (and what people who have lost the most know about love), and how we hold *our own company* (and why the capacity to be alone is the precondition for everything else). None of it is new; most of it is very old. But the oldest counsel about how to live has a way of sounding radical precisely when the world is fastest, loudest, and most insistent that you be somewhere other than here.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The trap of the finish line

**What happened.** The MIT philosopher Kieran Setiya once diagnosed the midlife crisis not as a failure of nerve but as a *structural* problem in how we relate to time, and the diagnosis has only aged into greater relevance. In ["How Schopenhauer's thought can illuminate a midlife crisis,"](https://aeon.co/ideas/how-schopenhauers-thought-can-illuminate-a-midlife-crisis) he starts from Schopenhauer's bleak observation that desire condemns us to "swing endlessly to and fro" between the *pain* of wanting and the *boredom* of having got. Setiya's insight is that this trap is baked into a life built out of what he calls *telic* activities — from *telos*, the Greek for end or goal. Telic activities aim at completion: finish the degree, land the promotion, ship the project, raise the kids. Each is defined by its own ending, which means every success quietly extinguishes the thing that gave the days their point, and hands you the question *now what?* His prescription is a shift of weight toward *atelic* activities — things with no finish line, valuable not for being completed but for being *done*: walking, listening to music, thinking, being with the people you love. A telic life is always deferring its meaning to a future that keeps receding. An atelic life locates meaning in the present participle — in the -ing.

**Why it matters.** This is one of the most quietly useful ideas in modern philosophy, because it reframes a feeling nearly everyone eventually has — *I did the things; why does it feel empty?* — as a fixable error of design rather than a personal defect. The achievement culture we swim in is relentlessly telic: it teaches us to treat every present moment as instrumental, a rung to somewhere else, which means the moment is never allowed to be enough. Setiya's move is not to abandon goals — you still need the degree, the project shipped — but to stop outsourcing *all* of life's value to their completion, and to recover the atelic activities that are their own reward. The test is simple and searching: if you subtracted every goal you're currently chasing, what would remain that you'd still want to be doing? Whatever survives that subtraction is where your present tense actually lives. Most of us have let it grow alarmingly small.

*Source: Setiya, Aeon (2018); the argument is developed in his book* Midlife*. ~10 min.*

### ◆ 2. What the widows know

**What happened.** If brief 1 is about time in the abstract, this is about the moment time runs out. In a searching 2026 Aeon essay, ["Abyss: What Ukrainian War Widows Know About Love and Loss,"](https://aeon.co/essays/what-ukrainian-war-widows-know-about-love-and-loss) the Ukraine-born philosopher and psychoanalyst Julie Reshe gathers the testimonies of women whose husbands were killed in the war, and refuses the tidy consolations our culture reaches for. She draws on the philosopher Catherine Malabou's concept of *destructive plasticity* — the idea that some losses don't simply wound the self that was there; they *destroy* it and force an unrecognizable new self to form in its place. Grief, in Reshe's telling, is not a problem to be processed and closed, a set of stages to move through toward "recovery." It is a transformation you do not survive intact, and the love it carries does not end when its object is gone. The widows do not "get over" their husbands; they carry them, changed, into a life that is not the one they were living.

**Why it matters.** This is the hardest of the three doors and the one that throws the others into relief, because nothing collapses the deferral of the present tense faster than the knowledge that the present is all anyone is ever guaranteed. Our dominant scripts for grief are quietly *telic* — they treat mourning as a project with a completion state, a wound that should heal so you can "move on" — and Reshe's testimony is a rebuke to that impatience. Some things are not for finishing. The counterpart lesson, for those not in the abyss, is not the cheap *memento mori* of "live each day like it's your last," which is exhausting and impossible. It is quieter: that the people you love are, right now, in the present tense, alive and reachable, and that the ordinary unremarkable afternoons with them are not the waiting room for your real life. They are the thing itself. The widows know what the rest of us keep managing to forget.

*Source: Reshe, Aeon (2026). ~14 min; not a light read, and worth it.*

### ◆ 3. First, learn to be alone

**What happened.** The third door looks, at first, like the opposite of connection, and turns out to be its precondition. In ["Before you can be with others, first learn to be alone,"](https://aeon.co/ideas/before-you-can-be-with-others-first-learn-to-be-alone) the historian of philosophy Jennifer Stitt reconstructs an idea at the center of Hannah Arendt's thought — Arendt, who fled Nazism and thought harder than almost anyone about what makes a person capable of moral judgment. The crucial distinction is between *loneliness* and *solitude*. Loneliness is the desolation of being cut off from others; solitude is the fertile state of being alone *with yourself* — of, in Arendt's phrase, keeping oneself company, conducting the silent dialogue between "me and myself" that is the very activity of thinking. And here is the sharp end of it: Arendt believed that people who cannot bear their own company, who flee solitude, lose the capacity to think at all — and that thoughtlessness, the inability to hold that inner dialogue, is what makes people available for the worst kinds of conformity and cruelty. Solitude is not withdrawal from the world. It is the workshop where a self capable of meeting the world is made.

**Why it matters.** We are living through an unprecedented experiment in the abolition of solitude — a device in every pocket engineered to ensure no moment of being alone with oneself ever has to be endured, every gap in attention instantly fillable. Stitt's Arendt gives that its proper name and stakes: what's being eroded is not just "focus" or "boredom tolerance" but the interior room where thinking and conscience happen. The reframe completes the issue's arc. Being present in your one life (brief 1) and present to the people in it (brief 2) both depend on a capacity most of us are quietly losing — the ability to be present to *yourself*, without flinching toward the screen. Solitude and connection are not opposites; solitude is what makes real connection possible, because a person who cannot be alone brings no self to the meeting. Learn to be alone, Arendt says, and you will finally have something to bring to everyone else.

*Source: Stitt, Aeon (2017), on Hannah Arendt. ~8 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Live the questions** — the counsel that holds the whole issue together is a century old, in a letter. In 1903 Rainer Maria Rilke wrote to a young poet who wanted answers about how to live: *be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart, and try to love the questions themselves* — do not seek the answers, which cannot be given because you could not yet live them; live the questions now, and perhaps you will gradually, without noticing, live your way into the answer. Maria Popova's [reading of the passage](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/06/01/rilke-on-questions/) frames it as a stabilizing force rather than a resignation: a way of inhabiting uncertainty that neither rushes to false resolution nor collapses into paralysis. It is the deepest form of the present tense — not "hold on until you know," but "the not-knowing *is* the place you live, so live there well." Against a culture that treats every open question as an emergency to be closed, Rilke's patience is almost countercultural. Some questions are not asked so you can answer them. They are asked so you can be shaped by carrying them. ([The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/06/01/rilke-on-questions/), on Rilke's *Letters to a Young Poet*)

**🃏 WILDCARD: keep the terror in the awe** — a bracing corrective to every sanitized "gratitude practice." In a 2021 Aeon essay, ["To feel the awe of living, learn to live with terror and wonder,"](https://aeon.co/essays/to-feel-the-awe-of-living-learn-to-live-with-terror-and-wonder) the existential psychologist Kirk Schneider argues that genuine *awe* — the humility-plus-wonder that makes a life feel vast and worth it — cannot be extracted from its darker twin. Real awe includes the vertigo, the smallness, the brush with the abyss; it is the whole overwhelming bigness of existence, terror and beauty together, and a wonder scrubbed clean of its terror is just decoration. His target is what he calls "mechanised living" — the anesthetized, optimized, risk-managed default that trades aliveness for comfort. The prescription is a kind of radical presence: to stay open to being *awed*, which means staying open to being unsettled. It rhymes with everything else here. The present tense is not always pleasant. But numbing yourself to its terror numbs you to its wonder in the same stroke, and a managed life safe from awe is the one loss you arrange for yourself. (Aeon, 2021)

**No one is self-made** — a 2,300-year-old solvent for the tyranny of achievement. In ["Zhuangzi and the case against meritocracy,"](https://aeon.co/essays/zhuangzi-and-the-case-against-meritocracy) the philosopher Christine Abigail Tan draws on the Daoist concept of *ziran* — roughly, "self-so" or spontaneous arising — to dismantle the flattering modern conviction that success is *deserved*, the fruit of an isolated individual will. For Zhuangzi, action arises from the convergence of countless circumstances no one authored: your gifts, your era, your teachers, your luck, the ten thousand things. This is not fatalism; it is a loosening of the white-knuckled grip on outcomes that brief 1 diagnosed. Hold your achievements lightly, because they were never as self-made as the story says — and hold your failures lightly for the same reason. It pairs with Setiya like a hand in a glove: stop measuring your life by a finish line you were never solely driving toward anyway. (Aeon, 2026)

**As intelligence gets cheap, presence gets dear** — the one dispatch from the machine world, because it bends back toward the theme. This month the price of machine intelligence hit new lows — one industry index put the cost of frontier-model tokens at roughly half its summer peak, and GPT-4-class capability has fallen something like 99.9% in cost since 2023, with rival labs now [competing on price and efficiency rather than raw power](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/01/ai-token-prices-lows.html). Treat the exact figures as indicative, but the direction is unmistakable: fluent, tireless cognition is becoming one of the cheapest things in the world. Which is worth sitting with, because scarcity is where value goes. If analysis, summary, and plausible prose are approaching free, the things that appreciate are precisely the ones this issue is about — presence, judgment held under real uncertainty, the unoptimized attention of one person actually with another. The machine can generate the words. What it cannot do, to borrow a line, is *be with you*. That may turn out to be the whole ballgame. (CNBC / Silicon Data index, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Three doors, one room. Setiya says stop deferring your life to a finish line and recover the activities that are their own reward. Reshe says the present, and the people in it, are the only thing you are ever actually given, and grief is the price and the proof of having loved them. Arendt says you cannot truly be with anyone until you can bear to be alone with yourself, because that silent inner dialogue is where a self worth bringing to others gets made. Rilke says live the questions; Schneider says stay open enough to be awed, terror and all; Zhuangzi says hold it all lightly, because you made less of it than you think. Notice that none of this is advice about *doing more*. It is, almost entirely, advice about *being here* — about relocating the center of gravity of a life out of the deferred future and the rehearsed past and into the present participle, the -ing, the only tense anything is ever alive in. This is not a small ask in 2026. Nearly every system around us — the achievement culture, the notification, the newsfeed, now the infinitely available machine intelligence — is engineered to keep you somewhere other than where you are, because your restless attention is the product. Which makes the oldest counsel the most quietly subversive thing you can practice. Maria Popova, writing this month on [how to change a life,](https://www.themarginalian.org/2026/09/09/how-to-change-your-life/) reaches for the image of unclenching the fist — letting the old story slip into the river of time. The present tense is not a technique. It is a fist, slowly opening. Start there, this weekend, with the hand you're holding this on.
