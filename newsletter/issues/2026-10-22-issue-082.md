# The Daily Compass #082 — Inside the box

*Thursday, October 22, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Around 1959 the publisher Bennett Cerf bet Theodor Geisel, better known as Dr. Seuss, $50 that he could not write a book using only 50 different words. Geisel had just written *The Cat in the Hat* with a vocabulary of a couple of hundred words, and Cerf wanted to see how far the idea could be pushed. The result, published in 1960, was *Green Eggs and Ham*, which uses exactly 50 distinct words and became one of the best-selling children's books ever written. The limit did not cramp the book; it shaped it. The repetition, the rhythm and the stubborn back-and-forth between Sam-I-am and his reluctant friend all grow out of the narrow vocabulary. This issue is about constraints: when limits on time, money, tools or rules make work better, when they simply make it worse, and what the AI race reveals about invention under pressure.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The box and the treadmill

**What happened.** David Epstein, whose earlier book *Range* argued for breadth and experimentation, has spent his latest book making what sounds like the opposite case. In [*Inside the Box: How Constraints Make Us Better*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/240336791-inside-the-box) (Riverhead, May 2026), he argues that total freedom can be paralysing and that limits, whether imposed by circumstance or chosen deliberately, often produce better work. His examples range from Dr. Seuss's 50-word bet to Keith Jarrett's celebrated *Köln Concert*, improvised on a faulty piano, and to engineers who achieved more because they had less. The opposing evidence is older but important. In ["Creativity Under the Gun"](https://hbr.org/2002/08/creativity-under-the-gun) (*Harvard Business Review*, 2002), Teresa Amabile, Constance Noonan Hadley and Steven Kramer analysed some 9,000 daily diary entries from 177 employees on 22 project teams and found that heavy time pressure usually killed creativity. But not always. People under pressure who felt they were "on a mission", focused on one important goal and protected from distractions, could still be highly creative. People on a "treadmill", rushing to meet deadlines that seemed arbitrary and fragmented, almost never were.

**Why it matters.** Taken together, the two pieces suggest a more precise rule than "constraints are good" or "pressure is bad". A constraint helps when it narrows attention toward a goal people understand: a vocabulary limit, a budget that forces a simpler design, a deadline with a clear reason behind it. It hurts when it simply adds stress without adding focus, as with a deadline imposed for its own sake or a cut that removes the time needed to think. Some reviewers have criticised Epstein for leaning on anecdotes, and the distinction between focusing limits and mere hardship is the one that matters most. For leaders, the practical lesson is to explain why a limit exists, protect the people working under it from other distractions, and be wary of constraints that only make work harder without making it clearer.

*Source: Epstein, Inside the Box (2026); Amabile, Hadley & Kramer, Harvard Business Review (Aug 2002). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 2. Born in a downturn

**What happened.** Is a recession a bad time to start a company? Common sense says yes: money is scarce, customers are cautious and investors are nervous. A working paper by Daniel Bias of Vanderbilt and Alexander Ljungqvist of the Stockholm School of Economics, ["Great Recession Babies: How Are Startups Shaped by Macro Conditions at Birth?"](https://cepr.org/publications/DP18442), finds something more interesting. Using detailed data on innovative American startups, and the essentially random timing of patent decisions to separate the effect of the recession from the kind of founders who start firms in hard times, they found that startups born during the Great Recession did better over the long run. They were [about 12% more likely](https://www.hhs.se/en/houseoffinance/research/featured-topics/2023/are-recessions-a-good-time-to-start-a-business) to survive to their seventh anniversary than startups founded in other years between 2002 and 2012, grew employment and sales faster, and went on to produce more original inventions. The surprise was the mechanism. Easier or harder funding did not explain the difference. The labour market did: in a weak job market, recession-born startups were able to keep their founding inventors and build strong research teams around them, because those people had fewer attractive alternatives.

**Why it matters.** The paper refines the popular idea that scarcity builds strong companies. The useful constraint was not a lack of money, which hurts startups as much as anyone, but a lack of outside options for talented people, which made teams more stable. The authors are careful to add that recessions do not create superstar firms; the benefits were largest for startups that began with weaker growth. For founders and investors, the lesson is that the conditions at birth matter, and that a downturn can be a good moment to assemble a team that would be hard to hold together in a boom. For established companies, it is a reminder that stability of talent is itself a competitive advantage, and that cutting deeply in hard times can mean giving rivals the very people they need.

*Source: Bias & Ljungqvist, CEPR Discussion Paper DP18442. ~40 min (paper).*

### ◆ 3. Designing around the wall

**What happened.** The most consequential constraint in AI today is political. US export controls have cut Chinese AI labs off from the most advanced Nvidia chips, on the theory that limiting computing power will slow their progress. The response has been a growing effort to make AI models and Chinese hardware fit each other. On September 30, DeepSeek, the Chinese lab whose efficient models surprised the industry last year, released [open-source programming tools](https://letsdatascience.com/news/deepseek-releases-ascend-software-with-huawei-847f368b) for Huawei's Ascend AI chips, including an Ascend version of its TileLang programming language and a set of computing and communication libraries, each mirroring tools it had previously built for Nvidia hardware. Huawei supported the work, and the industry analysts at SemiAnalysis have described the relationship between the two companies as increasingly symbiotic, with model and chip being shaped around each other.

**Why it matters.** Whether Chinese chips can match Nvidia's for training the largest models remains unproven, and export controls do impose real costs. But the episode illustrates the theme of this issue on a geopolitical scale. A constraint designed to slow a competitor can also force it to build capabilities it would otherwise have bought, in this case a domestic chip ecosystem and software tuned tightly to it. DeepSeek's earlier reputation rested on getting more out of less computing power; the same pressure is now pushing it to co-design software and hardware. For policymakers, it is a reminder that restrictions change incentives as well as capabilities. For companies everywhere, it is a reminder that a rival denied the easy path may find a different one, and that the path they find may be harder to copy.

*Source: DeepSeek release via LetsDataScience (Sep 30, 2026); SemiAnalysis (Oct 2026). ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the ten-minute turn** — in the early 1970s the young Southwest Airlines was losing money and had to sell one of its four planes. The problem was how to keep flying the same schedule with three. According to the story [told by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2015/06/28/418147961/the-man-who-saved-southwest-airlines-with-a-10-minute-idea), Bill Franklin, the head of ground operations, worked out a way to turn a plane around at the gate in about ten minutes, far faster than the industry norm, by having crews do everything in parallel, like a racing pit stop. The constraint of one missing plane produced the operating model that defined Southwest for decades: quick turns, more flights per aircraft and lower costs. The company had not set out to invent a new strategy. It had set out to survive with less. (NPR, 2015)

**🃏 WILDCARD: seventeen ways to leave the sea** — crabs are marine animals, yet some species have learned to live entirely on land, and a new study finds that they have done so independently [as many as 17 times](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-crabs-reveal-evolution.html). Comparing 14 species, the researchers found that the more fully a crab had adapted to life on land, the more complete the genes in its gut bacteria for breaking down tough plant material such as lignin and cellulose. The same hard constraint, a diet of fibrous land plants, appears to have pushed separate lineages toward similar partnerships with microbes. Evolution, faced with the same box, keeps arriving at the same solution. (phys.org, Oct 2026)

**The constraint is the idea** — the D&AD awards, among the most demanding in advertising and design, [reported this autumn](https://www.adobomagazine.com/insight/dad-finds-creativity-thriving-under-constraints-in-2026/) that work which turns a limit into an advantage had become one of the strongest trends in this year's entries. One example: Saudia, the Saudi airline, worked with artists on designed stickers that let travellers' cultural purchases count toward extra baggage allowance, turning a frustrating rule into part of the experience. The jury's broader observation was that the best briefs had shifted from "what should we say?" to "what can we build, change or invent?" (D&AD Trend Report 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1807 William Wordsworth published a sonnet about sonnets. It begins with images of people content in narrow spaces, nuns in their convent rooms, hermits in their cells, students in their "pensive citadels", bees in foxglove bells, and then turns to his own craft: "In truth the prison, into which we doom / Ourselves, no prison is: and hence for me, / In sundry moods, 'twas pastime to be bound / Within the Sonnet's scanty plot of ground." The sonnet's fourteen lines were a constraint he chose, and he found in them not confinement but relief. He ends by offering the form as solace to those who have felt "the weight of too much liberty". It is a phrase worth carrying into any week of open calendars, endless options and blank pages. Freedom is a gift, but it can also be a weight. Sometimes the most generous thing we can give ourselves, or our teams, is a smaller plot of ground and a clear reason to cultivate it well.
