# The Daily Compass #070 — The electric century

*Friday, October 9, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

For most of the last hundred years, power meant oil. Whoever pumped it, shipped it or guarded the straits it passed through held a kind of leverage no other commodity could match. That is starting to change, slowly and unevenly. Solar panels have become so cheap that farmers in Punjab buy them the way they once bought diesel pumps. Cars, heaters, factories and data centers are switching from burning fuel to drawing current. A new word, "electrostate," has entered the vocabulary as a rival to the old "petrostate." But electricity is not a commodity you can simply pump and ship. It has to be generated, moved and balanced every second, and the countries that do that badly can be rich in energy and still sit in the dark. This issue asks what the electric century really means: whether the new word describes anything real, what happens when citizens give up on a failing grid and build their own, and why a country with some of the world's largest oil reserves spent another summer unable to keep its lights on.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. What is an electrostate?

**What happened.** The term "electrostate" was coined by Kingsmill Bond and his colleagues at the energy think tank Ember, as the opposite of a petrostate: a country whose economy runs mainly on electricity rather than on burning fuels. It has caught on fast, mostly as shorthand for China's dominance of solar panels, batteries and electric cars. In a new essay on his Substack, ["What is an electrostate?"](https://janrosenow.substack.com/p/what-is-an-electrostate), the Oxford energy-policy professor Jan Rosenow points out that the word is widely used but rarely defined, and he tries to fix that. His working definition is a country where a large share of final energy use comes as electricity. That test produces surprises. By this measure, he notes, Bahrain runs more of its economy on electricity than the United States does, even though nearly all of its power is generated from gas. Norway gets close to half its final energy from electricity and is still one of the world's great exporters of oil and gas. Rosenow's conclusion is that "electrostate" is not a club a country is in or out of. It is a direction of travel.

**Why it matters.** The essay is a useful cure for slogans. Electrification and clean energy are related but not the same thing: a country can electrify on gas, as Bahrain has, or on coal, as China largely still does. Scott Moore of Brookings made a harder-edged version of the point in August in ["Electrostates vs. petrostates"](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/electrostates-vs-petrostates-the-us-china-and-the-hormuz-energy-shock/), arguing that the contrast between the two is overstated. When the energy shock from the Strait of Hormuz hit this year, he writes, China's most important buffer was not its solar fleet but its continued reliance on fossil fuels, especially coal, although its clean-technology industries may still give it a more lasting advantage. For oil-producing countries in the Gulf and Iraq, the lesson cuts both ways. The shift to electricity does not mean oil and gas stop mattering overnight. But the direction of travel is clear, and a country that keeps selling fuel while failing to build a strong electric system at home is preparing for the wrong century.

*Source: Rosenow, Bright Spots (Oct 2026), ~10 min; Moore, Brookings (Aug 10, 2026), ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. When citizens build their own grid

**What happened.** The most dramatic electricity story of the decade did not come from a government plan. It came from Pakistan's rooftops. As Beth Gardiner reported for [*Yale Environment 360*](https://e360.yale.edu/features/pakistan-solar-boom), solar's share of Pakistan's electricity generation rose from about 4% in 2021 to 14% in 2024. The drivers were economic rather than environmental: electricity bills that soared after 2022 as subsidies were cut, an unreliable grid, and a flood of cheap Chinese panels. Households, farmers and businesses simply bought panels and installed them, mostly without asking anyone. Muhammad Mustafa Amjad of the Islamabad think tank Renewables First argues that the boom shows developing countries do not have to be laggards in the energy transition. But it also created a problem. Every household that moves its daytime consumption onto its own roof leaves fewer paying customers to cover the fixed costs of the grid, and that pushes prices up for those who remain. Analysts call this the utility "death spiral."

**Why it matters.** Pakistan shows what happens when the cost of generation collapses faster than institutions can adapt. Cheap solar gives individuals a way out of a failing system, which is good for them and possibly bad for the system they leave. The poorest households, who cannot afford panels, end up paying more for a shrinking grid. This is the central tension of the electric century in poorer countries: generation is becoming abundant and decentralized, while the grid, which is still needed at night, for industry and for those who cannot exit, becomes a harder business to sustain. The countries that handle this well will treat rooftop solar as a resource to be integrated, with storage, fair tariffs and a grid that can absorb it, rather than as a threat to be taxed away. The ones that handle it badly will end up with two systems, one for those who can afford to leave and one for those who cannot.

*Source: Gardiner, Yale Environment 360 (Jul 2025), ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Rich in energy, short of power

**What happened.** No country illustrates the gap between owning energy and delivering electricity more painfully than Iraq. According to the Middle East Economic Survey, in an analysis titled ["Iraq's Electricity Permacrisis Deepens"](https://www.mees.com/2026/10/2/power-water/iraqs-electricity-permacrisis-deepens/aed769e0-be68-11f1-9712-25ae62f0540a) (Oct 2), Iraq's peak electricity demand rose 9% this summer to a record 60 gigawatts, double its 2020 level, while supply fell to about 22 gigawatts. Demand in most Gulf states fell this year as regional conflict disrupted their economies; Iraq's kept climbing. MEES describes the deficit as structural. The Ministry of Electricity's own spokesman put demand at 60,000 megawatts against a summer supply plan of "30,000 MW at best," and acknowledged problems of mismanagement and poor planning. MEES's central conclusion is that more power plants and more imports will bring only limited relief unless Iraq reforms its heavily subsidized distribution system, in which much electricity is lost, unbilled or unpaid.

**Why it matters.** Iraq's crisis is often described as a shortage of fuel or plants, and both are real. But the deeper problem is the one the first two briefs point to. Electricity is a system, not a commodity: it depends on investment, maintenance, billing, prices that cover costs, and a grid that can carry power where it is needed. A country can sit on vast oil reserves and still fail at all of those. Through the same summer of long daily outages, Iraqi households fell back, as they have for years, on neighborhood generator subscriptions and, increasingly, rooftop solar: partial exits from a grid they no longer trust, much like Pakistan's. For anyone building a business in Iraq, the electricity gap is not background noise. It shapes when customers are online, how much they can spend, and what infrastructure they trust. And for a country whose budget depends on oil, the electric century poses a question that no amount of crude will answer: who will build and pay for a grid that works?

*Source: MEES (Oct 2, 2026), paywalled; [IraqiNews](https://www.iraqinews.com/iraq/iraq-electricity-demand-60000-mw-summer-plan/) (Sep 26, 2026). ~8 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Soviet power plus electrification** — in December 1920, with Russia still in civil war, Lenin told the Eighth All-Russian Congress of Soviets that "Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country." The congress approved the [GOELRO plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOELRO) on December 22, the first great state electrification program, drafted by a commission under the engineer Gleb Krzhizhanovsky. Lenin called it the party's "second program." Whatever one makes of the regime, the slogan captured something that remains true: governments have long understood that electrification is not a technical project but a political one, a way of reaching every village and binding it to the state. A century later, the countries that treat the grid as national infrastructure in that sense, rather than as a utility to be patched each summer, are the ones pulling ahead. (Lenin, 1920)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the spiders that glow in the abyss** — sea spiders, or pycnogonids, are strange, long-legged marine animals that are not true spiders. A new study in [*Deep Sea Research Part I*](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-legged-giants-deep-ocean.html) by Jérôme Mallefet and colleagues reports the first evidence that some of them glow. Working in a makeshift darkroom on a research ship off southeastern Australia, the team examined giant sea spiders hauled up from depths of nearly 5,000 meters and found that two species, including *Colossendeis tasmanica*, give off a deep blue light, strongest in their legs. The light seems to come from cells linked to the gut, and the researchers suspect the animals may get the ability from their diet. What the glow is for remains unknown. In the deepest dark on Earth, it turns out, even spiders make their own light. (Phys.org / Science News, Oct 2026)

**Do data centers raise your bill?** — the one AI item this week. The political fight over AI's electricity demand is now about who pays. On September 30, a bipartisan group of US senators unveiled a [permitting reform bill](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/heinrich-lee-capito-and-whitehouse-introduce-bipartisan-american-affordability-and-jobs-act-of-2026) that would require data centers to pay the full cost of grid upgrades built to serve them rather than passing those costs to households, though no vote is expected until after the November elections. The evidence on whether data centers have raised bills so far is more surprising than the politics suggests. A [study by economists at the Electric Power Research Institute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19777) (June 2026), which uses the 1947 interstate highway plan to isolate cause from coincidence, finds that between 2015 and 2024, doubling a state's data-center capacity *lowered* residential electricity prices by about 3.5%, because it spread the grid's fixed costs over more sales. The authors warn that this could reverse as AI demand outpaces supply. (EPRI; US Senate, Sep 30, 2026)

**The scale of the build** — the International Energy Agency's new [special report on electrification](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/1c696cc3-e251-46a9-8d4d-9edb2a4e806a/Electrification.pdf) (Sep 2026) estimates that investment in power generation, grids and end-use electrification will reach about $2 trillion in 2026, and that grid investment alone has risen from around $315 billion in 2015 to $450 billion in 2025. The money is moving. The question for countries like Iraq is whether any of it arrives. (IEA, Sep 2026)

A careful episode on a fight where both sides talk past each other: critics who say data centers drain aquifers, and defenders who call their water use a rounding error. Shedd, an expert in data-center cooling, explains how cooling liquid now runs directly to the chips and why running it hotter can sharply reduce the water needed. **Queue it** for a clear, technical answer to a question that comes up in every data-center debate, including in the Gulf, where water is scarce. **Radio Davos — "Demand for electricity is soaring: how can our power systems keep up?"** (Oct 2026)
Varun Sivaram, founder of Emerald AI, and the World Economic Forum's Divya Reddy discuss how surging demand from AI, combined with fuel shocks from war, is straining power systems. Sivaram's memorable analogy is that the grid is an eight-lane highway that is empty most of the year, so a data center that cuts its use at the few peak moments can avoid the need for a costly ninth lane. **Queue it**, with the caveat that it is a polished production and Sivaram is describing the approach his own company sells. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In 1900, at the Paris Exposition, the American historian Henry Adams stood in the hall of machines before the great electric dynamos and, as he wrote in [*The Education of Henry Adams*](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/henry-adams/the-education-of-henry-adams/text/chapter-25), began to feel them "as a moral force, much as the early Christians felt the Cross." The dynamo, he thought, was the new symbol of the age. Thirty-three years later, the Japanese novelist Jun'ichirō Tanizaki wrote the counter-argument, a short book called [*In Praise of Shadows*](https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/05/28/in-praise-of-shadows-tanizaki/). Electric light, he argued, had flooded Japanese homes and erased a beauty that depended on darkness: the glow of lacquerware by candlelight, the depth of a dim room, the gold that only reveals itself in shadow. Between Adams's awe and Tanizaki's grief lies most of what is worth saying about the electric century. Electricity is the closest thing modern life has to a miracle. It powers hospitals, refrigerators, classrooms and the machines that may soon think for us, and anyone who has lived through a summer of outages in Baghdad or Basra knows that its absence is not poetic. But Tanizaki's point was never that light is bad. It was that every technology changes what we notice and what we lose, and that a civilization should choose its light deliberately rather than simply turning everything on. The task of the coming decades, for countries and for each of us, is to have enough power and to remember what it is for.
