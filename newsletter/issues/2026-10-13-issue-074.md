# The Daily Compass #074 — The city as an idea machine

*Tuesday, October 13, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In the year 762, the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur chose a site on the west bank of the Tigris and began building a capital shaped like a perfect circle. Four gates faced the roads to Kufa, Basra, Syria and Khurasan; the palace and the great mosque stood at the centre. Within a few generations Baghdad was among the largest cities on earth, and under al-Ma'mun it hosted the House of Wisdom, where scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian learning into Arabic and added to it. The round city did not survive, but the idea behind it did: a city is not just a place where people live. It is a machine for bringing people, money and ideas together, and some cities are much better at it than others. This issue asks what kind of machine a city is: why some cities make things and others mostly spend, how Chinese cities turned themselves into venture investors, and what remote work is doing to the downtowns that made the old machine run.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Producer cities and consumer cities

**What happened.** The economist Dietrich Vollrath, in ["The Wrong Kind of City?"](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/07/the-wrong-kind-of-city) for *Asterisk* magazine, revives a distinction made a century ago by the sociologist Max Weber. Some cities are *producer cities*: they grow because they make things that the rest of the world buys, as Manchester did with cotton and Pittsburgh with steel. Others are *consumer cities*: they grow because they collect income produced elsewhere, through taxes, rents or resource revenues, and spend it locally. Weber's examples were imperial capitals such as Beijing and Moscow; Vollrath's modern examples include the Gulf. The distinction matters because it predicts very different futures. Vollrath draws on his own research with Douglas Gollin and Remi Jedwab, which found that countries rich in natural resources often urbanize *without* industrializing. Resource revenues flow into the capital, people follow the money into services and government jobs, and the city grows quickly while producing very little that can be sold abroad. Producer cities, by contrast, tend to be where new technologies, firms and skills are born, because making tradable goods forces constant competition and learning.

**Why it matters.** For anyone who lives or works in an oil-funded city, this is uncomfortable but useful. A city can look prosperous, with new towers, malls and traffic, while its economy depends almost entirely on revenues that arrive from outside. Such a city can stay comfortable as long as the revenue keeps coming, but it does not build the skills or firms that would let it survive without that revenue. The practical test is simple: what does this city sell to people who do not live in it? Media, software, logistics, tourism and specialised services can all turn a consumer city into a producer one, but only if they compete in markets beyond the city's own payroll. Vollrath's essay is also a reminder that the most important urban policy questions are not about roads or zoning but about what the local economy is for.

*Source: Vollrath, Asterisk, Issue 07 (c. Oct 2024); Gollin, Jedwab & Vollrath, Journal of Economic Growth (2016). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 2. When cities act like venture funds

**What happened.** The latest issue of *Works in Progress* carries ["China's Growth Machine"](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/chinas-growth-machine/) by Robin Rivaton and Ben Southwood, an explanation of one of the stranger engines of Chinese growth: city governments behaving like risk-loving venture capitalists. Their clearest example is Hefei, a provincial capital that was once considered a backwater. In 2008 the city put about $500 million into a new LCD panel factory for the display maker BOE, at a time when the project looked like a large gamble. The bet worked, and Hefei became a major centre for displays and semiconductors. In 2020 it did it again, rescuing the struggling electric-car maker NIO in exchange for a stake of about 24% and bringing its headquarters to the city; by the authors' account the investment returned more than 30% a year. Across China, they argue, thousands of local governments compete to attract firms with land, capital and fast permits, turning the country into something like a continent-sized Silicon Valley in which cities, not just private investors, place the bets.

**Why it matters.** The model has costs that the authors do not hide: heavy local debt, wasteful duplication and industries with far more factories than buyers. Many city bets fail, and the losers are often taxpayers. But the essay offers a different way to think about economic development. Most governments act as regulators or grant-givers; Hefei acted as an investor that wanted a return, owned a stake and picked industries where it could build a cluster of suppliers around one big firm. For cities that want to become producer cities rather than consumer ones, the lesson is not to copy China's debt but to copy its seriousness: choose a few industries, put real capital behind them, measure the returns and be willing to stop funding failures.

*Source: Rivaton & Southwood, Works in Progress, Issue 26 (Oct 12, 2026). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 3. The downtown that may not come back

**What happened.** Why are some downtowns busy again and others still half-empty years after the pandemic? A new paper in the *American Economic Review* by Ferdinando Monte, Charly Porcher and Esteban Rossi-Hansberg, ["Remote Work and City Structure"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20231414) (August 2026), offers an answer. In their model, people go to the office partly for the benefits of meeting others there, and those benefits depend on how many other people also go. If most colleagues stay home, the office is less valuable, so even fewer people go. That feedback means the same city can settle into two very different stable outcomes: a busy downtown, or a quiet one. The data fit the story. In the largest US cities, commuting trips have stabilized at about 60% of pre-pandemic levels, while smaller cities have largely returned to normal. The authors estimate that cities stuck in the low-commuting equilibrium lose about 2.3% in welfare, a large number for a whole metropolitan area.

**Why it matters.** The paper's message is that a quiet downtown is not necessarily what people want; it may be a coordination failure. Individually, each worker chooses rationally to stay home, but together they destroy the thing that made the office worth visiting. That has two practical implications. For cities, small policies that push enough people back at the same time, such as anchor events, transport improvements or shared office days, can tip a downtown from one equilibrium to the other. For companies, the lesson is the same at a smaller scale. Office days work best when the right people are present together; scattered attendance gives employees the cost of commuting without the benefit of meeting.

*Source: Monte, Porcher & Rossi-Hansberg, American Economic Review (Aug 2026); NBER Working Paper 31494. ~45 min (paper).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the Round City** — al-Mansur's [Madinat al-Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_City_of_Baghdad), the "City of Peace," was begun in 762 and largely finished by about 766. Its double walls enclosed a circle roughly two kilometres across, with residential rings inside and an open central court holding the caliph's green-domed palace and the main mosque. Within decades the city had spilled far beyond its walls, along the river and into markets on both banks, and the circle itself was gradually built over. The design was perfect for a court; the growth came from trade, which never respected the circle. (Encyclopaedic sources)

**🃏 WILDCARD: letters from a Hittite epidemic** — archaeologists at Kayalıpınar in central Turkey, thought to be the Hittite city of Samuha, have [recovered 131 new clay tablets](https://www.dailysabah.com/life/hittite-royal-archive-with-184-tablets-unearthed-in-central-turkiye/news) this season, bringing the site's archive to 184. The texts date to the reign of Tudhaliya II, about 3,400 years ago. Among them are reports of a high-fever disease spreading from city to city and killing children, records of the illness and funeral rites of a still-nursing princess, and a letter from generals asking for reinforcements. The excavation is led by Çiğdem Maner of Koç University. It is a rare glimpse of what an epidemic felt like inside a Bronze Age network of cities. (Daily Sabah, Oct 2026)

**AI piles into one metro** — the one AI item this week. According to Crunchbase data, [as summarised by FourWeekMBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-crunchbase-q3-2026-159b-venture-27-billion-dollar-rounds-ai/), global venture funding reached about $159 billion in the third quarter, and AI companies took about $102 billion of it, or 64%. A record 27 rounds were worth $1 billion or more. The San Francisco Bay Area alone accounted for about 24% of global venture investment. The local effect is visible: Zumper puts the [median one-bedroom rent in San Francisco at about $4,400](https://kioncentralcoast.com/news/top-stories/2026/09/29/san-francisco-one-bedroom-rents-at-record-high-up-25-year-over-year-report-finds/), up about 25% from a year earlier, against almost flat rents nationally. Remote work was supposed to spread the tech economy out; the latest wave of capital has concentrated it in one city more than ever. (Crunchbase, Oct 2026; Zumper via KION, Sep 2026)

**Dubai crosses 4.8 million** — Dubai's population passed 4.8 million residents at the end of September, according to figures reported by Gulf News, an increase of more than 220,000 since the end of 2025. Few cities anywhere are growing that fast, and nearly all of the growth comes from people moving in, which makes Dubai a living test of Vollrath's question about what a city produces. (Gulf News, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Italo Calvino's *Invisible Cities* (1972) is a book of imaginary conversations between Marco Polo and the emperor Kublai Khan. Polo describes dozens of cities, each stranger than the last, built on stilts, made of threads, remembered only by their inhabitants' desires, until Polo admits that every time he describes a city, he is saying something about Venice, and perhaps about every city at all. At the end, the Khan worries that all cities are drifting toward an infernal one. Polo answers that the inferno is not something in the future; it is already here, the place where we live every day and that we form by being together. There are two ways to escape suffering it, he says. The first is easy: accept the inferno and become so much a part of it that you no longer see it. The second takes constant attention and learning: seek and learn to recognise "who and what, in the midst of the inferno, are not inferno, then make them endure, give them space." It is a fair description of what good cities do, and what good city builders do. Every city has its traffic, its waste and its broken promises. The work is to find the things inside it that are working, the square where people meet, the workshop that sells to the world, the library that stays open late, and give them room to grow.
