# The Daily Compass #113 — What the market will bear

*Sunday, November 22, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

For most of human history there was no such thing as a price tag. You walked into a shop, asked what something cost, and the answer depended on who you were, how you were dressed and how hard you bargained. The change began with an unlikely group: the Quakers. Believing that charging different people different prices for the same thing was dishonest, Quaker merchants set a single price and stuck to it. In the nineteenth century, big-city retailers turned the idea into a business model. Alexander Turney Stewart's "Marble Palace" on Broadway, which opened in the 1840s, offered [one fixed price for everyone](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/alexander-turney-stewart-and-the-development-of-the-department-store-18231876/A9D769CDB038FF844DA3CDEC6B076D44), and John Wanamaker in Philadelphia, who called haggling "a war between the seller and the buyer", made the marked price and the money-back guarantee famous. He did not invent the price tag, but he helped make it normal. As NPR's *Planet Money* [has told it](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/415287577), fixed prices also suited the new giant stores: clerks no longer needed to be skilled hagglers, so they could be trained quickly. For about 150 years, one price for everyone was the norm. Now, with data and algorithms, prices are becoming personal again. This issue is about pricing: why it is the most powerful lever in business, how people judge what is fair, and how AI is quietly changing who sets the price.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The one-percent lever

**What happened.** Of all the decisions a company makes, few move profit as much as price. In ["Managing Price, Gaining Profit"](https://store.hbr.org/product/managing-price-gaining-profit/92507) (*Harvard Business Review*, 1992), the McKinsey consultants Michael Marn and Robert Rosiello used the average economics of more than 2,400 companies to show that a 1% improvement in price, with sales volume unchanged, raised operating profit by about 11%, far more than the same improvement in variable costs, volume or fixed costs (consultancy analysis). Later McKinsey work found that much of the potential leaks away between the list price and what a company actually collects, through discounts, rebates, payment terms and free freight, a cascade it called the ["pocket price waterfall"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/the-power-of-pricing). Warren Buffett has put the point most bluntly. In a 2010 interview with the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, he said: ["The single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power. If you've got the power to raise prices without losing business to a competitor, you've got a very good business. And if you have to have a prayer session before raising the price by a tenth of a cent, then you've got a terrible business."](https://ritholtz.com/2016/03/fcic-buffett/) But pricing power can be destroyed by careless use. In February 2012, J.C. Penney's new chief executive, Ron Johnson, abolished most coupons and sales in favour of "fair and square" everyday prices. Customers who had loved hunting for bargains stayed away; comparable-store sales [fell about 25%](https://money.cnn.com/2013/02/27/news/companies/jc-penney/) that year, and Johnson was ousted in April 2013. In July 2011 Netflix split its DVD and streaming plans, raising the price of the popular combination by 60%, and in the following quarter it [lost about 800,000 US subscribers](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001065280/000119312511278716/d246709dex991.htm). Sometimes the most powerful pricing move is not to move at all. Costco has sold its hot dog and soda combo for $1.50 since 1985, and in March its chief executive, Ron Vachris, said the price ["will not change as long as I'm around"](https://fortune.com/2026/03/21/costco-hot-dog-price-1-50-ceo-confirms/). The company makes most of its profit from membership fees, and the hot dog is a promise to members that the deal is honest.

**Why it matters.** Most companies spend far more management time on costs and sales volume than on price, although price is usually the bigger lever. Three lessons follow. First, measure what you actually collect, not your list price; the waterfall of discounts and concessions is where many companies quietly give away their margin. Second, pricing power comes from value customers cannot easily get elsewhere, so the real work of pricing is done long before the price is set, in product, brand and customer relationships. Third, a price is also a message. JCPenney's customers read "fair and square" as "no more deals"; Netflix's read the split as contempt. Costco's unchanging hot dog shows the opposite: a visible, symbolic price can build trust that supports everything else a company sells.

*Source: Marn & Rosiello, HBR 70(5) (1992; consultancy); Marn, Roegner & Zawada, McKinsey Quarterly (2003); Buffett, FCIC interview (May 26, 2010); CNN Money (Feb 27, 2013); Netflix shareholder letter (Oct 24, 2011); Fortune (Mar 21, 2026). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. What feels fair

**What happened.** People do not judge prices only by how much they pay. They judge them by whether they seem fair, and those judgements follow rules that economists long ignored. In ["Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking"](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/course131/Kahneman-FairnessConstraintProfit-1986.pdf) (*American Economic Review*, 1986), Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler asked people about a hardware store that raised the price of snow shovels from $15 to $20 the morning after a big snowstorm. 82% called it unfair. Their broader finding was that people accept price rises that pass on higher costs, but resent rises that exploit a sudden surge in demand, and that firms which ignore this pay for it in lost goodwill. Small cues matter too. In field experiments with a mail-order clothing catalogue, Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester found that prices ending in 9 [raised demand](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2003/effects-of-9-price-endings-on-retail-sales-evidence/), in some tests selling more at $49 than at $44, with the effect strongest for new items customers had no reference price for (*Quantitative Marketing and Economics*, 2003). Many companies, especially young ones, leave money on the table. Working with the online recruitment firm ZipRecruiter, Jean-Pierre Dubé and Sanjog Misra randomised the prices offered to new customers and found that the company had been charging too little: [moving to the profit-maximising single price](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23775) would have raised profits by about 55% (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2023). Tailoring prices to each customer would have added more profit, but cut consumers' gains by about 23%. And how startups price is changing. A 2025 survey of more than 240 software companies by the newsletter writer Kyle Poyar found that [hybrid pricing](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization), combining a subscription with charges for usage, had risen from 27% to 41% of companies in a year, while pricing per user seat was falling (newsletter survey).

**Why it matters.** Price is not just a number; it is part of a relationship, and customers police it with a strong sense of fairness. The practical lessons for leaders are to explain price rises in terms of costs when you can, to avoid raising prices at the moment customers are most desperate, and to test before assuming. The ZipRecruiter experiment is a useful warning for founders: many start-ups set a price once, often too low, and never test it. Price experiments with new customers are among the cheapest and most valuable experiments a young company can run. The same research also marks a limit. The more precisely a firm can tailor prices to each customer, the more profit it can extract, and the closer it comes to the line where customers decide they are being exploited.

*Source: Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, AER 76(4) (1986); Anderson & Simester, QME 1 (2003); Dubé & Misra, JPE 131(1) (2023); Poyar, Growth Unhinged (Jun 2025; newsletter survey). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. The price nobody set

**What happened.** Increasingly, prices are set by software, and research suggests that pricing algorithms can learn to keep prices high without anyone telling them to. In ["Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion"](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20190623) (*American Economic Review*, 2020), Emilio Calvano and colleagues let simple learning algorithms compete on price in a simulated market. Without communicating, they learned to charge above competitive prices and to punish any rival that undercut them. Real markets show signs of the same. Studying German petrol stations as pricing software spread, Stephanie Assad, Robert Clark, Daniel Ershov and Lei Xu found that in towns with two stations, margins [rose by about 28% when both adopted algorithms](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/726906), but did not change when only one did (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2024). A newer study by Sara Fish, Yannai Gonczarowski and Ran Shorrer found that pricing agents built on large language models, told only to maximise long-run profit, [quickly settled on high prices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806) in competitive markets (arXiv; not yet peer reviewed). Regulators are responding. In November 2025 the US Justice Department reached a [proposed settlement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requires-realpage-end-sharing-competitively-sensitive-information-and) with RealPage, whose software helps landlords set rents, requiring it to stop using competitors' confidential data in its pricing models and to accept a monitor; RealPage admitted no wrongdoing. The other frontier is personal pricing. In December 2025 an investigation by Consumer Reports and two advocacy groups found that Instacart shoppers buying identical items from the same store at the same time were sometimes [shown prices up to 23% apart](https://www.consumerreports.org/media-room/press-releases/2025/12/instacart-ends-ai-driven-pricing-practice-following-a-joint-consumer-reports-investigation-with-groundwork-collaborative-and-more-perfect-union/); Instacart ended the price tests while disputing the findings. Since then several US states have restricted "surveillance pricing", setting individual prices from personal data. Maryland's ban for groceries and delivery apps [took effect on October 1](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/maryland-becomes-the-first-state-to-restrict-surveillance-pricing), and in August the Federal Trade Commission proposed a policy on when personalised pricing counts as unfair.

**Why it matters.** Competition law was built to catch people who agree to fix prices. Algorithms can reach high prices without any agreement at all, which is why regulators are shifting their attention to what data pricing tools may use and what customers must be told. For businesses, the lessons are practical. If you use pricing software, know what data it learns from, and be wary of any tool that pools confidential information from your competitors. If you personalise prices, assume customers will find out and ask whether you could explain the practice to them comfortably. And remember the hardware store: the fairness norms that Kahneman and his colleagues documented in 1986 apply just as strongly to an algorithm as to a shopkeeper, and an algorithm can break them faster and at greater scale.

*Source: Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello, AER 110(10) (2020); Assad, Clark, Ershov & Xu, JPE 132(3) (2024); Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer, arXiv 2404.00806; US DOJ (Nov 24, 2025); Consumer Reports (Dec 2025); Skadden (May 2026); FTC (Aug 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the wheat merchant's dilemma** — in the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas asked a question that still troubles business ethics. A merchant brings wheat to a town where it is scarce and dear, knowing that other merchants are on their way with more. Must he tell the buyers, which would bring the price down? Aquinas concluded that he [need not, as a matter of justice](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/summa.SS_Q77_A3.html): selling at the price currently offered is not unjust. But if he did tell them, or lowered his price, that would be "exceedingly virtuous". The distinction between what is permitted and what is admirable is exactly the space in which modern pricing decisions, from surge fares to algorithmic rents, now sit. (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.77)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the 70-year nickel Coke** — a 6.5-ounce bottle of Coca-Cola cost five cents from 1886 until 1959, through two world wars, the Depression and decades of inflation. In a study in the *Journal of Money, Credit and Banking*, Daniel Levy and Andrew Young found [three reasons](https://econ.biu.ac.il/sites/econ/files/shared/people/LevyDaniel/jmcb4.pdf): long-term contracts that fixed the price of syrup to bottlers, the spread of coin-operated vending machines, and the simple fact that there was no convenient coin between a nickel and a dime (2004). A price that cannot move easily is a price that does not move. (JMCB, 2004)

**Price tags that change** — supermarkets are replacing paper price labels with electronic ones that can be updated in seconds. Walmart said in 2024 that it would [install digital shelf labels in 2,300 stores](https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2024/06/06/new-tech-better-outcomes-digital-shelf-labels-are-a-win-for-customers-and-associates) by 2026, arguing they save staff time. Critics fear they open the door to surge pricing at the grocery store. In July, New Jersey responded with a law that bans surveillance pricing for groceries and imposes a [one-year freeze on new electronic shelf labels](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/new-jersey-enacts-fair-price-protection-act-prohibiting-surveillance-pricing-for-groceries) from February while the state studies them. The price tag, invented to make prices the same for everyone, may become the battleground over whether they stay that way. (Walmart; Morgan Lewis)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The Book of Proverbs, compiled well over two thousand years ago, contains a verse that every salesperson will recognise: ["It is naught, it is naught, saith the buyer: but when he is gone his way, then he boasteth."](https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/Proverbs%2020:14) The buyer runs down the goods to get a better price, then goes home and brags about the bargain. Haggling has always been a game of hidden information, in which each side tries to learn more about the other than it reveals. The fixed price tag ended that game for a century and a half by putting the information in plain view. Algorithms threaten to restart it, with one difference: this time, only one side can see the cards. Whether prices stay fair will depend less on the technology than on whether companies, and the people who regulate them, decide that one price for everyone is still worth defending.
