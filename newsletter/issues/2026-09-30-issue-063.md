# The Daily Compass #063 — Who pays for progress

*Wednesday, September 30, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

Every argument about AI and work eventually reaches for history, and it usually reaches for the reassuring part. The looms came, the weavers grumbled, and within a generation there were more jobs than ever; the tractor emptied the farms and filled the factories; the spreadsheet killed the bookkeeper and hired the analyst. *Technology always creates more work than it destroys.* It is true in the long run, and it is the long run that the reassurance is really about. This issue goes back to the historical record and reads the part the reassurance skips: who absorbed the cost while the long run arrived, how long the arriving took, and what the people who resisted were actually resisting. The answers are more useful than the slogan. The losses were real and lasted a lifetime. The famous resisters were not afraid of machines. And the fight now forming around AI has an ingredient the earlier ones mostly lacked, which may be why it feels so bitter so early.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The unemployment the textbooks forgot

**What happened.** The economic historian Benjamin Schneider has put numbers on the biggest documented episode of technological unemployment in history, and it is not the one people usually cite. In ["Technological Unemployment in the British Industrial Revolution: The Destruction of Hand-Spinning"](https://academic.oup.com/past/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/pastj/gtae049/8109253) (*Past & Present*, February 2026), he reconstructs how many people spun yarn by hand before the spinning jenny and water frame arrived in the 1760s and 1770s. His estimate: around 1770, hand-spinning employed roughly 8% of the entire population, nearly all of them women and children, usually working at home to top up a family's income. Mechanization erased that work within a couple of decades. Drawing on more than 2,000 observations from contemporary commentators, county agricultural surveys and the 1834 Poor Law Commission's questionnaires, Schneider shows the damage began in the 1780s and was still visible in the mid-1830s. That is half a century of missing income in the households least able to lose it. The new factory jobs existed, but they were somewhere else, went to someone else, and came too late. (The [*Past & Present* blog post](https://pastandpresent.org.uk/a-historical-episode-of-job-destroying-technology-technological-unemployment-in-the-british-industrial-revolution/) is the accessible version.)

**Why it matters.** This is the rigorous answer to "technology always creates more jobs." In aggregate and eventually, yes. But "eventually" here meant fifty years, and the aggregate hid a transfer. The gains went to mill owners and consumers of cheap cloth, while the losses landed on rural women whose earnings had kept families above the poverty line. Economic histories long missed it because those women's work was informal and home-based, the kind of labor that rarely shows up in employment statistics. That should sharpen how we read today's reassurances about AI. The question was never whether a new technology raises total output. It is who carries the cost during the transition, how long the transition takes, and whether anyone is counting the people it passes over. On Schneider's evidence, the honest answer in the most famous industrial revolution was: the least visible people, for a lifetime, and almost nobody.

*Source: Schneider, Past & Present no. 270 (Feb 2026). ~45 min for the paper; ~8 for the blog post.*

### ◆ 2. The Luddites were not against machines

**What happened.** If the spinners are the forgotten losers of industrialization, the Luddites are its most misremembered resisters, and the misremembering is back in use. "Luddite" is again a political insult, thrown at anyone who suggests slowing AI's rollout. The writer Brian Merchant, whose book *Blood in the Machine* retells the 1811–1816 uprising, uses his newsletter essay ["Understanding the Luddites in the age of AI"](https://www.bloodinthemachine.com/p/understanding-the-luddites-in-the) to correct the record. The original Luddites were skilled textile workers, croppers and framework knitters, and many understood the machines better than the men who bought them. They did not smash frames because they feared technology. They smashed them because factory owners were using particular machines to cut wages, deskill the trade, get around long-standing rules on apprenticeship and quality, and flood the market with shoddy goods. Their targets were selective: the frames owned by employers who broke the rules. Parliament's answer was to make frame-breaking a capital crime in 1812 and to send thousands of troops into the manufacturing districts.

**Why it matters.** Getting the Luddites right changes the shape of the argument. If Luddism means "fear of the new," then anyone who objects to AI is simply behind the times, and the only real question is how quickly they will adapt. If Luddism means "a fight over who controls a technology and who captures its gains," then the objection is a bargaining position about distribution, which is a legitimate and normal part of how societies absorb new tools. Most of the rules that later made industrial work bearable (limits on hours, safety law, collective bargaining) came from people making exactly that kind of argument. None of this makes resistance automatically right or wise. It does mean that "Luddite" used as an insult is usually an attempt to avoid the distributional question by pretending the objection is about the machine. The useful question to ask of any automation plan is the Luddites' own: who decided this, and who gets the savings?

*Source: Merchant, Blood in the Machine (2026). ~12 min.*

### ◆ 3. Why this backlash feels different

**What happened.** The economists Martin Beraja (Berkeley) and Noam Yuchtman (LSE) asked a question that follows naturally from the first two briefs. Every transformative technology has provoked fears, so why does AI already provoke so much hostility, when polls find roughly half of American adults more concerned than excited about it, and before it has displaced workers at anything like the spinners' scale? In ["Why Is AI So Contentious?"](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-is-ai-so-contentious/), presented at the Brookings Papers on Economic Activity conference last week, they sort the objections into three kinds. *Economic* concerns (jobs and wages) and *existential* ones (loss of control) have clear precedents in the reactions to mechanization, electrification and nuclear power. The third has no real counterpart in earlier technologies: a *moral* grievance. They call it AI's "original sin." The systems were built by taking the writing, art, code and expertise of millions of people, without asking and without paying, and then selling back tools that compete with those same people.

**Why it matters.** This is where the history pays off. The spinners lost income to machines that were built with other people's inventions and capital. Today's writers, illustrators and programmers are being asked to compete with machines built partly out of their own work. That makes the grievance feel less like displacement and more like expropriation, which is closer to what the Luddites were actually angry about. It also explains why the standard policy menu feels thin. Retraining, wage insurance or even a universal basic income address lost income. They do not address the sense that something was taken. The paper is a conference draft and its framework will be argued over. But if its diagnosis is right, AI's legitimacy problem will not be solved by the size of the productivity gains alone. It will depend on whether the people whose work trained these systems come to see themselves as participants in the bargain rather than as its raw material.

*Source: Beraja & Yuchtman, BPEA conference draft (Sep 25, 2026). Draft; conclusions may change. ~40 min for the paper.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Keynes's grandchildren** — in 1930, with the Depression already underway, John Maynard Keynes wrote ["Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren,"](https://www.economicsnetwork.ac.uk/archive/keynes_persuasion/Economic_Possibilities_for_our_Grandchildren.htm) the essay that put the phrase "technological unemployment" into general use. He described it as a new disease: our ability to economize on labor outrunning our ability to find new uses for it. Then he looked a hundred years ahead, to 2030, and made two predictions. Living standards in rich countries would rise four- to eightfold, and the working week could shrink to about fifteen hours. He got the first roughly right and the second badly wrong: we took the gains mostly as more goods, not more time. His real point was about what comes after scarcity, when "the permanent problem" becomes how to live wisely and well. With 2030 now four years off and a new technology reviving his vocabulary, the essay reads less like an old forecast and more like an unanswered question addressed to us. (Keynes, 1930)

**🃏 WILDCARD: Aristotle's classroom, found** — at Mieza in northern Greece, where ancient sources say Aristotle tutored the teenage Alexander, the archaeologist Angeliki Kottaridi's team has now [uncovered the teaching halls](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/archaeologists-unearth-the-2400-year-old-school-where-aristotle-taught-alexander-to-be-great-180989581/) of the royal school Philip II built to train Macedon's next ruling class. The finds include a lecture hall with stone desks, bronze styluses, an exercise ground, and two banquet halls with colorful mosaic floors, the kind of decoration usually found in palaces. It is the physical setting of one of history's most famous educations. The world's most influential philosopher taught the boy who would conquer most of the known world, and neither made the other in his own image. (Excavation ongoing since 2024; 2026 season reported this month.)

**America, 1926, in the mirror** — Derek Thompson spent his summer inside *Recent Social Trends*, a forgotten 1,500-page government survey of 1920s America published in the early 1930s, and wrote up [the eerie rhymes](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/america-1926-an-absurdly-deep-dive). Then as now: a national birthday, a rising stock market shadowed by unease, public fear of "technological unemployment," and a new medium (radio then, social feeds now) remaking how people got their information. The report's authors returned again and again to one worry, "balance," between city and farm, factory and field, speed and tradition. The difference is that we know what came next for them. That knowledge is exactly why the survey is worth reading now. (derekthompson.org, 2026)

**Where's the intelligence explosion?** — the one current dispatch from the AI debate, and a useful dose of arithmetic. In a guest essay for Noahpinion, the technologist and novelist Ramez Naam [models the "recursive self-improvement" loop](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/wheres-the-intelligence-explosion) that fast-takeoff scenarios depend on, in which AI speeds up AI research, which speeds up AI. His reading of current data is that the loop exists but is far weaker than it would need to be to run away, and that capability has been growing roughly with the logarithm of computing power. Exponentially rising compute therefore buys something closer to steady, linear progress than an explosion. It is one model, not a proof. But it is a good corrective to the week's louder predictions, and a reminder that the question is measurable. (Noahpinion, Sep 2026)

Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook's three-part series on how the French Revolution turned into the Terror. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Walter Benjamin died eighty-six years ago this week, on September 26, 1940, at Portbou on the Spanish border, fleeing the Nazis. Among his last writings were the theses ["On the Concept of History,"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/benjamin/1940/history.htm) and the best-known of them describes a Paul Klee drawing he owned, the *Angelus Novus*. Benjamin read it as the angel of history. Its face is turned toward the past, where we see a chain of events, and it sees a single catastrophe piling wreckage at its feet. It would like to stay, wake the dead and make whole what has been smashed. But a storm is blowing from Paradise, caught in its wings so hard it cannot close them, driving it backward into a future it cannot see. "This storm," Benjamin wrote, "is what we call progress." It is not an argument against progress; the storm is also what gave the spinners' grandchildren cheap cloth and longer lives. It is an argument about where to look. The standard story of technology keeps its eyes on the future and on the average. Benjamin's angel keeps its eyes on the wreckage and on the particular people in it: the women whose spinning wheels went silent, the croppers hanged at York, the writers whose work became training data. We are about to find out which way AI's history is told. The spinners suggest the costs can be large and long. The Luddites suggest the resistance is usually about who pays. The Brookings paper suggests this time the grievance has a new edge. None of that settles whether the storm is worth it. It only asks us to look, as we go, at who is standing in it.
