# The Daily Compass #088 — What looks like a plateau

*Wednesday, October 28, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

On the evening of October 15, 1929, Irving Fisher of Yale, the most celebrated American economist of his day, spoke to the Purchasing Agents Association at a dinner in Manhattan. Stock prices, he said, had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau." He told his audience that rising earnings had largely justified the boom, and that he expected the market to be "a good deal higher than it is today within a few months." The *New York Times* ran the story the next morning under the headline "Fisher Sees Stocks Permanently High." Two weeks later the market crashed, and Fisher, who had borrowed heavily to buy shares, lost his fortune. His line became the most quoted forecast in financial history, usually shortened to "a permanently high plateau." The hedge in the original, "what looks like", has quietly dropped away in the retelling, which is itself a lesson: the world remembers our certainty, not our caveats. This issue is about overconfidence: why big projects nearly always overrun, why confident leaders are rewarded even when they are wrong, and what happens when the machines we consult turn out to share our habit of being surer than we should be.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The iron law of big projects

**What happened.** Last Friday the Australian government and Snowy Hydro announced the latest cost of Snowy 2.0, a giant pumped-hydro scheme in the Snowy Mountains: a target of A$22.2 billion, with A$25 billion allowed for, and completion pushed back to late 2029. When Malcolm Turnbull launched the project in 2017, the headline figure was about A$2 billion. The contract signed in 2019 was about A$5.1 billion, and a 2023 "reset" put the cost at A$12 billion, presented as a firm number. The newest estimate is [nearly double that](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/news/national/2026/10/23/snowy-2-0-cost-blowouts) and roughly eleven times the original. Snowy 2.0 is not unusual. Bent Flyvbjerg of Oxford, who has assembled a database of more than 16,000 projects in 136 countries, reports in *How Big Things Get Done* (2023, with Dan Gardner) that only 8.5% came in on both budget and schedule, and only 0.5% delivered on budget, on time and with the promised benefits. In a 2011 *Harvard Business Review* study of 1,471 IT projects, Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier found an [average overrun of 27%](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2229735) that hid a fat tail: one project in six was a "black swan", with cost overruns averaging 200%.

**Why it matters.** Daniel Kahneman described the underlying error from personal experience. In the 1970s he led a team writing a school curriculum and asked everyone to estimate how long it would take; the answers clustered around two years. He then asked a colleague with long experience of such projects how other teams had fared. About 40% never finished, the man admitted, and those that did took seven to ten years. Kahneman's own project took eight. The forecasts of the team members were built from the inside, from their plan and their enthusiasm; the colleague's came from the outside, from what actually happens to projects of that kind. Kahneman and Dan Lovallo later turned this into a practical method, reference-class forecasting: before estimating your own project, find a class of similar past projects and start from their actual distribution of outcomes. Flyvbjerg adds a harder point, that some forecasts are not merely optimistic but deliberately low to win approval. For any leader signing off a major investment, the most useful question is not "is this plan good?" but "what happened to the last twenty plans like it?"

*Source: Snowy Hydro / Australian government (Oct 23, 2026), via The New Daily; Flyvbjerg & Gardner, How Big Things Get Done (2023); Flyvbjerg & Budzier, HBR (Sep 2011); Kahneman & Lovallo, Management Science (1993). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. Confident at the top

**What happened.** Overconfidence is not a beginner's mistake; some of the best evidence for it comes from the people running large companies. For about a decade, Itzhak Ben-David, John Graham and Campbell Harvey asked American chief financial officers to forecast the next year's stock-market return and to give a range they were 80% sure would contain it. In ["Managerial Miscalibration"](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/managers-are-miscalibrated) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2013), drawing on more than 13,000 forecasts, they found that the actual return fell inside those 80% ranges only 36% of the time. The executives whose ranges were too narrow about the market were also too sure about their own firms, and their companies invested more and borrowed more. Ulrike Malmendier and Geoffrey Tate, measuring overconfidence by whether chief executives held on to their own company's stock options far longer than was prudent, found that such CEOs had [65% higher odds of making acquisitions](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:89:y:2008:i:1:p:20-43), especially diversifying deals, and that markets reacted worse to their bids. And among founders, a classic 1988 survey of nearly 3,000 new business owners by Arnold Cooper, Carolyn Woo and William Dunkelberg found that 81% rated their own chances of success at seven out of ten or better, and a third rated them ten out of ten.

**Why it matters.** If overconfidence is so costly, why does it persist? Research by Cameron Anderson, Don Moore and colleagues at Berkeley suggests one answer: people who think they are better than others tend to be granted higher status in groups, whether or not they are, and in follow-up studies they were not punished much even when their overconfidence was exposed. A paper published this year in *Psychological Review* by Chris Dawson of Bath and David de Meza of the LSE goes further, arguing that overconfidence works as a costly signal of ability and that, once statistical artefacts are removed, the most able people tend to be the most overconfident. That claim is contested, but the practical message is consistent: organisations reward confidence, so they get more of it than accuracy justifies. The remedies are structural rather than personal. Ask for ranges, not single numbers, and check them afterwards. Separate the people who propose an investment from the people who forecast its returns. And when someone is very sure, ask what they would expect to see if they were wrong.

*Source: Ben-David, Graham & Harvey, QJE 128(4) (2013); Malmendier & Tate, JFE 89(1) (2008); Cooper, Woo & Dunkelberg, Journal of Business Venturing (1988); Anderson et al., JPSP (2012); Dawson & de Meza, Psychological Review (2026). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 3. Machines that are surer than they should be

**What happened.** In 2017 a team at Cornell led by Chuan Guo showed in ["On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks"](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html) that as neural networks became deeper and more accurate, they also became overconfident: the probability they attached to their answers ran ahead of how often they were right. The question now matters far more, because millions of people take advice from chatbots. A working paper by Don Moore of Berkeley Haas, one of the leading researchers on human overconfidence, with Jacob Bien of USC and two Berkeley graduates, tested [11 leading large language models](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/research/ai-chatbots-are-overconfident-like-humans/) from several companies on whether they knew what they did not know. On average the models were overconfident, reporting about 88% confidence while being right about 79% of the time, according to a University of California summary. They also displayed a classic human pattern, the "hard-easy effect": most overconfident on difficult questions, and slightly underconfident on easy ones. Newer reasoning models were better calibrated, but not well calibrated. The paper is under review and not yet peer-reviewed. Meanwhile, a survey by the research firm Charter of more than 5,000 American office workers who use AI, [reported by Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/21/ai-trust-executives-survey) last week, found that 32.8% of executives said they trust AI more than their own judgment, about twice the share of individual contributors.

**Why it matters.** Put the two findings together and the risk becomes clear. AI systems tend to sound most sure precisely where they are most likely to be wrong, and the people with the most authority are the most inclined to defer to them. One of the survey's authors observed that AI suits the "seldom wrong, never in doubt" style some executives already have. Moore's view is that chatbots' confident fabrications are not an alien flaw but an echo of ordinary human overconfidence, possibly reinforced by training that rewards confident, helpful-sounding answers. For leaders, the response is the same as for confident colleagues. Ask the model for its uncertainty and its reasons, not just its answer. Treat fluent confidence on a hard question as a warning sign rather than a reassurance. And keep human judgment in the loop for exactly the decisions where the stakes, and the difficulty, are highest.

*Source: Guo et al., ICML (2017); Michael, BenShushan, Bien & Moore, working paper (2026), via Berkeley Haas; Charter survey, via Axios (Oct 21, 2026). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the qualifier that sank** — the *Titanic* is remembered as the ship advertised as "unsinkable". The record is subtler. In 1911 the trade journal *The Shipbuilder* described the ship's watertight compartments and remotely closed doors as making her "[practically unsinkable](https://www.snopes.com/history/titanic/unsinkable.asp)", and White Star Line material made similar claims; the company later insisted it had never promised more. "Practically unsinkable" was common shipbuilding language of the time. What the public carried away was the word without the hedge, much as Fisher's "what looks like" vanished from his forecast. Overconfidence is often a collective act: experts qualify, and audiences delete the qualification. (Snopes; Encyclopedia Titanica)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the oldest shipwreck, probably** — this month Turkish archaeologists announced a Bronze Age wreck off Kekova Island near Antalya, carrying more than 30 amphorae at a depth of about 30 metres. Headlines around the world called it the [world's oldest shipwreck](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/14/worlds-oldest-shipwreck-found-off-coast-of-turkiye), some 4,400 to 4,700 years old. The researchers themselves were more careful. No timber survives to be carbon-dated, so the age rests on the style of the pottery, and they describe the find as potentially the oldest known, pending further work; a peer-reviewed report calls its findings preliminary. It is a neat case of calibrated scientists and overconfident headlines. (Al Jazeera / Journal of Maritime Archaeology, Oct 2026)

**Overconfident in both directions** — overconfidence is not only boldness; sceptics can be too sure as well. In 2022 the Forecasting Research Institute asked domain experts and "superforecasters" to predict AI milestones. An interim accuracy check published in September found that both groups badly [underestimated progress](https://the-decoder.com/top-ai-experts-badly-underestimated-how-fast-the-field-is-moving-study-finds/): on four benchmark questions, experts gave the outcomes that occurred an average probability of about 25% and superforecasters about 10%, and AI reached gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad in July 2025, years before either group's median forecast. On some real-world measures, such as the spread of self-driving cars, forecasters had instead expected faster change than occurred. The lesson is not to trust optimists or pessimists, but to keep score. (Forecasting Research Institute, Sep 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

In the introduction to *The Descent of Man* (1871), Charles Darwin answered critics who insisted that the origin of humanity could never be known. "It has often and confidently been asserted, that man's origin can never be known," he wrote, "but ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge: it is those who know little, and not those who know much, who so positively assert that this or that problem will never be solved by science." The sentence is usually quoted as a jab at the ignorant, but it is also a description of how knowledge feels from the inside. The more we learn about a subject, the more we see its edges, its exceptions and the ways it can surprise us. That is why the best forecasters, engineers and leaders speak in ranges and keep a record of where they were wrong. Confidence is not the enemy; it is what lets us act. The danger is confidence that has stopped checking itself against the world. Fisher's plateau, the unsinkable ship and the two-year curriculum all share that flaw. The remedy is less glamorous than certainty: a habit of asking, before we commit, what the record says, and of admitting, afterwards, what we got wrong.
