# The Daily Compass #045 — The expensive brain

*Tuesday, September 1, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

There is a barnacle, *Sacculina*, that begins life with a brain and ends it without one. As a larva it needs the brain: it has to find a crab, judge it, and choose exactly where to attach. But once it has burrowed in and made the crab its home and its host, the brain is only a bill — an expensive, energy-hungry organ with nothing left to do — so the barnacle sheds it, dissolving into little more than a root system and a set of gonads. It does not merely stop using its intelligence. It *disposes* of it, because evolution is a ruthless accountant and a brain that has stopped earning its keep is a cost, not a trophy. We are living through the largest bet on intelligence in the history of the species — trillions of dollars, gigawatts of power, the reorganization of whole economies around the premise that more cognition is always and everywhere better. This issue is a quiet argument with that premise, made not by Luddites but by a barnacle, a venture capitalist, and an accountant's spreadsheet: intelligence is a cost, priced against its fit to the task, and the winning move is very often *less* of it, better aimed.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The barnacle that shed its brain

**What happened.** A lovely essay in Nautilus — ["The Virtue of Being Simple"](https://nautil.us/the-virtue-of-being-simple-1283320) — uses *Sacculina*, a parasitic barnacle, to attack one of our deepest unexamined assumptions: that more intelligence is always an improvement. The barnacle's free-swimming larva has a nervous system precisely because it faces a hard cognitive problem — locate a host crab, assess it, pick an attachment point. Once that problem is solved and the parasite is installed, the brain becomes pure overhead, and it is discarded; over evolutionary time, *Sacculina*'s ancestors were free-living barnacles that kept their brains into adulthood, and the lineage has been getting *simpler* — and more successful — ever since. Intelligence, here, is not the summit of a ladder. It is a tool the organism picks up for a job and puts down the moment the job is done.

**Why it matters.** This is the biological rebuke to intelligence-maximalism, and it lands hard in 2026. We have built an entire civilization-scale project on the axiom that cognition is a universal good — that a smarter model, a smarter worker, a smarter everything is a strict improvement, worth almost any cost. Evolution, which has been running the experiment for four billion years, comes to the opposite conclusion far more often than we'd like: intelligence is metabolically ruinous, it earns its keep only in environments that actually reward it, and where it doesn't, the ruthless move is to shed it. The barnacle isn't a cautionary tale about decline; it's a lesson in *fit* — it got exactly as simple as its niche allowed, and thrived. The question the age refuses to ask, and *Sacculina* poses without a word: for how many of the tasks we're frantically pouring intelligence into is the intelligence actually earning its enormous bill — and how many are crabs already caught?

*Source: Nautilus (Aug 4). ~11 min.*

### ◆ 2. You can't teach the founder

**What happened.** A sharp public disagreement about where competence actually comes from. Paul Graham argues that universities trying to produce founders are doing it backwards — ["How Universities Should Prepare Founders"](https://paulgraham.com/prepare.html) — and should stop building entrepreneurship centers and hiring deans of entrepreneurship, and instead pour that money into the people teaching the hard technical subjects: computer science, mechanical engineering, molecular biology. His claim is that founders are a *downstream* effect of deep domain competence — you get startups by making people genuinely, unusually good at something real, not by teaching them "entrepreneurship" as a portable skill. Bill Aulet of MIT, who literally wrote the system for teaching it, [pushed back](https://www.d-eship.com/articles/a-good-start-to-a-debate-but-not-the-final-answer/): the discipline *can* be taught, structured, and transmitted, and pretending otherwise romanticizes a process that has a real curriculum.

**Why it matters.** Strip the founders framing away and this is a fight about the same thing as brief 1: whether the valuable capacity is *general* — a transferable, teachable, maximizable "smarts" — or *specific*, grown slowly out of deep immersion in one hard domain and largely inseparable from it. Graham's wager is that the thing that matters can't be abstracted from the material; it lives in the details of a discipline, not in a meta-skill floating above them. That is exactly the capacity the current AI project is least able to shortcut and most eager to claim it can. It rhymes with a thread this newsletter keeps pulling (issue #033's "un-indexable," #039's "out of the lab"): the fungible, general, teachable version of a thing commoditizes toward zero, and what stays scarce is the grounded, particular competence that only comes from years inside the problem. You can teach the framework. You cannot teach the fifteen years of being wrong about the same material until you finally weren't.

*Source: Graham (late Aug); Aulet, d-eship.com (Aug 27). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Priced by the megawatt

**What happened.** And here is intelligence with its bill printed in dollars and watts. Tomasz Tunguz reframes a frontier AI lab as, quite literally, a factory — ["Revenue per Megawatt & The AI Model Factory"](https://theoryvc.com/blog-posts/revenue-per-megawatt-the-ai-model-factory) — one that "buys wholesale electricity by the megawatt and resells it as cognitive work." The proposed unit of measurement is *revenue per megawatt*: how much economic value a lab wrings out of each unit of raw power it converts into thinking. His striking claim is that the number has moved violently in the labs' favor — from something like negative-94% gross margins to on the order of $50 million of revenue per megawatt — not because the models got dramatically smarter, but because *serving* them got dramatically more efficient, squeezing more useful cognition out of each watt.

**Why it matters.** This is the barnacle's lesson written in the language of capital, and it quietly inverts the story everyone tells about the AI race. The metric that's actually improving, and actually determining who survives, isn't raw intelligence — it's the *cost-efficiency* of intelligence, the amount of useful work extracted per unit of the very real, very physical energy that thinking consumes. Machine cognition, it turns out, has a metabolism too, and the market is pricing it exactly the way evolution prices the barnacle's: not "how smart" but "how much value per calorie burned." It reframes the whole capex debate (issue #036) and the "nobody buys the best model" finding (issue #030) into a single hard number. The frontier lab that wins will not be the one with the most intelligence. It will be the one, like every efficient organism that ever lived, that gets the most done per watt — and knows which of its expensive faculties it can afford to shed.

*Source: Tunguz, Theory Ventures (Aug 30). ~9 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — after a month defined by the agent-swarm incident, the sharpest fresh idea is a governance one: Tyler Cowen's ["The Least Bad Way to Regulate AI"](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/the-least-bad-way-to-regulate-ai.html) proposes a private, non-profit self-regulatory body for AI labs — explicitly modeled on FINRA, the securities industry's own regulator — authorized and overseen by Washington but run so the labs are, within limits, their own first cops. It's an attempt to thread between "do nothing" and heavy statutory licensing, and whatever its flaws, it's an actual mechanism rather than another warning. (Aug 27)

**Machine, or "machine"?** — Cowen again, with a mental-model provocation worth holding — ["Anthropomorphizing AI?"](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/anthropomorphizing-ai.html) He grants that AI sentience is a category error, but warns the fashionable over-correction — a stern refusal to ever speak of a model's "goals" or "intent" — throws away a *useful* abstraction, the same way an economist models people "as if" they maximize utility while knowing they don't. Set it against Edsger [Dijkstra's 1985 broadside](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD09xx/EWD936.html) against anthropomorphism in computing — that treating machines as minds corrupts clear thinking — and you have a clean, live, forty-year argument about whether the "as if" clarifies or seduces. (Aug 31)

**The sheriff versus the lab** — a marker in the slow collision between the state and the frontier labs: a federal judge ruled on Aug 27 that the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a "supply-chain risk" was unlawful. Small in itself, but it's an early data point on a question this decade will litigate constantly — who actually holds authority over the companies building the most strategically consequential technology of the age, and by what process. ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/tech/anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-unlawful-hnk), Aug 27)

**⏳ TIMELESS: Simon's ant** — Herbert A. Simon, *The Sciences of the Artificial*, 1969. Simon asks you to watch an ant make its way home across a beach — veering around a pebble, up a dunelet, pausing, resuming — and to marvel at the intricate, purposeful-looking path. Then comes the deflation that founded a field: "an ant, viewed as a behaving system, is quite simple. The apparent complexity of its behavior over time is largely a reflection of the complexity of the environment." The intelligence you're admiring is mostly in the *beach*, not the ant. Why now: it is the sixty-year-old antidote to attributing genius to whatever produces clever-looking behavior — the founding caution of bounded rationality, and the exact question to ask of every dazzling model demo. How much of this is the mind, and how much is the shape of the sand it's crossing? Simple system, complex world, and we keep crediting the system. ([The Sciences of the Artificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sciences_of_the_Artificial), 1969)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the brain's other bill** — the megawatts of brief 3 are not abstract; they land in specific towns, and the pushback is getting real. On a recent Odd Lots, the writer Jasmine Sun reports from the ground of the data-center backlash — the residents of Wisconsin and Michigan communities organizing against the hyperscale builds arriving next door, over water, power prices, and the sheer scale of the things. It's the physical invoice for machine cognition, denominated not in tokens but in transformers and zoning fights — a reminder that every watt of thinking has to be *somewhere*, and someone lives there. ([Odd Lots](https://www.bloomberg.com/oddlots), Aug 24)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

The deepest assumption of the moment is so basic we rarely say it out loud: that intelligence is a good in itself, a thing you want more of the way you want more health or more freedom, with no natural ceiling and no real downside but cost. Almost nothing in nature agrees. Evolution treats intelligence as it treats every other expensive organ — as a wager that has to keep paying, subject to constant, merciless review, shed the instant the environment stops rewarding it. The barnacle gives up its brain and flourishes. The founder's edge turns out to live not in general brilliance but in the unglamorous depth of one hard domain. The frontier lab's survival hinges not on being the smartest but on being the most efficient per watt. And Simon's ant reminds us that much of what looks like intelligence is really just the shape of the world reflected in a simple thing moving through it. None of this is an argument against intelligence, which is glorious and has built everything we have. It's an argument against intelligence-*maximalism* — the unexamined faith that more is always better, always worth it, always the goal. The wiser question, the one the barnacle and the accountant and the ant all ask in their different languages, is the question of fit: not *how smart can this be?* but *how much intelligence does this actually need — and what is all the rest of it costing?* Sometimes the most intelligent thing a system can do is carry less brain.
