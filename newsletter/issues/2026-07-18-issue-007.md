# The Daily Compass #007 — The exponential and the accountant

*Saturday, July 18, 2026 · ~18 minute read*

A weekend issue built on collisions: the most-read AI-and-work writer says the agent era has arrived; the sharpest accountant on the internet says the unit economics already disproved it. IBM just watched a fifty-year moat evaporate in one earnings call, and an essayist explains why everyone you know suddenly wants to bake bread. Hold the contradictions — that's where the thinking is.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The exponential and the accountant

**What happened.** Ethan Mollick published ["The Twilight of the Chatbots"](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-twilight-of-the-chatbots): the prompt-check-reprompt era is ending, displaced by long-running agents that experts *manage* — Opus built 2–17 weeks of engineering work in 14 autonomous hours for $251 in tokens, and domain experience, not profession, now predicts who benefits. Dan Davies answered, in effect, with ["Tokenalysis and John Henry"](https://backofmind.substack.com/p/tokenalysis-and-john-henry): an accountant's dismantling of lab economics — 40-60% inference margins mean little when sales costs scale with revenue and the competitive equilibrium looks like airlines.

**The excerpts** — Mollick:

> "The instability is what happens when institutions that move at the speed of people (or worse, committees) try to track a capability curve that is very much not human in nature… We are very bad at feeling exponentials from the inside, and we are currently inside one."

Davies:

> "When large companies are telling their employees to be sensible and use AI tokens wisely, then the game is up. The race is over and John Henry won against the steam hammer. If you need a human being in the loop to decide on the allocation of AI tokens, then all those predictions of mass redundancy are gone."

**Why it matters.** These are the two best essays of the month, and they cannot both be fully right. Mollick's frame says the turbulence is structural — human-speed institutions tracking an inhuman curve, gap only widening. Davies's frame says the price system has already voted: if intelligence were truly about to replace labor wholesale, nobody would be *rationing* it through human judgment. The synthesis worth holding: capability curves and unit economics are different curves, and most 2026 arguments fail by reading one as if it were the other.

*Sources: Mollick (Jun 30); Davies (mid-July). ~25 min together.*

### ◆ 2. IBM's moat, Hirschman's exit

**What happened.** IBM suffered the worst stock day in its 115-year public history as mainframe sales faltered. Ben Thompson's read in [the weekly Stratechery roundup](https://stratechery.com/2026/mainframes-and-main-characters/): this isn't cyclical — "the real concern for IBM is that AI's ability to port the essential backend programs that run on archaic technology will mean those missed sales never come back." The most durable switching costs in enterprise computing — decades of COBOL nobody dared touch — are dissolving because agents can now do the porting (recall the Bun rewrite from issue #001: the playbook already exists).

**The excerpt** — Albert Hirschman's 1970 classic *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (via [Peter Levine's explainer](https://sites.tufts.edu/civicstudies/2022/01/27/exit-voice-and-loyalty/)), the week's real skeleton key:

> "Loyalty is praiseworthy if a flawed group has the potential to improve, because groups are valuable resources. But loyalty is problematic if the group is too unjust, because then remaining a member constitutes complicity."

**Why it matters.** Hirschman's frame — every member of a declining institution chooses between *exit* (leave) and *voice* (stay and push), with loyalty buying time for voice — ties the week together. IBM's customers just gained exit after fifty years of enforced loyalty; watch every legacy-lock-in franchise get repriced on the same logic. The frontier labs are living the inverse: the talent wars collapsed the cost of exit (400+ ex-Apple employees at OpenAI; researchers hopping labs quarterly), and per Hirschman, cheap exit *starves institutions of voice* — the people who would have argued for change leave instead. Cheap exit everywhere may be the deepest structural fact of the AI economy.

*Sources: Thompson (Jul 17); Hirschman (1970). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Why everyone suddenly wants to make things by hand

**What happened.** Aeon published Joshua Habgood-Coote's essay on "skill nostalgia" — [the contemporary hunger](https://aeon.co/essays/the-radical-reasons-why-you-dream-of-making-things-by-hand) for beekeeping, sourdough, calligraphy, workwear — traced from the Spitalfields silk weavers through Ruskin and Morris to today's AI-terrified craft revival, ending on Pope Leo XIV's 2026 encyclical warning that AI will "de-skill workers."

**The excerpt:**

> "When earlier periods of skilled practice are treated as more fully human, the social order that produced them can easily appear better than what replaced it. The past, then, seems superior to the present. This is why such arguments risk becoming reactionary. When we mourn the uninvention of skills, we may also — unless we are very careful — end up mourning the loss of the social structures that surround them."

**Why it matters.** This is the missing half of the delegation debate. Issue #006's Bartleby Option asked what you should *refuse* to offload; Habgood-Coote explains what the refusal reaches for — and offers the crucial distinction between the *reactionary* strain (the past as destination: Heidegger, "Make America Skilled Again") and the *radical* strain (the past as calibration for better hopes about work). As agents absorb execution (brief 1), the craft impulse will only intensify; the question is which strain of it wins. His answer is the useful one: use the weavers to sharpen what you want from the future, not to move into the past.

*Source: Joshua Habgood-Coote, Aeon (Jul 6). ~20 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The frontier, compressed** — Gemini 3.5 Pro missed its third deadline; July 17 reporting says the rebuilt model fell short on coding and long-horizon reasoning and slips "by several months," with Google weighing a stopgap (still zero official statements). Meanwhile the actual frontier story came from China: Moonshot's open-weight **Kimi K3**, which Zvi calls a candidate for "another DeepSeek Moment" — a point ahead of Opus 4.8 on Artificial Analysis, three behind Fable. (Zvi, [AI #177 Part 2](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-177-part-2-wish-you-were-here), Jul 17)

**Faking versus making** — Dan Davies revives sociologist Harry Collins's imitation game (a jury of physicists once judged Collins, a sociologist, the *real* gravity-wave physicist) to ask whether LLMs have "interactional expertise" — talking sensibly about a field — or "contributory expertise": doing original work in one. The sturdiest frame yet for the does-it-understand debate. (Dan Davies, [Back of Mind](https://backofmind.substack.com/p/faking-versus-making), Jul 3)

**Neither master nor slave, but steward** — The New Atlantis's Brian Boyd argues an embodied general agent would be "in a partial but real sense, an artificial form of life," and that the right stance is neither subjugation nor the worship he predicts many will offer: "How ought we to respond to such a being? …neither as its master nor as its slave, but as its steward." (Brian J. A. Boyd, [The New Atlantis](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/will-ai-be-alive), 2025)

**Systemic fragility, Wuhan edition** — new open-source reporting: in May 2019, fires destroyed two of Hubei's three licensed hazardous-waste disposal firms weeks apart, leaving Wuhan's entire virology network one remote option. Not an attribution claim — a fragility claim: "It was not home to the institutional transparency, regulatory enforcement, and infrastructure redundancy that research of that nature demands." ([The New Atlantis](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/a-new-piece-of-the-puzzle-of-covids-origin), Summer 2026)

**The mitochondrial theory of mind** — Quanta profiles Columbia's Martin Picard, who argues energy flow, not genes, is the consilience point for health, mood, maybe consciousness: "If the energy stops flowing, there's no more you." Patients with mitochondrial disease pay "a 180-calorie tax every day of life." (Rachel Nuwer, [Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/martin-picards-mitochondrial-theory-of-mind-20260717/), Jul 17)

**Spoiled is the goal** — Morgan Housel, from Adam Smith's Scotland (where mothers lost eighteen of twenty children) to your kids: each generation's comfort isn't a side effect of the last one's toil, it was the point — progress just swaps lower-order worries for higher ones. Reframes every "entitled generation" complaint, including the AI-abundance version. (Morgan Housel, [Collab Fund](https://collabfund.substack.com/p/long-term-money), Apr 2026)

**"There is no such thing as an A player in the abstract"** — Graham Duncan's classic on people-judgment: your biases co-create every interview, references beat interviews 5-to-1, and "if you first see their dysfunction, you should know you have yet to find their genius, and vice versa." As teams shrink and leverage-per-hire explodes, this is the highest-ROI essay an operator can reread. (Graham Duncan, [grahamduncan.blog](https://grahamduncan.blog/whats-going-on-here/))

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Machine Stops** — Forster, 1909: humanity in underground cells, every need met by the Machine, all contact through glowing plates — "I see something like you in this plate, but I do not see you." Why now: this week's Hard Fork features a woman in rural Washington relying on an AI companion robot, and a segment on culture collapsing into "taste slop." Forster annotated it 117 years early: "Something 'good enough' had long since been accepted by our race." (E.M. Forster, [full text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Machine_Stops), 1909)

**🃏 WILDCARD: You probably own this 7-Eleven** — The New Atlantis walks through a Virginia convenience store you likely part-own via your 401(k), tracing how REITs, triple-net leases, and zoning turned the built environment into "an industrial-scale investment product." The punchline, from an interviewed economist: "they're all pork bellies… the most replaceable, the most boring assets actually can often have the highest value on the market." Placelessness as policy, not fate. ([The New Atlantis](https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/you-probably-own-this-7-eleven), Summer 2026)
