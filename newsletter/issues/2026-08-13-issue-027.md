# The Daily Compass #027 — Revealed preference

*Thursday, August 13, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Professed belief is cheap. What you actually believe shows up somewhere more expensive — in the position you hold, the institution you rebuild, the subject you can't stop returning to. Today, three views of that gap: an economist's test for whether you truly fear AI (you'd be buying the puts, and you're not), a military historian's test for whether a war has really changed (the schools change, or it hasn't), and a prolific writer's involuntary confession that one subject has eaten his entire output. Behind them stands George Kennan, whose most famous idea was not an argument but a behavior — a belief about an adversary's insides, enacted patiently across forty years.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Buy the puts or admit you don't believe it

**What happened.** Tyler Cowen turned a decision-theory lens on the AI-doom discourse in ["Mistakes in Financial Economics."](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/mistakes-in-financial-economics.html) His own view: the median case is that AI massively boosts the economy (so shorting it is foolish), but there's a non-trivial extinction tail (against which shorting is also pointless — you won't be around to collect). The clean resolution for anyone who genuinely holds a high p(doom): stay long the portfolio but buy cheap, far-out-of-the-money puts, which pay off in the bad-but-not-terminal scenarios. His pointed observation is that almost none of the loudest AI doomers actually do this.

**Why it matters.** This is the market's version of a lie detector. Revealed preference — what your money does, not what your posts say — is the hardest belief to fake, and Cowen is quietly calling a large bluff: if you were as sure as you sound, your portfolio would show it, and it doesn't. That cuts more than one way, and it's not a gotcha so much as a discipline, the same one this newsletter arrived at from other doors — #024's judgment made accountable, #025's "act like it is science" demand for a falsifiable number. A belief you've taken no position on, in any currency — money, time, reputation, career — is a belief you should suspect you don't really hold. The test isn't whether you can argue for doom. It's whether you've bought the puts.

*Source: Cowen, Marginal Revolution (Aug 12). ~5 min.*

### ◆ 2. When war changes, schools change

**What happened.** In War on the Rocks, Brig. Gen. Matthew Tracy and Benjamin Jensen made the institutional version of the same argument — ["The Next School of War."](https://warontherocks.com/the-next-school-of-war-educating-for-tomorrows-battlefield/) Their template is Gerhard von Scharnhorst, who after Prussia's catastrophe against Napoleon concluded that the deepest reform wasn't new weapons or new tactics but a new way of *educating* officers — continuous, rigorous, lifelong. Their claim for 2026: AI-enabled drones, multi-domain war, and the collapse of the old planning cycles have changed war again, and a military that hasn't rebuilt its schools hasn't actually absorbed that — whatever its doctrine documents say.

**Why it matters.** An institution's revealed preference is its curriculum. You can tell what an organization truly believes has changed not from its press releases but from what it now teaches its own people, because the school is where a belief gets paid for in the scarcest currency an institution has: the time and formation of its next generation. It's the same test as brief 1, scaled up from a portfolio to an academy. And it generalizes past the military to every organization in this newsletter's beat — the AI-era firm that says everything is different but still onboards, promotes, and trains exactly as before has told you, in the one language that can't lie, that it doesn't really believe its own memo. Scharnhorst's insight was that you lose the next war in the classroom of the last one. The schools are the tell.

*Source: Tracy & Jensen, War on the Rocks (Aug 12). ~18 min.*

### ◆ 3. The subject that ate the syllabus

**What happened.** Zvi Mowshowitz, whose weekly AI roundups are among the most exhaustive documents in the field, published his ["Monthly Roundup #45"](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/monthly-roundup-45-august-2026) — his standing venue for everything *other* than AI (fertility, housing, education, the small pleasures) — and noted, half-ruefully, that every single post he'd written since the last monthly had been primarily about AI. He voiced a wish to get back to the other subjects "if things are not fully on fire," and the structure of his own output answered him: they are.

**Why it matters.** This is revealed preference as an involuntary confession — not a bet placed but an agenda hijacked. Zvi did not *decide* that AI should crowd out everything else he finds worth thinking about; the acceleration decided it for him, and his archive is the honest record. It's the most human version of the issue's idea: when the ground moves fast enough, it doesn't just change your answers, it monopolizes your questions, and you notice only in retrospect, from the shape of what you actually produced. Every reader of this newsletter is running the same experiment on a smaller scale — check what genuinely occupied your attention this month against what you'd have said your interests were. The gap is the measurement. And the uncomfortable corollary is that a subject doesn't need to win the argument to win your life. It only needs to keep being on fire.

*Source: Mowshowitz, Don't Worry About the Vase (Aug 12–13). ~15 min.*

**AI-backed securities** — Matt Levine points his oldest frame at the newest boom: "the basic business of finance is slicing up cash flows… you put the stuff in a box, the box issues securities," and the box is now full of data-center and AI-compute cash flows. The financialization of the buildout (issue #026's unmarked risk) proceeds on schedule — the risk isn't disappearing, it's being tranched. ([Money Stuff](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-08-12/ai-backed-securities), Aug 12; paywalled)

**Nobel before Pulitzer** — a provocation making the rounds via 3QD: AI will plausibly win a Nobel-level scientific result before it writes a Pulitzer-level novel, because science rewards *closing* well-specified problems (the machine's strength) while literature rewards *opening* them (still ours). The exact seam from issue #020's rejected NeurIPS papers, stated as a testable race. ([3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/anthropics-felix-rieseberg-why-ai-wins-a-nobel-before-a-pulitzer.html), ~Aug 12)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Sources of Soviet Conduct** — George Kennan ("X"), Foreign Affairs, 1947. The essay that named containment — the argument that a rival's behavior flows from its internal structure, so the right response is not decisive confrontation but "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant" shaping of its environment until the structure itself changes. Why now: it is the purest historical case of this issue's idea — a strategy that was a *belief enacted over decades*, not a slogan declared once, and the live US–China "is this containment, and should it be?" debate keeps reaching for it. Kennan's genius was to make a forecast about an adversary's insides and then build forty years of patient behavior on top of it. ([Foreign Affairs, via Norton archive](https://www.wwnorton.com/college/history/archive/resources/documents/ch31_02.htm), 1947)

**🃏 WILDCARD: why rivers are so mathematical** — 3QD on Hack's law (1957): the length of a stream scales with its drainage area raised to about the 0.6 power, a clean fractional exponent that holds across creeks and continental river systems alike. Nobody designed it; the water and the sediment negotiated it. A small, lovely reminder that some of the deepest order in the world is enacted, not decreed — revealed preference, geology's version. ([3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/why-are-rivers-so-mathematical.html), Aug 12)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

A last turn of the same screw, from 3 Quarks Daily's [defense of the middlebrow](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2026/08/why-we-should-learn-to-embrace-the-middlebrow.html). Virginia Woolf, in a 1932 letter so furious she never sent it, wrote that if anyone dared call her "middlebrow" she would take her pen and stab him — and the heat of that refusal is itself a revealed preference, a map of the hierarchy she actually carried. The essay's quieter point is that the lowbrow long ago got reclaimed and celebrated while the middlebrow — the earnest, the improving, the merely good — is still the thing the cultured profess to despise and privately consume. It rhymes with everything above: what we say we value and what we actually reach for are two different ledgers, and the second one is the true one. The doomer's portfolio, the army's curriculum, the writer's archive, a reader's honest afternoon — each is a confession written in the only ink that doesn't wash out. Better to read your own ledger on purpose than to be surprised, later, by what it says you believed.
