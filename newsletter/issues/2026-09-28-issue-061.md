# The Daily Compass #061 — The hockey stick that wasn't

*Monday, September 28, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Growth is the water business swims in — the word on every deck, the axis on every chart, the promise in every pitch. And almost everything we believe about it is subtly, expensively wrong. We believe there is a *playbook* — that somewhere in the accumulated wisdom of Lean Startup and PLG and the growth canon is a repeatable recipe. We believe in the *hockey stick* — that real success curves upward exponentially, and anything slower is failure. We believe, lately, that in the age of AI the old arithmetic of customers and costs has been suspended. This Monday issue takes a cold shower to all three. Its three briefs — from a retired VC who taught the subject for fifteen years, a two-time unicorn founder with a spreadsheet, and a venture investor doing the unit-economics math on the AI era — converge on an unglamorous truth: growth is not a playbook you run or a curve you're owed, but a physics of convergence, saturation, and payback that the mythology exists precisely to obscure. And the deepest lesson comes, as it often does, from evolutionary biology.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The playbook that eats itself

**What happened.** Jerry Neumann — a retired venture investor who spent fifteen years teaching entrepreneurship at Columbia — has written the most bracing thing about startups this year, and it is an act of demolition. In ["We Have Learned Nothing,"](https://colossus.com/article/we-have-learned-nothing-startup-pundits/) he observes that after twenty-five years of ever-more-sophisticated startup pedagogy — Lean Startup, product-market fit, growth playbooks, the whole apparatus — founders are *no more likely to succeed than before*. His explanation is not that the advice is bad but that its very success destroys it, via what he calls a Red Queen dynamic: once a method is widely known, everyone adopts it, everyone converges on the same answers, and everyone therefore builds the same undifferentiated company — at which point the method confers no advantage and most of those look-alike companies fail. A playbook, by becoming common knowledge, cancels itself. Neumann follows the logic all the way down, to a conclusion that includes his own essay: a real science of entrepreneurship would have to be so honest about this that it refused to be systematized into yet another recipe.

**Why it matters.** This is a scalpel to the whole business-advice industry, and its blade cuts well past startups. Any edge that can be fully written down and widely read stops being an edge the moment enough people read it — in investing, in careers, in strategy. The value was never in *knowing the playbook*; by the time it's a playbook, the value has been arbitraged away. What's left, Neumann argues, is the part that *can't* be systematized: judgment, taste, the willingness to do the specific thing the recipe doesn't cover because it can't. This is deflating if you were hoping to buy success in book form, and liberating if you've suspected all along that the confident frameworks were selling a false certainty. The uncomfortable takeaway for anyone building anything: the more your plan looks like everyone else's best practice, the more surely it has already been priced out of working.

*Source: Neumann, Colossus (Mar 2026). ~25 min; a proper essay.*

### ◆ 2. Almost nothing grows exponentially

**What happened.** If brief 1 kills the playbook, this one kills the shape we imagine success makes. The two-time unicorn founder Jason Cohen, in a longform A Smart Bear essay, ["The Myth of Exponential Hypergrowth,"](https://longform.asmartbear.com/exponential-growth/) makes a claim that sounds heretical and is merely arithmetic: *almost no company actually grows exponentially.* What founders and investors breathlessly call "exponential" is, on inspection, *quadratic* (you add a roughly constant increment each period — 10, then 20, then 30) or *logistic* (an S-curve that accelerates, then saturates). Cohen walks the real growth curves of HubSpot, Facebook, Apple, and others and shows the same structure every time: not one smooth exponential rocket but a *stack of S-curves* — a series of campaigns, products, and market expansions, each of which ramps and then flattens, laid end to end into what only resembles exponential growth from a distance. True exponential growth, where the increment itself compounds without limit, essentially doesn't occur in the wild for long.

**Why it matters.** The exponential expectation is not just wrong; it is actively destructive, because it misreads *health* as *failure*. A company adding solid, constant increments — genuinely succeeding — will look like it's "decelerating" against an exponential yardstick, and its board will panic, its leaders will chase reckless swings to bend the line back up, and a good business will be managed into the ground for failing to be an impossible one. Cohen's reframe hands you a better instrument: figure out which curve you're actually on, recognize that every S-curve saturates, and grow by *deliberately stacking the next curve* — the next product, market, or channel — before the current one flattens, rather than praying the current one defies mathematics. It's the difference between expecting a miracle and engineering a sequence. Growth, done honestly, is a relay of finite sprints, not one infinite one.

*Source: Cohen, A Smart Bear (evergreen; undated on-page). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. The customer sets the clock

**What happened.** The third myth is the newest: that in the age of AI, the boring unit economics of customers no longer matter. The venture investor Kyle Harrison takes the opposite view in ["The Mega-Million Dollar Customer,"](https://investing101.substack.com/p/the-mega-million-dollar-customer) and does the math. Using fresh churn benchmarks, he shows how a customer's *payback clock* silently determines the shape of an entire company: small, self-serve customers (under ~$10K a year) churn at 3–5% a month — 31–46% a year — and must pay back in 6–9 months, which forces a company built for velocity and volume; enterprise customers ($100K+) churn under 1% a month and pay back over 18–24 months, which permits a company built for depth and patience. Who your customer is sets your sales comp, your support model, your hiring ramp, your whole metabolism. And his sharpest point cuts against the AI exceptionalism: because AI is *not* free to serve — every token, every inference, every agent loop costs real money, dragging AI-native gross margins toward ~50% versus classic SaaS's 75–85% — the old disciplines of churn, payback, and net retention don't become quaint in the AI era. They become *more* binding, not less.

**Why it matters.** This is the ground truth under the other two briefs. Neumann says the playbook won't save you and Cohen says the curve won't, and Harrison says here is what actually governs you instead: the arithmetic of how much a customer is worth, how long they stay, and what they cost to serve. It's a useful corrective to a specific 2026 delusion — that AI's magic dissolves economics — when in fact thinner margins make economics the whole game. But the deeper point generalizes into a diagnostic you can run on any business, including one you're thinking of joining or funding: *what does this company's customer force it to be?* The payback clock is destiny. Read it, and most of the strategy questions answer themselves; ignore it, and no growth playbook or hockey-stick dream will save you from the compounding math running quietly underneath.

*Source: Harrison, Investing 101 (~Sep 21, 2026). ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the Red Queen** — the principle Neumann borrows is one of the great ideas in evolutionary biology, and it is worth having whole. In 1973 the paleontologist Leigh Van Valen proposed "a new evolutionary law" to explain a puzzle: why the odds of a species going extinct don't fall the longer it has already survived. His answer, named for the Red Queen in *Through the Looking-Glass* — *"it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place"* — is that every species is locked in constant coevolution with its competitors, predators, and prey, all of them adapting at once, so that any improvement is immediately met by the improvements of everyone else. You run flat out simply to hold your position; stop, and you fall behind. Transplanted to markets, it is the deepest frame in this issue: a competitive edge is never a possession you bank but a lead you must keep re-earning, because the moment your advantage is legible, everyone copies it and the ground resets. This is why there is no permanent playbook (brief 1) and no growth curve that runs forever unchallenged (brief 2). The race has no finish line. That is not a bug in competition; it *is* competition. ([Van Valen, "A New Evolutionary Law," 1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Queen_hypothesis))

**🃏 WILDCARD: why the monoculture is fragile** — a brand-new physics result that turns out to be Neumann's thesis in another language. A Northwestern team led by Adilson Motter, publishing in *Science* this month, demonstrates a counterintuitive principle they call [disorder-promoted stability](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260919031022.htm): carefully balanced *heterogeneity* — disorder — can make a network (a power grid, an ecosystem, a set of coupled oscillators) *more* stable than a perfectly uniform, symmetric one. Our intuition says uniformity equals robustness; the math says the opposite, which may be why natural networks are so messily various. Set it beside brief 1 and it shivers: when every founder runs the identical playbook, an industry becomes a monoculture — perfectly uniform, and therefore, per Motter, perfectly *fragile*, all its members exposed to the same shock at once. Difference isn't just where advantage hides; it may be what keeps the whole system standing. The homogeneity a universal playbook produces isn't only un-competitive. It's unstable. (Science, Sep 2026)

**Say "vendor financing," not "circular"** — the most useful reframe in the AI-bubble discourse, and a hype-deflator that cuts against the panic for once. As alarm mounts over the "circular" deals wiring the AI build-out together — Nvidia funding OpenAI, which buys Nvidia chips, on and on — the economist Noah Smith [pushes back](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/should-we-worry-about-ais-circular): this is not dot-com-style fraudulent round-tripping. The money is real, the chips exist, the contracts are public; a supplier with the cheapest capital on earth financing a capital-hungry customer is textbook *vendor financing*, a century-old practice that built the railroads and telecom. That doesn't make it safe — the honest question, Smith grants, is whether AI revenue growth outpaces the debt stack being built to bet on it, and critics counter that vendor financing can manufacture the very demand it claims to serve. But "circular," said with a smirk, is an accusation masquerading as an analysis. The real risk isn't the shape of the money; it's whether the revenue shows up. (Noahpinion, 2026)

**Compounding vs. the step-change** — a clean lens from Byrne Hobart's [*The Rocketship*](https://www.thediff.co/archive/the-rocketship/): the biggest companies grow in two very different ways, and confusing them wrecks forecasts. One is *slow compounding* — a core business quietly getting a bit better and bigger every year, the tortoise that wins on time. The other is the *step-function* — a discrete new revenue line or capability that resets the trajectory overnight. Most great outcomes are a "close race" between the two, and the strategic question for any builder is which engine they're actually running: are you compounding a core, or waiting on a step-change — and are you managing (and valuing) yourself accordingly? It pairs with Cohen: the step-functions are how you stack the next S-curve before the last one flattens. (The Diff, May 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Set the three briefs together and the mythology of growth quietly collapses into something truer and more useful. There is no playbook, because any playbook widely known cancels itself (Neumann). There is no exponential, because real growth is a relay of finite S-curves that each saturate and must be deliberately re-stacked (Cohen). And there is no escape from the arithmetic, because who your customer is and what they cost to serve sets the metabolism of the whole enterprise, AI or not (Harrison). Underneath all three runs the Red Queen: an advantage is never banked, only rented, and the rent is due every day, because everyone else is running too. The economist Herbert Stein once compressed half of this into a law — *if something cannot go on forever, it will stop* — and every exponential dream eventually meets it. But notice that the picture this leaves is not bleak; it's *actionable*. If growth is convergence-prone, then differentiate — do the thing the playbook can't specify. If growth is a stack of S-curves, then stop praying for a miracle and start engineering the next curve before the current one flattens. If growth is governed by unit economics, then read the payback clock and build the company your customer actually requires. The hockey stick was always a story we told to make a hard, finite, competitive process feel like destiny. Put the story down, and what's left is better: not a promise of exponential magic, but a craft — the patient, un-systematizable, endlessly re-run work of staying one step ahead in a race with no finish line.
