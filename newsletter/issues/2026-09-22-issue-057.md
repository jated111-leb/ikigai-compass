# The Daily Compass #057 — How would we know?

*Tuesday, September 22, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

Here is a small, uncomfortable exercise. Take the last confident claim you made about something that mattered — a strategy, a hire, a market call, a belief about whether some tool is making you faster — and ask what would have to be true for it to be wrong, and whether you have ever actually checked. Most of us run our lives and our companies on a surprising amount of evidence we have never audited: numbers we half-remember, stories that sound right, self-reports we trust because they are our own. This issue is about the gap between a claim that *sounds* true and one that would *survive being checked* — and it walks that gap through three domains where the evidence we lean on quietly betrays us: the strategy memo, the history textbook, and the productivity dashboard. It closes with an old and generous idea about why, even so, a certain amount of miscalculation may be exactly what lets us get anything done at all. The thread is not cynicism. It is the one question that separates a belief from a bet: *how would I know if I'm wrong?*

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The strategy that fails its own test

**What happened.** Roger Martin — the strategist behind *Playing to Win*, about as close to a marquee name as the discipline has — used his newsletter this month to do something bracingly simple: propose an *audit* for any strategy argument. His essay, ["The Logic of Strategy Arguments,"](https://rogerlmartin.substack.com/p/the-logic-of-strategy-arguments) sets a two-part bar. A serious strategy claim must present *explicit logic* — a chain of reasoning you can inspect — *and* attached *data* that bears on it. When a claim offers neither, Martin says, it has simply failed, and you should treat it as failed no matter how credentialed the source or how fashionable the framing. His running example is a critique of the push (associated with Rita McGrath) to retire the old strategy frameworks in favor of intangible-asset-based ones; his point is not that the new view is wrong but that, as argued, it clears neither bar. And the sting in the tail is aimed at the reader: the experts and their gatekeepers — journals, conferences, the whole apparatus of authority — *cannot be counted on to do this verification for you*. You have to run the audit yourself.

**Why it matters.** This is a mental model disguised as a book review, and it generalizes far past strategy. Most of what circulates as insight in business — the confident deck, the thought-leader post, the framework with a memorable 2x2 — survives on *fluency* rather than *logic plus evidence*, because fluency is what gets rewarded and almost no one applies the test. Martin's audit is deflationary in the best way: it does not ask whether an idea is exciting or whether a smart person said it, only whether it exposes its reasoning and shows its data. Apply it honestly for a week and a startling amount of what you took for analysis turns out to be *assertion with good production values*. The discipline is not to become a cynic who believes nothing; it is to notice, every time, whether the thing in front of you is a claim you could check or a claim you are being asked to admire.

*Source: Martin, "A New Way to Think" / Substack (Sep 14, 2026). ~9 min.*

### ◆ 2. The tariff that didn't

**What happened.** Move the same audit from the boardroom to the history book, and a live political argument falls apart. The Dartmouth trade historian Douglas Irwin — author of *Clashing over Commerce*, the standard history of US trade policy — gave a long-form Q&A to AEI, ["Did Tariffs Make America Great?",](https://www.aei.org/economics/did-tariffs-make-america-great-a-long-read-qa-with-trade-historian-douglas-a-irwin/) that dismantles one of the most-repeated claims in current economic politics: that the high tariffs of the late nineteenth century *built* American industrial might, and therefore that tariffs can do so again. Irwin's verdict is blunt — *it is difficult to make the case that high import tariffs were an important factor driving late-nineteenth-century US growth.* The real engines were abundant natural resources, a fast-growing population, and an unusual openness to immigrants, capital, and ideas. The tariff-greatness story, he argues, is a textbook case of *post hoc ergo propter hoc* — the economy grew *while* tariffs were high, so the tariffs must have *caused* the growth — propped up by statistics that don't survive scrutiny.

**Why it matters.** This is the historian's version of Martin's audit, and it lands on something a policy claim shares with a strategy claim: correlation wearing the costume of causation. "It happened alongside X, therefore X did it" is perhaps the most seductive bad argument in public life, precisely because the correlation is usually *real* — the tariffs genuinely were high, the growth genuinely did happen. What's missing is the counterfactual and the mechanism: *would growth have been slower without them, and through what channel?* When Irwin supplies those, the story inverts — America may have grown impressively *despite* its tariffs, on the strength of the very openness the tariffs worked against. The lesson travels well past trade policy. Any time a nation, a company, or a person credits its success to the most visible feature of its past, ask what else was true at the same time — and whether the thing being praised was the cause, the coincidence, or the cost.

*Source: Irwin, AEI (May 19, 2026). ~18 min.*

### ◆ 3. The number that dissolved

**What happened.** Now the hardest case, because here the audit turns on the auditors themselves. In 2025, the research group METR produced what may have been the single cleanest measurement in all of AI: a randomized controlled trial finding that experienced open-source developers were *19% slower* when allowed to use AI coding tools — while *believing* they'd been about 20% faster, a nearly forty-point gap between the stopwatch and the feeling. It became the most-cited piece of hype-deflation in the field. The twist arrived quietly in [METR's February 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/): the clean result did not so much reverse as *dissolve*. A small group of returning developers now measured faster; a larger group of new ones measured slightly slower; the confidence intervals sprawled across zero. And the deepest finding was methodological: METR concluded they can *no longer reliably run the experiment at all*, because developers have become so dependent on these tools that few will accept being placed in the no-AI control group. The instrument broke on contact with the thing it was built to measure.

**Why it matters.** This is the most honest data point in AI, and it refuses to comfort anyone. It denies the skeptic the tidy "AI makes you slower" headline and denies the booster the "10x overnight" one; what it leaves is harder to tweet — that the load-bearing variable is the *human learning curve*, not the model release notes, and that our introspection about our own productivity is close to worthless. Set it beside the broader trouble with AI benchmarks — contaminated test sets, models that can reproduce answer keys, evaluations the labs themselves have grown wary of citing — and a genuinely unsettling picture forms: we are scaling a general-purpose technology *faster than our ability to measure whether it is working*. That is not an argument for panic or for hype. It is an argument for epistemic humility as an operating discipline — for treating every confident number about AI's impact, including the ones you like, as provisional until someone can tell you how it was measured and how the measurement could fail.

*Source: METR blog (updated Feb 24, 2026). ~10 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: The hiding hand** — and here is the counterweight the whole issue needs, so it doesn't curdle into "trust nothing." In a 2018 Aeon essay, ["What Silicon Valley won't admit about technology and progress,"](https://aeon.co/essays/what-silicon-valley-wont-admit-about-technology-and-progress) the historian Edward Tenner revived Albert Hirschman's marvelous concept of the *hiding hand*: the paradox that if innovators could accurately foresee the difficulties ahead, they would never begin — but *because* they underestimate them, they start, and once committed they improvise their way through. Our systematic miscalculation of difficulty, in other words, is not just a bug in human judgment; it is sometimes the very thing that lets hard and worthwhile things get done. This is the deepest reply to an issue about bad evidence. Yes, audit your claims, demand logic and data, distrust the seductive correlation — *and* recognize that a life lived strictly inside what the evidence can prove would attempt almost nothing. The mature posture is not to eliminate the miscalculation but to know which kind you're making: the productive underestimate that gets you moving, or the motivated one you're using to avoid the audit. ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/what-silicon-valley-wont-admit-about-technology-and-progress), 2018)

**🃏 WILDCARD: the tax that never shows up on the bill** — the largest costs are often the ones no one measures. The journalist Annie Lowrey's idea of the ["time tax"](https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/archive/2021/07/how-government-learned-waste-your-time-tax/619568/) — first named in a 2021 Atlantic essay, now expanded into a book (*The Time Tax*, August 2026) — is the mountain of hold music, re-submitted forms, eligibility mazes and waiting rooms that citizens must climb to claim benefits they're already owed. It never appears on a budget line, so it is nearly invisible to the accounting, yet it is one of the most *regressive* levies in modern life: it falls hardest on the poor, the sick, and the exhausted, the people with the least time to spare, and it quietly erodes trust in the institutions that impose it. Keep it as a permanent lens. Whenever someone shows you a system that looks efficient, ask where it has hidden its costs — usually in someone else's unmeasured hours. (The Atlantic, 2021; book Aug 2026)

**The apprenticeship is the first casualty** — the reassuring macro headline about AI is "no widespread, economy-wide job displacement," and it's true. It is also hiding a distributional story. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, using ADP payroll data through mid-2026, finds employment of workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations now running [about 19% below](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) where it would be had it tracked their less-exposed peers — with *no* comparable gap for experienced workers, and the divergence widening steadily since August 2025. It works through a hiring freeze at the bottom, not layoffs. The sharp, non-obvious frame: AI's first casualty isn't the worker, it's the *apprenticeship* — the entry-level rung on which experienced workers were once made. A ladder with no bottom step is a problem you feel only years later. (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026)

**The bubble with no villain** — the most useful thing said about the AI-capex question this year came from Benedict Evans, whose annual [*AI Eats the World*](https://www.ben-evans.com/) argues that each major spender — the hyperscalers pouring hundreds of billions into infrastructure — is behaving *rationally given its own position*, and yet the aggregate has the structure of a bubble: no single villain, a systemic dynamic. Set his frame against the live dispute over whether AI's headline revenue is durable ARR or merely "experimental run-rate" that evaporates once customers finish their pilots, and against Ben Thompson's contrary case that agentic demand genuinely justifies the build-out, and you have the issue's theme in miniature: *the bull and the bear are both reasoning from evidence that cannot yet be audited.* When the data to settle a trillion-dollar question doesn't exist yet, notice that everyone claiming certainty is, necessarily, guessing. (ben-evans.com, 2026)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

**EconTalk — Daisy Christodoulou, "Meritocracy, Exams, and the Knowledge That Matters"** (~Sep 8, 2026)
The single best register fit of the week. Russ Roberts and the education researcher Christodoulou trace a wild thread — a failed Chinese civil-service exam that helped ignite the Taiping Rebellion, Britain's Northcote–Trevelyan reforms, Samuel Pepys's naval exams, Macaulay's conviction that a hard test on *anything* (alchemy, even Cherokee) would reveal the best minds — and then Christodoulou dismantles the punchline with evidence that skills are stubbornly *domain-specific*: chess masters, surgeons, even a Spielberg can't simply transfer their brilliance sideways. **Queue it** — the "surprising thread through history" is exactly this newsletter's voice, and the core finding *is* today's theme: the belief that an exam reveals general genius is itself a measurement error. ([Econlib](https://www.econtalk.org/meritocracy-exams-and-the-knowledge-that-matters-with-daisy-christodoulou))

The human counterweight. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Four glimpses of the same discipline. Martin audits the strategy memo for logic and data; Irwin audits the history textbook for the counterfactual it skipped; METR, most honestly of all, audits its *own* instrument and reports that it broke. What unites them is not doubt — doubt is cheap and can be struck as a pose — but *calibration*: the willingness to hold a claim, including a flattering one, only as tightly as the evidence for it will bear, and to say plainly how you'd know if you were wrong. That is harder than it sounds, because the whole ecology around us rewards the opposite: fluency over logic, the confident number over the honest interval, the story that credits our success to the thing we're proud of rather than the thing that happened to be true at the same time. And yet Hirschman's hiding hand keeps the discipline from collapsing into paralysis, which is its real failure mode. If you audited every claim to certainty before acting, you would never start the hard, worthwhile, underestimated thing — and starting it, misjudged and all, is how most of what matters gets built. So the posture that survives the issue is a double one, and it is not a contradiction. *Audit the claim as if everything depends on the evidence. Then, knowing the evidence is never complete, decide when to leap anyway — and be honest about which of the two you're doing.* The opposite of hype was never cynicism. It was calibration: knowing exactly how much you know, and acting with your eyes open on the rest.
