# The Daily Compass #099 — Only connect

*Sunday, November 8, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In 1969 a Harvard graduate student named Mark Granovetter sent a paper to the *American Sociological Review*. He called it "Alienation Reconsidered: The Strength of Weak Ties", and he framed it as a reply to the old idea that modern cities leave people isolated and adrift. The editor, reasonably enough, sent it to experts on alienation. They were unimpressed. One referee concluded that the author did not understand the concept, and the paper was turned down. Granovetter later admitted the reviewers had a point: he was not really writing about alienation at all. So he stripped every reference to it, kept the part about weak ties, and in 1972 sent the paper to the *American Journal of Sociology*. It was published in May 1973 as ["The Strength of Weak Ties"](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/07/strength-weak-ties), and became one of the most cited papers in the history of social science. Its argument is simple and still surprising: the people who change our lives are often not our close friends but our acquaintances, because they know things and people that we and our friends do not. The paper's own history is a small proof of its point. The idea was sound; it needed a different audience. This issue is about weak ties: why they matter, why we neglect them, and what happens to them when work goes remote and conversation goes to machines.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The strength of weak ties, tested

**What happened.** Granovetter's evidence came from a study of professional, technical and managerial workers in Newton, Massachusetts. Among those who had found their job through a personal contact, most had heard about it from someone they saw only occasionally or rarely, not from close friends. Close friends move in the same circles as we do, so they mostly know what we already know. Acquaintances act as bridges to other circles, and new information travels across bridges. For half a century the idea was mostly supported by observational studies. Then, in ["A causal test of the strength of weak ties"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abl4476) (*Science*, September 2022), Karthik Rajkumar, Guillaume Saint-Jacques, Iavor Bojinov, Erik Brynjolfsson and Sinan Aral analysed experiments in which LinkedIn had randomly varied its "People You May Know" recommendations for more than 20 million users over five years. During that time, users formed 2 billion new connections and recorded 600,000 job moves. The result both confirmed and refined Granovetter. Weaker ties did increase job mobility, but only up to a point: moderately weak ties, with some mutual connections, helped most, while the very weakest helped less. Weak ties mattered more in digital industries than in traditional ones. (The study also drew criticism because users were not told they were part of experiments.) Other research shows what bridging ties do inside companies. In ["Structural Holes and Good Ideas"](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/421787) (*American Journal of Sociology*, 2004), Ronald Burt asked managers at the defence company Raytheon for ideas to improve its supply chain. Those whose networks bridged gaps between otherwise separate groups produced ideas that senior executives rated more highly, and they were better paid and more often promoted.

**Why it matters.** Weak ties are how new information enters a person, a team or a company. Close teams are excellent at execution and trust, but they tend to recycle what they already know. The bridges matter most when the environment is changing, which is exactly when insiders are least likely to see it. There is an important limit, though. In "The Search-Transfer Problem" (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1999), Morten Hansen studied 120 new-product projects in a large electronics company. Weak ties between business units helped teams find useful knowledge quickly, but slowed the transfer of complex knowledge, which needed strong ties. The practical lesson for leaders is to manage both. Use loose, wide networks to search for ideas, people and early signals. Then build closer relationships around the few that matter, because weak ties can tell you something exists, but it usually takes strong ties to make it work.

*Source: Granovetter, AJS 78(6) (1973); Rajkumar et al., Science 377 (2022); Burt, AJS 110(2) (2004); Hansen, ASQ 44(1) (1999). ~35 min.*

### ◆ 2. Weak ties do not maintain themselves

**What happened.** If weak ties are so valuable, why do people use them so little? One reason is that we think we see our acquaintances more often than we do. In ["The small-world illusion"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v186y2025ics0749597824000797.html) (*Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 2025), Nadav Klein of INSEAD found across a series of experiments that people overestimate how often they bump into acquaintances. Chance encounters stick in memory; the many days when nothing happens do not. As a result, people overestimate how much help they will be able to get from acquaintances, and miss chances to ask. Another reason is that people underrate old connections. In ["Dormant Ties: The Value of Reconnecting"](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/dormant-ties-the-value-of-reconnecting/) (*Organization Science*, 2011), Daniel Levin, Jorge Walter and Keith Murnighan asked more than 200 executives to get advice on a current work project from people they had lost touch with for years. The advice from these reconnected contacts compared well with advice from current contacts, offering fresh perspectives along with the trust of an old relationship. And contact only becomes a tie if someone follows up. In ["Networking Frictions in Venture Capital, and the Gender Gap in Entrepreneurship"](https://www.gap.hks.harvard.edu/networking-frictions-venture-capital-and-gender-gap-entrepreneurship) (*Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*), Sabrina Howell and Ramana Nanda studied a Harvard Business School startup competition in which the number of venture-capital judges on each panel was effectively random. Men who happened to meet more investors were more likely to go on to found a venture-backed company; women were not. The difference was largely explained by follow-up: men were more likely to reach out to the judges afterwards.

**Why it matters.** Weak ties are valuable precisely because they are not part of everyday life, which means they fade unless someone makes the effort to maintain them. Leaders can act on this in a few ways. Reconnect on purpose: Levin and colleagues found that even the dormant contacts people expected to be least useful often turned out to be as helpful as their first choices. Ask the right people: in a field experiment with Singapore founders seeking investor introductions ([*Strategic Management Journal*, 2022](https://knowledge.insead.edu/entrepreneurship/foot-door-strategies-connect-investors)), Jared Nai and colleagues found that requests were about six times more likely to succeed when founders asked contacts close to the investor rather than contacts close to themselves. And do not leave follow-up to individual confidence. Howell and Nanda suggest that programmes should build follow-up meetings into their design, rather than relying on people to reach out on their own, because the people least likely to follow up are often those with the most to gain.

*Source: Klein, OBHDP 186 (2025); Levin, Walter & Murnighan, Organization Science 22(4) (2011); Howell & Nanda, JFQA (2023); Nai, Lin, Kotha & Vissa, SMJ 43(2) (2022). ~30 min.*

### ◆ 3. Fewer bridges, more chatbots

**What happened.** Two shifts are putting pressure on weak ties at work. The first is remote work. In ["The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01196-4) (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022), researchers at Microsoft and elsewhere analysed the emails, calendars and messages of 61,182 Microsoft employees in the United States before and after the company went fully remote in 2020. Collaboration networks became more static and more siloed: people spent about a quarter less time collaborating across groups and added fewer new contacts. The second shift is AI. A wave of new research asks what happens when people talk to chatbots instead of to other people. In a [study published in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*](https://news.ubc.ca/2026/04/texting-with-a-stranger-beats-a-chatbot-at-easing-loneliness/) this year, researchers at the University of British Columbia randomly assigned 296 first-year students to spend two weeks texting a randomly assigned fellow student, chatting with a supportive AI, or writing a short daily journal. The chatbot improved mood compared with journaling, but only the students texting a stranger became less lonely. In a much larger [pre-registered experiment](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_12882.html) with more than 12,000 French adults, the economists Louis Fréget, Ariell Reshef and Claudia Senik asked half the participants to have personal conversations with a generative AI every day for 28 days. Participants found the conversations pleasant, but [reported coverage](https://fortune.com/2026/08/16/ai-chatbot-research-loneliness/) suggests they ended the month lonelier, not less (the paper is a working paper and not yet peer-reviewed). And at work, the research firm Gartner reports from its surveys of employees that people increasingly take questions to AI tools rather than to colleagues, as satisfaction with collaboration falls.

**Why it matters.** AI and remote work both remove friction, and friction is where many weak ties are made: the question asked of someone from another team, the chat in the corridor, the conversation with someone you barely know. Each one feels inefficient; together they are how information and opportunities move around an organisation. The UBC researchers suggest one reason the stranger helped and the chatbot did not: people benefit from giving support as well as receiving it, and they gave more to a human. Leaders do not need to ban AI or force everyone back to the office to respond. They need to notice what the old friction was doing and rebuild some of it deliberately: cross-team projects, introductions, rotations, and an expectation that some questions go to people rather than to machines, especially questions that would connect someone to a part of the organisation they do not know.

*Source: Yang et al., Nature Human Behaviour 6 (2022); UBC/JESP (Apr 2026); Fréget, Reshef & Senik, CESifo WP 12882 (2026); Fortune (Aug 16, 2026); Gartner via TechTarget. ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: six degrees, revisited** — in 1967 the psychologist Stanley Milgram asked people, mostly in Omaha, Nebraska, to pass a letter towards a stranger in Boston through people they knew personally. The completed chains averaged about six steps, and "six degrees of separation" entered popular culture. Decades later, the psychologist Judith Kleinfeld went through Milgram's papers and [found](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/18417083) that the large majority of letters never arrived, so the famous number was less settled than it seemed. A much larger email replication, by Peter Dodds, Roby Muhamad and Duncan Watts ([*Science*, 2003](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/dodds2003b)), asked more than 24,000 participants to reach 18 targets in 13 countries. Only a few hundred chains were completed, and the researchers estimated the true typical chain length at five to seven steps. Their most interesting finding was about how successful chains worked: they ran mainly through intermediate and weak ties, often professional ones, and did not depend on a few highly connected "hubs". The world is small not because of a few super-connectors, but because of many ordinary acquaintances. (Dodds, Muhamad & Watts, Science, 2003)

**🃏 WILDCARD: how vampire bats make friends** — vampire bats famously share blood meals with roost-mates who failed to feed, but how do they start with strangers? In a study in *Current Biology* (2020), Gerald Carter and colleagues housed female vampire bats from two distant roosts together and watched new relationships form. Strangers began by [grooming each other](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/2020/03/bats-vampire-friendships-altruism/), a low-cost investment, and rising grooming predicted the first blood donation, a strategy the researchers called "raising the stakes". Only a minority of stranger pairs ever became food-sharing partners, and it took months. Earlier work by Carter and Gerald Wilkinson found that bats which had fed more non-relatives received more help when their usual donors were removed. Weak ties, it seems, are an insurance policy even for bats. (Current Biology, 2020)

**Less time together** — Americans are spending less time with other people. According to the 2025 American Time Use Survey, released in June, people aged 15 and over spent an average of [about 35 minutes a day socialising and communicating](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/05/americans-socializing-decline) in person, down from about 41 minutes a decade earlier, and only around three in ten socialised at all on an average day. The decline is steepest among the young. In that context, every one of the casual interactions discussed in this issue becomes scarcer, and more valuable. (Bureau of Labor Statistics via Axios, Jul 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

E. M. Forster's novel *Howards End* (1910) is about two families who live in different worlds: the cultured, idealistic Schlegels and the practical, business-minded Wilcoxes. Margaret Schlegel comes to believe that each world needs the other, and that most people's lives are divided against themselves. Forster gave the book a two-word epigraph, "Only connect...", and in the twenty-second chapter he lets Margaret explain it: "Only connect! That was the whole of her sermon. Only connect the prose and the passion, and both will be exalted, and human love will be seen at its height. Live in fragments no longer." Forster was writing about class, love and the divided self, not networks. But his instinct matches what Granovetter found six decades later. The most valuable connections are often the ones that cross a divide: between the familiar and the unfamiliar, between one world and another. Close ties keep us steady. Weak ties let the rest of the world in.
