# The Daily Compass #078 — The art of forgetting

*Sunday, October 18, 2026 · ~14 minute read*

In 1942 Jorge Luis Borges published a short story about a young man in Uruguay named Ireneo Funes who, after a fall from a horse, finds that he can no longer forget anything. Every leaf on every tree, every shape of every cloud, every moment of every day stays with him in complete detail. It sounds like a gift, and Borges shows it is a curse. Funes is troubled that a dog seen from the side at one moment should have the same name as the same dog seen from the front a minute later; each is a different memory to him. He can recall everything and understand almost nothing, because understanding requires leaving things out. This Sunday issue is about forgetting, which turns out to be less a failure of memory than one of its most important functions: in the brain, in the way we learn, and now in the machines we are building to remember for us.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. Memory is for deciding, not recording

**What happened.** For most of the history of neuroscience, forgetting was treated as a defect: memories fade because the brain cannot hold on to them. In a 2017 review in the journal *Neuron*, "The Persistence and Transience of Memory", the neuroscientists Blake Richards and Paul Frankland of the University of Toronto argued for a different view. The purpose of memory, they wrote, is not to keep a faithful record of the past but to help us make good decisions in the future. On that view, forgetting is an essential part of the system. It clears out outdated information that might mislead us, and it helps the brain extract general patterns rather than clinging to specific details, which makes it easier to deal with new situations. They pointed to active mechanisms in the brain that weaken or overwrite memories, including the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus, and to animal experiments in which forgetting an old route made it [easier to learn a new one](https://neurosciencenews.com/forgetting-smarter-6947/).

**Why it matters.** The idea has a practical edge for anyone who works with information, which is to say everyone. A person, team or company that remembers everything in equal detail is in Funes's position: overwhelmed by particulars and slow to see what has changed. The organisations that adapt best are often those that know what to let go of, the old assumptions about customers, the strategies that worked in a different market, the reasons a past project failed that no longer apply. Richards and Frankland's argument is a useful corrective to the instinct to archive everything and treat every lesson as permanent. The question to ask is not "what do we know?" but "what do we know that is no longer true?"

*Source: Richards & Frankland, Neuron (2017). ~25 min (paper).*

### ◆ 2. The curve that governs learning

**What happened.** The scientific study of forgetting began with one man and a list of nonsense. In the early 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus taught himself thousands of meaningless syllables, combinations like "DAX" or "BOK", and then measured how long it took him to relearn them after various delays. The results, published in 1885 as *Über das Gedächtnis* ("On Memory"), produced the famous forgetting curve: we lose most of what we learn quickly, within hours or days, and then forget more slowly after that. For more than a century the curve rested largely on one person's data. In 2015 Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros of the University of Amsterdam [repeated the experiment](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644) in *PLOS ONE*, with one subject spending some 70 hours learning lists and relearning them after delays from 20 minutes to a month. The results closely matched Ebbinghaus's, with one twist: a small upward jump in retention at the one-day mark, which the authors suspect reflects the effect of sleep.

**Why it matters.** The forgetting curve is the foundation of one of the most reliable findings in the science of learning: spacing. Because memories decay quickly at first, reviewing material at increasing intervals, after a day, then a few days, then a week, keeps it far more effectively than cramming it all at once. The same logic applies beyond the classroom. A training programme delivered in a single intensive week will be mostly forgotten within a month unless it is reinforced. A strategy announced once at an all-hands meeting will fade unless it is repeated. Leaders often assume that something said clearly has been learned. Ebbinghaus suggests that it has merely been heard, and that the real work of making it stick lies in the repetitions that follow.

*Source: Ebbinghaus, Über das Gedächtnis (1885); Murre & Dros, PLOS ONE (2015). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 3. Can a machine really forget?

**What happened.** As AI models absorb vast amounts of data, a new question has become pressing: how do you make one forget? Privacy laws such as Europe's "right to be forgotten" give people the right to have their data deleted, and companies may need to remove copyrighted, harmful or simply wrong information from models already trained. The field that tries to do this is called machine unlearning, and a new preprint suggests it may be measuring the wrong thing. In ["K-Bench: A Benchmark for LLM Unlearning in Agentic Deployments"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12808) (arXiv, September 2026), the authors note that standard tests count a model as having forgotten something if its final answer no longer reveals it. But AI systems are increasingly deployed as agents that reason step by step, call tools and read documents. K-Bench checks every channel such an agent exposes, including its reasoning, its tool calls and the results those tools return. The authors find that a method can appear to erase a secret from the model's answers while the same information still surfaces elsewhere, for instance through a tool that looks it up again.

**Why it matters.** The finding is a reminder that forgetting is a property of a system, not just of a single part. Deleting a fact from a model's weights means little if the agent can retrieve it from a search engine, a database or its own stored memory. That has implications for privacy regulation, for companies promising to delete customer data and for anyone building AI agents with long-term memory. It also echoes the first two briefs. In people, too, forgetting is not simply erasure; old knowledge can be suppressed but still return when the right cue appears. Designing machines that forget well, keeping what is useful and genuinely letting go of what should be gone, may turn out to be as hard, and as important, as designing machines that remember.

*Source: K-Bench, arXiv 2609.12808 (Sep 2026). ~30 min (paper).*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the man who could not forget** — Borges invented Funes, but the Soviet neuropsychologist Alexander Luria met a real one. For nearly thirty years he studied a Moscow journalist he called "S.", Solomon Shereshevsky, whose memory seemed to have no limit; he could recall long tables of numbers or passages in languages he did not speak years after seeing them once. Luria's 1968 book, [*The Mind of a Mnemonist*](https://www.librarything.com/work/25289), describes how this gift became a burden. Every word S. heard produced vivid images that crowded his mind, making it hard to follow a conversation or grasp the meaning of a story he could repeat word for word. He is said to have tried to rid himself of unwanted memories by imagining them written down and burned. (Luria, 1968)

**🃏 WILDCARD: a wound from 75,000 years ago** — in Shanidar Cave in Iraqi Kurdistan, famous for its Neanderthal burials, researchers have described the remains of a Neanderthal known as Shanidar A, found under about seven and a half metres of rock and sediment. A study [published on October 15](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/mena/2026/10/15/earliest-evidence-of-human-ancestor-killed-with-wooden-spear-found-in-iraqi-cave/) in *Scientific Reports* reports a round hole about 15 millimetres wide in the left shoulder blade, probably made by a sharpened wooden spear, which may be the earliest known example of lethal injury from such a weapon. Whether it was a hunting accident or an attack cannot be known. The same individual had survived an earlier blow to the face long enough for plaque to build up on a split tooth, which the lead author, Emma Pomeroy, suggests would have required care from others. (Scientific Reports / The National, Oct 2026)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Near the end of Borges's story, the narrator reflects on what Funes's perfect memory has cost him. He suspects that Funes was not very capable of thought, because, in Andrew Hurley's translation, "to think is to ignore (or forget) differences, to generalize, to abstract." In Funes's overcrowded world, the narrator says, there was nothing but particulars. It is a strangely consoling passage for anyone who has ever felt guilty about forgetting a name, a book or a lesson. The details fall away so that the shape can appear. A good memory, in a person, a company or a culture, is not the one that keeps everything. It is the one that keeps the right things, lets the rest go without regret, and still has room, on a quiet Sunday, for something new.
