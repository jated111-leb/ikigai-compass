# The Daily Compass #095 — The Delayer

*Wednesday, November 4, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

In June 217 BC, Hannibal ambushed and destroyed a Roman army at Lake Trasimene in central Italy. In the emergency that followed, Rome appointed Quintus Fabius Maximus dictator, and Fabius did something that infuriated his countrymen: he refused to fight. He shadowed Hannibal's army through the hill country, where the Carthaginian cavalry was of little use, harassed its foraging parties and cut off its supplies, but declined every invitation to a pitched battle. Romans mocked him as *Cunctator*, "the Delayer". The next year Rome tried the opposite. In 216 BC the consuls Varro and Paullus led an army of unprecedented size against Hannibal at Cannae, in southern Italy, and it was annihilated; ancient writers put the Roman dead at between about 48,000 and 70,000. Rome returned to Fabius's strategy of attrition, and over the following years Hannibal, far from home and unable to win the decisive battle he needed, was gradually worn down. The insult became an honorific. The Roman poet Ennius is said to have written that one man, by delaying, restored the state. Patience is hard to defend in the moment, because its benefits arrive late and its costs are visible every day. This issue is about the long game: the pressure on companies to deliver results now, what the famous marshmallow test really measured, and why the AI boom is testing investors' patience.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The tyranny of the quarter

**What happened.** In a famous survey published in 2005, John Graham, Campbell Harvey and Shiva Rajgopal asked 401 financial executives how they managed reported earnings. In ["The Economic Implications of Corporate Financial Reporting"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v40y2005i1-3p3-73.html) (*Journal of Accounting and Economics*), 78% said they would give up economic value in exchange for smoother earnings, and 55% said they would avoid starting a project with a clearly positive net present value if it meant falling short of the current quarter's consensus forecast. The executives were describing what they believed, not what they had necessarily done, but the admission was striking. A 2017 study by the McKinsey Global Institute, ["Measuring the economic impact of short-termism"](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/overview/in-the-news/the-case-against-corporate-short-termism), built an index of how long-term companies' behaviour was, measured by investment, earnings quality and other factors, and applied it to 615 large and mid-sized American companies from 2001 to 2014. The long-term companies grew revenue about 47% faster cumulatively and earnings about 36% faster than the rest, though the study shows correlation, not cause. The debate has now reached regulators. In May the US Securities and Exchange Commission [proposed](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/09/insights-september-2026/sec-semiannual-reporting) letting public companies report every six months instead of every quarter. The proposal drew a record number of comment letters, more than 200,000 and around 241,000 by mid-September according to Thomson Reuters, most of them opposed, and a final rule is not expected this year.

**Why it matters.** The research on reporting frequency itself is mixed. A study of American companies that moved to more frequent reporting in the mid-20th century found that their investment fell afterwards, consistent with managerial short-termism. But when Britain dropped mandatory quarterly reports in 2014, research by Robert Pozen and colleagues found no material change in companies' investment. The cadence of reporting may matter less than the culture of managing to it. Jeff Bezos made this point in Amazon's first letter to shareholders, for 1997, titled "It's All About the Long Term", in which he promised to make decisions in light of long-term market leadership rather than "short-term Wall Street reactions". For leaders, the practical lesson is to decide in advance which investments will be protected from quarterly pressure, explain them to investors and boards before the pressure arrives, and resist the temptation, admitted by so many executives in the Graham survey, to sacrifice tomorrow's value for today's number.

*Source: Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal, JAE 40 (2005); McKinsey Global Institute (Feb 2017); SEC proposal (May 2026), via Skadden and Thomson Reuters (Sep 2026); Kraft, Vashishtha & Venkatachalam, The Accounting Review (2018); Pozen et al., Harvard Kennedy School. ~30 min.*

### ◆ 2. What the marshmallow test really measured

**What happened.** In experiments at Stanford's Bing Nursery School between the late 1960s and early 1970s, Walter Mischel offered young children a choice: one treat now, or two if they could wait while the experimenter left the room. Follow-up studies of small groups of those children found that the ones who had waited longer went on to better outcomes, including higher SAT scores, and the "marshmallow test" became the most famous experiment on self-control. Later research has complicated the story. In ["Revisiting the Marshmallow Test"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/a-new-approach-to-the-marshmallow-test-yields-complex-findings.html) (*Psychological Science*, 2018), Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan studied more than 900 children from a much larger and more diverse sample. The link between waiting at age four and achievement at 15 was about half the size of the original estimates, and it shrank by about two-thirds again once the children's family background and early cognitive ability were taken into account. An earlier experiment had suggested why. In ["Rational snacking"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3730121/) (*Cognition*, 2013), Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri and Richard Aslin first showed 28 children an adult who either kept or broke promises during an art activity. In the marshmallow task that followed, children who had seen a reliable adult waited about four times as long as those who had seen an unreliable one. Waiting, the authors argued, is partly a rational bet on whether the promised reward will actually arrive.

**Why it matters.** The revised picture has a direct lesson for leaders. Patience in an organisation is not only a trait people bring with them. It depends heavily on whether the environment rewards it, and above all on whether promises have been kept before. Teams that have seen long-term projects cancelled, promised promotions withheld or bonuses quietly changed will rationally discount any future reward, and grab what they can now. Teams that have seen commitments honoured will wait. A leader who wants a patient organisation therefore has to do something less glamorous than preaching long-term thinking: make fewer promises about the future, keep every one of them, and let people see the delayed rewards actually arrive.

*Source: Watts, Duncan & Quan, Psychological Science 29 (2018); Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin, Cognition 126 (2013); Mischel, Shoda & Peake (1988). ~25 min.*

### ◆ 3. The payback that markets would not wait for

**What happened.** On October 28 Alphabet, Google's parent company, reported third-quarter results that showed the cost of the AI build-out. Capital spending reached about $51.4 billion in the quarter, roughly double a year earlier, and free cash flow was negative by about $9 billion, the second consecutive quarter of cash burn. The company kept its forecast of $195 billion to $205 billion in capital spending for 2026, and its chief financial officer said spending would increase significantly in 2027. Revenue from its cloud business more than doubled, but investors focused on the cash and on weak growth in search. The next day Alphabet's shares fell by [roughly a tenth or more](https://www.heygotrade.com/en/news/alphabet-q3-earnings-miss-sinks-stock-reignites-ai-capex-fears-googl-nasdaq-futures/), with reported figures ranging from about 9% to 12%, wiping out several hundred billion dollars of market value; by some measures it was the company's worst day on record. A survey last year by the advisory firm Teneo had already shown the gap in expectations: most chief executives of large companies said returns from AI would take more than six months, while a majority of institutional investors expected them within six months. A study in the *Journal of Consumer Psychology* suggests AI may be making customers less patient too. In ["Time is shrinking in the eye of AI"](https://www.psypost.org/artificial-intelligence-makes-consumers-more-impatient/) (January 2026), Yuanyuan Li and colleagues found that people advised by an AI agent, rather than a human, were more likely to choose a smaller reward now over a larger one later, apparently because AI's association with speed made delays feel longer.

**Why it matters.** The AI boom is one of the largest long-term bets in corporate history, made by companies answerable to investors who are paid quarterly. Alphabet's experience shows how quickly patience can run out when the payback is uncertain and the costs are visible every three months. It does not settle whether the spending will pay off; that will take years to judge. But it carries two lessons for any leader making long-horizon bets, with or without AI. First, the case for patience has to be made in advance and in specifics: what milestones will show progress before profits arrive, and what would cause the bet to be scaled back. Second, the audience's patience is itself a resource that can be depleted. Customers, employees and investors who are used to instant answers will judge delays more harshly. Framing the wait concretely, with dates and milestones rather than open-ended intervals, may help.

*Source: Alphabet Q3 2026 results, via HeyGoTrade, CNBC and others (Oct 28–29, 2026); Teneo Vision 2026 survey (Dec 2025); Li, Lin, Gong, Wang & Janiszewski, Journal of Consumer Psychology 36(1) (2026), via PsyPost. ~25 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the oaks that were never planted** — one of the most beloved stories about long-term thinking concerns New College, Oxford. In the 19th century, the story goes, the great oak beams of the college hall were found to be riddled with beetles, and the college forester revealed that the founders had planted a grove of oaks centuries earlier for exactly this moment. Stewart Brand popularised the tale in *How Buildings Learn*, but he also noted that the college's archivist had no knowledge of such a grove. [On closer inspection](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/oak-beams-new-college-oxford), the woodland that supplied the replacement timber was acquired only in the 1440s, after the hall was built, and the roof had already been rebuilt more than once. The story survives because we want it to be true: it flatters the idea that someone planned for us. Real long-term thinking is less romantic. It usually looks like Fabius, making unpopular choices one season at a time. (Brand, How Buildings Learn; Atlas Obscura)

**🃏 WILDCARD: what a waiting dog's brain looks like** — researchers at Eötvös Loránd University in Budapest recorded the brain activity of 14 trained family dogs, using non-invasive EEG, while the dogs obeyed a command not to take a treat or toy in front of them. In a study published in August in *Animal Cognition* and widely reported in September, they found a rise in frontal theta waves while the dogs held back that [resembles the signal](https://www.popsci.com/environment/dog-brain-self-control-no/) linked to self-control in humans, in both frequency and location on the head. The authors caution that the signal cannot distinguish between restraint, attention and anticipation. Still, a dog sitting quietly beside a treat may be doing something recognisably like what a child does with a marshmallow. (Animal Cognition, Aug 2026)

**Venture is slower than it looks** — the venture industry runs on ten-year funds, but the cash takes longer. According to Carta's [first-quarter report](https://carta.com/data/vc-fund-performance-q1-2026/) on more than 2,700 American venture funds, fewer than 20% of funds from the 2017 and 2018 vintages had yet returned their investors' money in cash, and even a top-decile 2017 fund had paid back only about 1.18 times what was invested. Founders, too, tend to be older than the myth suggests: a study of 2.7 million American founders by Pierre Azoulay, Benjamin Jones, Daniel Kim and Javier Miranda (*American Economic Review: Insights*, 2020) found that the average founder of the fastest-growing start-ups was 45 when they started. Overnight success usually takes a decade or two. (Carta, Q1 2026; AER: Insights, 2020)

## ── THE QUEUE ─────────────────────────────────

**Afford Anything — "Acorns CEO: The Scariest Part Of Investing Is the First Step, with Noah Kerner"** (new; October 30, 2026)
Paula Pant talks with Noah Kerner, chief executive of the investing app Acorns, who admits that fear kept him from investing through most of his twenties and cost him a decade of compounding. The most useful section, "Patience vs. prediction", contrasts trying to guess one's way to quick wealth with the dull discipline of a diversified portfolio held for decades, and asks why prediction markets, sports betting and buy-now-pay-later products pull so many people towards the quick hit. It is framed around personal finance, but the argument applies to any long-term bet. **Queue it**. ([Afford Anything](https://affordanything.com/fear-of-investing-noah-kerner/))

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Two thousand years after Fabius, another general faced an invader he could not defeat in open battle. In Tolstoy's *War and Peace*, Field Marshal Kutuzov, commanding the Russian army against Napoleon in 1812, resists the young officers and courtiers who demand a decisive engagement. "It is not difficult to capture a fortress but it is difficult to win a campaign," he tells Prince Andrew in Louise and Aylmer Maude's translation. "For that, not storming and attacking but patience and time are wanted." Later, alone with his thoughts as the French army begins to weaken, he reflects: "Patience and time are my warriors, my champions!" Tolstoy's Kutuzov is not passive. He knows exactly what he is waiting for, and he understands that time is working on his side, wearing down an enemy far from home. That is the difference between patience and mere delay. Patience is a strategy with a reason: a belief, grounded in evidence, that the passage of time will improve one's position. For leaders, the hard part is not waiting. It is knowing what one is waiting for, explaining it to those who must wait too, and keeping the promises that make their patience rational.
