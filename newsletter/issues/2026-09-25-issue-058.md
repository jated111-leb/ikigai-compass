# The Daily Compass #058 — Mostly plumbing

*Friday, September 25, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

The leadership industry sells charisma. Vision, presence, the decisive individual who bends the room — a whole economy of books, keynotes, and executive coaching rests on the premise that organizations rise and fall on the special qualities of the person at the top. This issue is a quiet argument against that premise. Not that leaders don't matter, but that when you look hard at what actually determines whether people can function inside an organization — whether they burn out or stay, check out or commit, feel like colleagues or like strangers sharing a building — the evidence keeps pointing away from the stage and toward the infrastructure. Toward the unglamorous, mostly invisible things: whether people know what their job is, whether the org is built so they can be known by others, what a manager privately assumes about human nature, and what power quietly does to the person who holds it. Today's three briefs relocate "leadership" from performance to plumbing, and the timeless capstone is a sixty-five-year-old idea that still explains most of the rest.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. The stressor that isn't workload

**What happened.** A meta-analysis just published in the *Journal of Vocational Behavior* did something the field rarely manages: it settled an argument with overwhelming evidence. Gargi Sawhney of Auburn and colleagues pooled [515 studies spanning six decades and roughly 800,000 workers](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S000187912600028X) to weigh the three classic "role stressors" against each other — role *overload* (too much to do), role *conflict* (competing demands), and role *ambiguity* (not knowing what your job actually is, how success will be judged, or which decisions are yours to make). The popular villain, the one every wellness program and workload survey targets, is overload. But it lost. Across nearly every outcome the researchers tracked — stress, burnout, job performance, and intention to quit — the single most damaging factor, explaining the largest share of the variance, was *role ambiguity*. Not having too much work. Not knowing what the work is.

**Why it matters.** This is a quietly radical finding, because ambiguity and overload call for opposite responses and flatter opposite kinds of leaders. Overload is a resourcing story — hire more people, add tools, protect people's calendars — and it lets a manager feel benevolent while changing nothing about their own behavior. Ambiguity is a *clarity* story, and clarity is unglamorous, effortful, and squarely the manager's job: to say, in plain words, what this role is for, what "good" looks like, and where the edges of someone's authority actually fall. It is the least charismatic act in management and, this evidence suggests, close to the most important. The reframe for anyone who leads: before you conclude your exhausted team has a workload problem, consider that they may have a *definition* problem — and that the fix is not more resources but more sentences, said clearly, about who owns what.

*Source: Sawhney et al., Journal of Vocational Behavior (2026). ~15 min for the writeups; the paper is dense.*

### ◆ 2. The leader you're crediting

**What happened.** If brief 1 says the manager's real job is unglamorous, this one asks a sharper question: how much of what we call "good leadership" is even the leader's doing? A conceptual critique in the Cambridge journal *Industrial and Organizational Psychology* takes aim at the reigning orthodoxy in management — the idea that good leadership is best understood through the *psychological safety* and *trust* it produces. The critique, ["But what is good leadership?",](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/industrial-and-organizational-psychology/article/but-what-is-good-leadership-a-conceptual-critique-of-defining-leadership-through-psychological-safety-and-trust/D4286495DDC35D7B4166FB8B55F94583) makes a deceptively simple logical objection: if you *define* a good leader as one whose team feels safe and trusting, you have argued in a circle. The leader is judged effective because the team feels safe; the team's safety is attributed to the leader's effectiveness. Worse, it invites textbook omitted-variable bias, because a team can be psychologically safe for reasons that have little to do with the person nominally in charge — peer norms, the wider climate, the composition of the group itself — and crediting all of it to leadership systematically overstates how much the leader is doing.

**Why it matters.** This lands on one of the most fashionable ideas in modern management and refuses to let it off the hook, and the discipline it demands generalizes far past academia. "Psychological safety" has become the thing every organization says it wants and every good manager is supposed to manufacture — but if we can't cleanly separate the leader's contribution from the team's own chemistry, then a great deal of leadership credit (and blame) is being assigned on faith. The useful move is not to dismiss safety or trust, which plainly matter, but to hold the attribution loosely: to ask, of any thriving team, *what here is actually the leader, and what would be true regardless?* It is the same humility brief 1 requires, pointed at the top of the org chart instead of the middle. Much of what we celebrate as leadership may be the weather being credited to the weatherman.

*Source: Industrial and Organizational Psychology, Cambridge (2026), a commentary on the toxic-vs-incompetent-leadership debate. ~12 min.*

### ◆ 3. The office is a loneliness machine

**What happened.** The third piece of invisible architecture is the one nobody designs on purpose. A major integrated review in the *Journal of Management*, ["All the Lonely People,"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01492063241313320) synthesizes 233 empirical studies to make a claim that cuts against how we usually talk about loneliness. Work loneliness, the authors (Julie McCarthy, Berrin Erdogan, Talya Bauer, and colleagues) argue, is not primarily a personal failing or a private mood people bring to the office — it is substantially *produced by the design of work itself*. Remote and hybrid arrangements, competitive incentive structures, high-churn teams, roles built for output rather than contact: these are features of organizational design, and they generate loneliness as reliably as a badly designed building generates drafts. The review also finds that empathic, empowering leadership measurably reduces it — meaning connection, too, is something the structure can be built to create or destroy.

**Why it matters.** This reframes the "loneliness epidemic" from a wellness problem — fixed with meditation apps and the occasional pizza Friday — into a *design* problem, which is a far more demanding and honest place to put it. If loneliness at work is a structural output, then the humane response is not to exhort individuals to "connect more" but to look at the architecture: how teams are composed, whether roles have any built-in reason for people to depend on and know one another, whether the incentives quietly reward isolation. It completes the issue's triad. Clarity (brief 1), honest attribution (brief 2), and belonging (here) are all things that live in the *design* of an organization, not the personality of its leader — and all three are routinely mistaken for soft extras when they are, in fact, the load-bearing walls.

*Source: McCarthy et al., Journal of Management, vol. 52 (2026). ~20 min; a proper review.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: Theory X and Theory Y** — the idea under all three briefs was named in 1960. In [*The Human Side of Enterprise*,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Human-Side-of-Enterprise) the MIT management scholar Douglas McGregor argued that every manager operates on an unstated assumption about human nature. *Theory X* assumes people inherently dislike work and must be coerced, monitored, and directed. *Theory Y* assumes people will exercise self-direction and seek responsibility when the conditions allow it. McGregor's enduring, unsettling insight was that these assumptions are *self-fulfilling*: manage people as if they're lazy and need control, and you build the surveillance and the rigidity that make them behave exactly that way; assume competence and give autonomy, and you tend to get it back. Sixty-five years on, it is the deepest frame for this whole issue — role ambiguity, misattributed trust, engineered loneliness are all, at bottom, symptoms of an organization run on Theory X assumptions it never examined. The question McGregor leaves every leader is quiet and hard: *not what your people are like, but what you assume they're like — and what that assumption is quietly building.* ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Human-Side-of-Enterprise), 1960)

**🃏 WILDCARD: power may cause brain damage (with an asterisk)** — a genuinely strange and slightly dangerous idea, offered with its own health warning. In a much-read 2017 Atlantic essay, ["Power Causes Brain Damage,"](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2017/07/power-causes-brain-damage/528711/) Jerry Useem gathered research — much of it from the psychologist Dacher Keltner — suggesting that holding power measurably degrades the neural machinery of empathy, specifically a process called "mirroring" by which we simulate other people's experience. The powerful, in these studies, become more impulsive and less able to see through others' eyes — as if the very thing that gets you promoted quietly erodes the perspective-taking that made you good in the first place. The asterisk matters, and it's the honest part: much of this rests on "power priming" experiments, and priming research has suffered serious replication failures; skeptics argue the brain-damage framing far outruns the evidence. Keep it, then, as a *hypothesis with teeth* rather than a settled fact — a reason for anyone gaining power to treat their own shrinking empathy as a live risk to manage, whether or not the neuroscience fully holds. (The Atlantic, 2017)

**The motivation we keep mispricing** — a clean companion to the timeless pick. In MIT Sloan Management Review, ["What Leaders Get Wrong About Employee Motivation,"](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/what-leaders-get-wrong-about-employee-motivation/) Marylène Gagné and Rebecca Hewett note that management still largely runs on *agency theory* — the assumption that people are self-interested and won't work hard unless incentivized and monitored — despite decades of evidence from *self-determination theory* that top performance flows from intrinsic motivation, which needs autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Their crisp prescription: give people the *why* (clear goals) and the *what* (direction), then hand them the *how* (autonomy). It rhymes exactly with brief 1 — clarity about ends, freedom about means — and with McGregor: control-based management doesn't just underperform, it actively corrodes the motivation it's trying to buy. (MIT Sloan, 2025)

**AI's real bottleneck is organizational, not technical** — the year's quiet enterprise story is not a capability ceiling but a management one. Across converging industry surveys, roughly 80% of enterprise apps now embed some AI agent, yet only about 31% of organizations run one in production, and a striking [48% now call their AI adoption a "massive disappointment,"](https://writer.com/blog/enterprise-ai-adoption-2026/) up from 34% a year ago. The blockers cited are not "the model isn't smart enough" — they're evaluation, governance, reliability, and data trustworthiness. In other words, the same unglamorous organizational plumbing this whole issue is about: clarity of purpose, trustworthy structure, the boring work of making something dependable. The constraint on AI value has moved from the model's IQ to the organization's ability to hold it. (Treat the exact percentages as directional — the sources are vendor and analyst surveys — but the direction is consistent everywhere.)

The perfect companion to brief 2, one register up. Diamond's new book — *Profits, Prophets, Coaches, and Kings: (When) Do Leaders Matter?* — presses the case that individual leaders matter far less than the histories suggest. Asked to name the single most irreplaceable leader in human history, he declines: conditions ripen, he argues, and somebody turns up — even Genghis Khan happened to be born at an auspicious moment for a conqueror of the steppes. **Queue it** as the deep-history version of this issue's skepticism about the heroic individual, with Cowen pushing back hard enough to keep it honest. ## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Put the pieces side by side and a single, deflationary picture forms. The thing most likely to burn your team out is not their workload but their not knowing what their job is. The psychological safety you credit to a great leader may be the team's own chemistry wearing the leader's name. The loneliness in your building is not a personal weakness of the people in it but a property of how the building was designed. And the assumptions you hold about whether people can be trusted with autonomy are quietly manufacturing the very behavior you use to justify them. None of these is a story about charisma, vision, or the decisive individual. They are stories about *architecture* — about clarity, attribution, belonging, and the assumptions baked into a structure — and architecture is invisible precisely when it's working, which is why it's so easy to credit the visible person on the stage instead. That is the quiet radicalism in this week's evidence, and it is oddly hopeful: if what determines whether people thrive is mostly structural, then it's mostly *buildable*, by ordinary managers doing unglamorous things, rather than reserved for the rare heroic leader. McGregor saw it in 1960 and it still holds. Most of leadership is plumbing — the humble, load-bearing, invisible work of making a place where people can actually function. The temptation is always to reach for the vision statement. The work is almost always to fix the pipes.
