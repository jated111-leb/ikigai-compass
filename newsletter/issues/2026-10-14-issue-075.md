# The Daily Compass #075 — Time to develop disagreement

*Wednesday, October 14, 2026 · ~15 minute read*

There is a story that Peter Drucker liked to tell about Alfred Sloan, the man who built General Motors into the largest company in the world. At a meeting of his top committee, Sloan is said to have asked whether everyone was in complete agreement on the decision in front of them. Everyone nodded. In that case, Sloan replied, he proposed postponing the matter until the next meeting, to give the group time to develop disagreement and perhaps understand what the decision was really about. The wording varies from telling to telling, but the point does not. Unanimity can be a warning sign, because it often means no one has looked hard at the problem. This issue is about dissent as an input to good decisions: why staged disagreement rarely works as well as the real thing, how seldom boards actually say no, and what a new study of AI teams reveals about the price of staying quiet.

### ◆ 1. Why the devil's advocate rarely works

**What happened.** If disagreement is valuable, the obvious move is to manufacture it: appoint someone to argue the other side. The psychologist Charlan Nemeth of the University of California, Berkeley, has spent much of her career testing that idea, and her results are a warning to anyone who runs meetings. In an earlier experiment, she found that a designated devil's advocate mostly led groups to bolster their original view: members spent their energy defending the position they already held rather than thinking about alternatives. In a follow-up study with Keith Brown and John Rogers, ["Devil's advocate versus authentic dissent"](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.58) (*European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2001), the team compared genuine minority dissent with several versions of role-played dissent, varying whether the advocate's true opinion was known and whether it matched the side they were arguing. Authentic dissent beat every version of the role-play, producing more solutions and better ones. As Nemeth's [Berkeley profile](https://psychology.berkeley.edu/user/111) sums up her findings, no role-playing technique stimulates divergent thinking as well as dissent that people really believe.

**Why it matters.** The likely explanation is that people can tell when an objection is staged. A colleague who is merely playing a part can be heard politely and then ignored; a colleague who actually believes the group is wrong forces everyone to wonder why. Nemeth's work does not say that structured techniques are useless. It says that they are a poor substitute for a culture in which real disagreement is safe to express. The practical lessons for leaders are to seek out people who genuinely see the problem differently, to ask for objections before stating your own view, and to treat a lone dissenter as a source of information rather than a problem to be managed. Nemeth's book on the subject, [*In Defense of Troublemakers*](https://behavioralscientist.org/stop-playing-devils-advocate-and-other-advice-for-better-decision-making/), makes the case at greater length.

*Source: Nemeth, Brown & Rogers, European Journal of Social Psychology 31 (2001); Nemeth, In Defense of Troublemakers (2018). ~20 min.*

### ◆ 2. What boards actually do in the room

**What happened.** Boards of directors exist, in theory, to challenge management. But what happens in the boardroom is usually hidden, so most research has had to infer board behaviour from outcomes. Miriam Schwartz-Ziv and Michael Weisbach found a rare way in. In ["What Do Boards Really Do? Evidence from Minutes of Board Meetings"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17509) (*Journal of Financial Economics*, 2013), they analysed the detailed minutes of board and committee meetings at eleven Israeli companies in which the government held a large stake, which recorded the discussions in unusual detail. The headline finding is stark. In the published version, boards disagreed with the chief executive on only about 2.5% of the issues they discussed, and in about 99% of cases they were presented with a single option rather than a choice. Boards were not idle: in about 63% of meetings they took at least one active step, such as asking for more information or taking an initiative of their own, or voting against the CEO. But outright disagreement was rare.

**Why it matters.** The study suggests that the most important decision in a boardroom is often made before the meeting, by whoever frames the agenda. When directors are shown one proposal, the choice becomes "approve or reject", and rejection carries a high social cost. When they are shown several options, disagreement becomes a normal part of choosing. The sample is small and specific, government-linked firms in one country, so the exact numbers should not be generalised too far. But the lesson travels well to any committee, investment panel or management team: if you want a group to think, bring it alternatives, not a single recommendation dressed up as a choice. Sloan's postponement was a way of doing the same thing, giving disagreement time to appear before the vote.

*Source: Schwartz-Ziv & Weisbach, Journal of Financial Economics 108(2) (2013); NBER Working Paper 17509. ~40 min (paper).*

### ◆ 3. The price of staying quiet, measured in machines

**What happened.** In 1985 the psychologists Garold Stasser and William Titus showed that groups have a blind spot. Give each member a different piece of information, so that the right answer only emerges if everyone shares what they know, and groups tend to discuss what they all already know and neglect the unique pieces, often choosing the wrong option. The setup became known as the "hidden profile" problem. A new preprint applies it to teams of AI agents. In ["Multi-agent discussion gains less when dissent is withheld"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38324) (arXiv, Sep 29, 2026), Chand Sahil Mansuri, Sadamori Kojaku of Binghamton University and colleagues build a simple model of team discussion around a few behaviours: withholding dissent, sticking to one's stated answer, and correcting toward the truth. The model predicts that discussion can overturn an initially wrong majority only when the rate of withheld dissent stays below a critical threshold. Experiments with teams of language-model agents, including on a hidden-profile benchmark, fit the prediction: the benefit of discussion shrinks as agents keep their disagreements to themselves. Instructing the agents not to withhold dissent increased the benefit.

**Why it matters.** The paper's subjects are machines, not people, and it is an early preprint, so the findings should be read as an analogy rather than proof about human teams. But the analogy is useful. It turns a familiar management idea, that people need to feel safe to speak up, into something closer to a measurable tipping point: below a certain level of self-censorship, discussion corrects errors; above it, discussion mostly confirms them. It also matters directly as companies start to build teams of AI agents to review code, check contracts or plan projects. If those agents are trained to be agreeable, they may reproduce one of the oldest failures of human committees, agreeing politely on the wrong answer. The design lesson is the same for people and for software: reward the voice that says "I see it differently."

*Source: Mansuri, Kojaku et al., arXiv 2609.38324 (Sep 29, 2026); Stasser & Titus, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (1985). ~30 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**⏳ TIMELESS: the original devil's advocate** — the phrase began as a job title. In 1587 Pope Sixtus V formally established the office of the [Promoter of the Faith](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devils-advocate) (*promotor fidei*), popularly called the devil's advocate, whose task was to argue against the canonisation of candidates for sainthood, scrutinising their lives and the miracles attributed to them. For four centuries the Church built organised scepticism into one of its most important decisions. In 1983 John Paul II reformed the canonisation process and the office's role was sharply reduced; the number of people declared saints rose markedly in the years that followed. Whatever one makes of the cause and effect, it is a neat illustration of what happens to the pace of approval when the formal voice of objection is turned down. (Britannica)

**🃏 WILDCARD: orangutan mothers arrange playdates** — orangutans are the most solitary of the great apes, but a new study in *PNAS* (Oct 6) suggests mothers go out of their way to give their young a social life. Drawing on 15 years and more than 32,000 hours of observation of wild orangutans at the Tuanan research station in Borneo, covering 31 young born to 14 mothers, researchers led by the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior found that mothers with infants of similar age shared more of the forest, and their young played together more often. In the days before a [playdate](https://phys.org/visualstories/2026-10-orangutan-mothers-playmates-young.amp), mothers steered their travel toward a neighbour's home range, then headed back afterwards, travelling farther and feeding less in the process. Socialising, even for an ape that mostly lives alone, is worth paying for. (PNAS / Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior, Oct 2026)

**Security makes dissent better, not quieter** — a study in *Scientific Reports* by Sujin Lee and Seungwon Jeong of KAIST and Zoe Kinias of Ivey Business School [reports](https://arynews.tv/employees-hide-crucial-ideas-when-they-lack-workplace-security-kaist-study-finds) that people who feel securely attached to their group make stronger dissenting arguments: more focused on the task, more detailed and more persuasive. In a second experiment, majority members rated arguments from secure dissenters as more convincing and were more willing to adopt them. The experiments used hypothetical decision scenarios, so this is lab evidence, but it cuts against the fear that a warm team culture breeds conformity. Done right, belonging is what makes disagreement possible. (KAIST, late Sep 2026)

**Fail first, on paper** — the most practical tool in this issue costs nothing. In a short 2007 *Harvard Business Review* piece, ["Performing a Project Premortem"](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem), the psychologist Gary Klein proposed that before committing to a plan, a team should imagine that it is a year later and the project has failed spectacularly, then spend a few minutes writing down why. The exercise works because it changes the social question. Instead of asking people to criticise a plan their boss supports, it asks them to explain a failure that has, in the story, already happened, which makes doubts easier to voice. (HBR, Sep 2007)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

"He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that." John Stuart Mill wrote the line in *On Liberty* (1859), in a chapter arguing that even true opinions decay into dead dogma unless they are constantly tested against opposition. Mill admired the Roman orator Cicero, who studied his opponents' arguments as carefully as his own, and he thought that if real critics were missing, we would have to imagine the strongest ones ourselves. An older tradition reached a similar conclusion by a different route. The Mishnah, the great code of Jewish law compiled around the year 200, records not only the rulings of the majority but the opinions of those who lost the argument, and in one passage it stops to ask why. One answer it gives is that a later court may come to prefer the minority view and rely on it. Another sage offers a different reason, and the text keeps both, so that even its explanation of why dissent is preserved is itself a recorded disagreement. That may be the best model for any organisation: not a culture in which everyone agrees, nor one in which everyone argues endlessly, but one that decides, acts, and still keeps a careful record of who said otherwise and why, in case they turn out to be right.
