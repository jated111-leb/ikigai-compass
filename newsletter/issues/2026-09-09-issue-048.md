# The Daily Compass #048 — The limits of foresight

*Wednesday, September 9, 2026 · ~16 minute read*

We have never had more machinery for knowing the future and deciding well. Prediction markets price the odds of everything from elections to earnings; alignment workshops promise to get a leadership team truly of one mind; strategy frameworks turn the terrifying open field of the future into a grid you can fill in. And yet the machinery keeps sliding off the actual decision. The forecast is excellent and no one at the top uses it. The team leaves the offsite convinced it agrees, and it doesn't. The strategy deck is immaculate and the company does the same thing it was always going to do. This issue is about two walls that all this apparatus keeps hitting — the ceiling of what can be known at all, and the stubborn human refusal to act on what we do know — and about the uncomfortable possibility that good judgment is the one thing we have never figured out how to manufacture, systematize, or delegate. It still comes down, every time, to a person willing to see clearly and choose.

## ── THE BRIEFS ────────────────────────────────

### ◆ 1. How much of the future is even knowable?

**What happened.** Superforecasting is no longer a curiosity; it's an industry, with prediction markets and calibrated forecasters wringing genuine, money-good signal out of a chaotic world. So Scott Alexander asks the deeper question in ["Does Forecasting Have Room At The Top?"](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/does-forecasting-have-room-at-the) — not *can we forecast* but *how much better can forecasting possibly get.* Is there vast headroom above the best human predictors — the kind an AI might one day seize — or are we already scraping against a hard ceiling set by the world's irreducible randomness? His answer is bracingly practical: even if we're near the ceiling, even a modest edge — four to twelve percentage points of accuracy over the best humans on the questions that matter — would be worth an extraordinary amount, because at the scale of markets and governments, small improvements in calibration compound into fortunes and spared catastrophes.

**Why it matters.** The framing quietly relocates the whole problem. The limit on foresight was never mainly our *skill* — it's the amount of genuine signal the future actually contains, which for many of the questions we care about most is brutally low. Some of tomorrow is knowable and we should fight hard for the last few points of accuracy on it; much of it is not knowable at all, and no amount of compute or cleverness will change that. The maturity the age lacks is the ability to tell the two apart — to pour resources into the predictable margin while refusing the fantasy that enough intelligence dissolves uncertainty itself. This is the first wall the machinery hits: not that our instruments are bad, but that there is a horizon past which there is simply nothing to see, and wisdom begins with knowing roughly where it sits. Everything after depends on being honest about how thin the knowable really is.

*Source: Alexander, Astral Codex Ten (Aug 4, 2026). ~14 min.*

### ◆ 2. The agreement that isn't there

**What happened.** Suppose the foresight exists. The second wall is that organizations are strangely good at not using it — starting by fooling themselves that they already agree. In the *Harvard Business Review* cover story ["The False Alignment Trap,"](https://hbr.org/2026/07/the-false-alignment-trap) BCG's Julia Dhar, Kristy Ellmer, and Philip Jameson argue that most change efforts fail not from bad execution but because senior leaders *believe* they share a view of the why, the what, and the how when they simply don't — a false consensus manufactured by vague language, conflict-avoidance, and the pressure to move fast. Their fix is engineered dissent: force specificity, make disagreement safe and early, assign real decision rights, extract explicit commitment before moving. It drew a sharp rebuttal from Steve Denning in [*Forbes*](https://www.forbes.com/sites/stevedenning/2026/07/09/why-the-management-thinking-in-the-false-alignment-trap-is-flawed/), who noted the article's own showcase — the jeweler Pandora — posted a decade of dismal shareholder returns *despite* all the alignment rituals, and warned that internal harmony can become theater that substitutes for the harder work of actually being right about the market.

**Why it matters.** Put the two together and you have the real trap, which is deeper than either author quite says. It's the same failure Chris Argyris diagnosed in his 1991 classic on why the smartest, most successful professionals are often the *worst* learners: having rarely failed, they've never built the muscle of examining their own reasoning, so when reality pushes back they get defensive and reason around it rather than through it. False alignment is that pathology gone collective — a room full of accomplished people, each too invested in being right to notice they're not even talking about the same thing. The cure and the counter-cure both matter: you need engineered dissent so the disagreement surfaces, *and* Denning's cold shower so the dissent stays aimed at the world and not just at the vibe in the room. The apparatus of alignment can produce the warm feeling of a decision without the substance of one — and the warm feeling is more dangerous than open conflict, because it doesn't know it's wrong.

*Source: Dhar et al., HBR (Jul/Aug 2026); Denning, Forbes (Jul 9). ~15 min.*

### ◆ 3. Strategy is broken, says the man who built it

**What happened.** Here is the constructive turn. Roger Martin — as close to a dean of business strategy as the field has — has spent the last stretch of his career arguing that the whole discipline is ["profoundly broken,"](https://rogermartin.medium.com/fixing-strategy-560e4c09ee0d) a "relic of 1960s technocracy" born when brilliant people believed they could analyze their way to universal truths. His indictment cuts to the method: you *cannot* analyze your way to a great strategy, because analysis, by its nature, only affirms the past — it works on the data you already have, which is a record of what already happened. Real strategy is an act of *imagination*: a bet on a "what might be" that you cannot prove in advance, made by the general managers who will actually have to live it out, guided by principles rather than spreadsheets, iterative and distributed rather than handed down by a planning staff. "Somebody who thinks of strategy as involving a lot of imagination," he writes, "will beat you."

**Why it matters.** Martin names the disease running under both prior briefs: our deep, technocratic wish to convert judgment into procedure — to make the risky, personal, imaginative act of deciding into something safe, analytic, and transferable. Forecasting apparatus, alignment frameworks, strategy decks: each is, at bottom, an attempt to get the fallible human out of the loop, to make the future yield to method. And each fails at exactly the same seam, because the part that matters — the leap past what the data can show, the willingness to commit to a "might be" — is precisely the part no method can perform for you. This is why the age's instinct to answer every hard decision with more analysis (and now more AI) is a category error dressed as rigor. The tools are real and worth having. But they clear the ground; they do not take the step. Somebody still has to imagine what isn't there yet and stake something on it — and that person cannot be replaced by a better model, only supported or gotten out of the way.

*Source: Martin, "Fixing Strategy" (Feb 2026). ~12 min.*

## ── RAPID FIRE ────────────────────────────────

**The buildout that can't see its own bill** — a sharp, unsettling read on how the AI capex boom is being financed so as to be, almost by design, hard to measure. [Naked Capitalism](https://www.nakedcapitalism.com/2026/09/ai-off-balance-sheet-obligations-hit-a-subprime-like-reset-wall-in-2027-and-2028.html) walks through the machinery — off-balance-sheet special-purpose vehicles that keep the debt off the headline books, circular vendor-financing deals that let the same dollars count as demand on more than one ledger, and depreciation schedules stretched well past the real economic life of the chips. The point isn't a bubble call; it's epistemic. When an industry's accounting is engineered not to reveal its own cost, the people deciding whether to keep building are flying, deliberately, on instruments that have been fogged. Brief 1's ceiling on the knowable is one thing; this is unknowability manufactured on purpose. (Sep 2026)

**Science without the second thoughts** — a cluster of new papers pours cold water on the "autonomous AI scientist." Across tens of thousands of agent runs, researchers found the systems reliably produce the *outward form* of discovery — a hypothesis, a result, a finished-looking manuscript — while ignoring disconfirming evidence in the large majority of their reasoning traces and almost never revising a belief when the data push back (arXiv 2604.18805 and companions, spring 2026). It is the exact theme of this issue in miniature: the ritual of inquiry performed without the one thing that makes it inquiry — vulnerability to being wrong. Closure in a workflow is not closure in a mind. (2026)

**The radical politics of the handmade** — a lovely [Aeon essay](https://aeon.co/essays/the-radical-reasons-why-you-dream-of-making-things-by-hand) reads the contemporary hunger for handmade, slow, artisanal things not as nostalgia but as a stance — heir to Ruskin and Morris, who invoked medieval craft to indict industrial society for severing people from the meaning of their own work. Why it belongs here: in an economy racing to automate the last manual and cognitive tasks alike, the wish to make something with your hands is a quiet argument about where meaning lives — not in the output, but in the doing. (Jul 6)

**Who actually gets to decide** — a practical companion to brief 2, from [*HBR*](https://hbr.org/2026/07/what-companies-get-wrong-about-decision-rights): companies botch "decision rights" by fixing roles before they've clarified the goal, treating the rights as a static list dictated from the top, and letting hierarchy quietly override whatever was formally assigned. The fix is to treat *who decides* as something co-created and periodically renegotiated — a live agreement, not an org-chart fossil. Dull-sounding, quietly foundational: most bad decisions are really unowned ones. (Jul/Aug)

**⏳ TIMELESS: The Hedgehog and the Fox** — Isaiah Berlin, 1953. Berlin built an entire theory of mind on a fragment from the Greek poet Archilochus — "the fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing" — dividing thinkers into hedgehogs, who force all of reality through a single grand system, and foxes, who hold many partial, contradictory truths at once without needing to resolve them. It was an essay about Tolstoy; it became something larger. Why now: forty years later Philip Tetlock ran the experiment for real, tracking expert predictions for two decades, and found the foxes reliably, measurably *out-forecast* the hedgehogs — the temperament that tolerates contradiction and distrusts the one big idea is the temperament that sees the future more clearly. It is the human original of brief 1's whole question, and a warning label for every confident, single-system account of where the world is going. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hedgehog_and_the_Fox), 1953)

**🃏 WILDCARD: life where nothing should live** — a wonder-forward dispatch from the edges of the possible: [Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-organisms-that-make-earths-harshest-places-home-20260720/) surveys the extremophiles — organisms thriving in boiling acid, deep ice, the salt-and-sulfur hell of Ethiopia's Danakil Depression, places that read like a list of ways to die. They quietly expand our sense of where the line of the survivable actually falls, which is much further out than intuition allows — a useful humility for a species forever sure it knows the limits of things, and a reminder that the range of the real routinely embarrasses the range of the expected. (Jul 20)

## ── CODA ──────────────────────────────────────

Notice what all our machinery is *for*. The prediction market, the alignment framework, the strategy process — each is, underneath, an attempt to take judgment out of the hands of a fallible, unscalable, unrepeatable human being and lodge it somewhere safer: in a method, a model, a procedure that anyone could run and everyone could trust. It is a deeply understandable wish. People are biased, tired, defensive, mortal; methods don't sleep. But every thread in this issue converges on the same stubborn finding: the part of good judgment that actually matters is exactly the part that won't transfer. It's the fox's tolerance for contradiction, which no checklist can install. It's the willingness to examine your own reasoning when the world pushes back — the thing Argyris found the most successful people least able to do. It's Roger Martin's imaginative leap to a "might be" that the data, by definition, cannot yet support. It's the courage to act on the thin, hard-won margin of the knowable while staring honestly at the vast dark beyond it. Lewis Thomas, the great physician-essayist, found it oddly consoling that we control so much less than we imagine — that our own cells run on domesticated bacteria we neither command nor understand, that the body is less a fortress than a negotiated truce. The dream of frictionless foresight and manufactured agreement isn't just difficult; it was never even our condition. Which leaves the unfashionable conclusion. The goal is not to engineer the person out of the decision. It is to find people brave enough to make the call and honest enough to own it when it's wrong — and then to give them the best instruments we can build, and get out of their way. The machine can clear the ground. Someone still has to take the step.
